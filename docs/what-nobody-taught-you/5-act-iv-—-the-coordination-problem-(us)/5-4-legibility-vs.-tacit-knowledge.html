5-4 Legibility vs. tacit knowledge

Part of 5 Act IV — The Coordination Problem (us)

Coordination at scale requires seeing at scale, and seeing at scale requires simplifying. That is the uncomfortable starting point. A tax authority cannot levy a tax on a village it cannot count; a health ministry cannot run a vaccination campaign without registers; a manager cannot allocate resources across forty sites without comparable numbers. So every large organisation — states above all — builds a simplified picture of the thing it wants to govern: maps, categories, units, identifiers, forms, standards. James C. Scott called this legibility, and his book Seeing Like a State (1998) is the standard treatment. Its central claim is not that legibility is evil but that it is lossy in a patterned way, and that the loss is invisible from the point of view of whoever built the grid.

The simplification machine

Legibility is a compression operation, and like every compression it discards by design. What a state needs is not a picture of society but a picture of the parts of society that can be counted, compared, and acted upon from the centre. So it standardises: permanent surnames, cadastral surveys, uniform weights and measures, registered addresses, standardised school curricula, coded medical diagnoses, licence categories.

Two features of this diagram matter. First, the grid is not neutral: choosing what to count is choosing what can be governed, and therefore what gets attention, budget, and power. Second, the excluded remainder does not vanish. It keeps operating, unmeasured, and eventually announces itself as an anomaly, a crisis, or a "culture problem" — which is the map–territory error arriving with an invoice.

The historian's tell for legibility is easy to spot: look for the moment a practice was turned into a form. Surnames were imposed for taxation and conscription, not because people lacked names. Forests were surveyed into uniform parcels so they could yield a predictable annual revenue. Patients became diagnosis codes so hospitals could be billed and compared.

Why legibility is not the villain

It is tempting to read this material as a defence of folk wisdom against bureaucrats. Resist it, because the argument collapses and the political implications are bad.

Legibility delivers things that simply cannot be delivered any other way. Epidemiology requires comparable case definitions; without them, a pandemic is a rumour. Anti-discrimination law is unenforceable without counts — you cannot show a pattern of exclusion with anecdotes. Land reform and inheritance rights run on cadastres and titles. Public health, food safety, and consumer protection are all downstream of standards. Hugo's Les Misérables aside, being unregistered is not freedom; it is usually invisibility in the distribution of public goods.

There is also the mirror-image failure. Local customary orders are frequently oppressive precisely in the ways that resist central scrutiny: caste exclusions, gender-based land rules, informal debt bondage, quiet understandings about who may speak at meetings. A state that can see individuals directly is more capable of protecting them from local power than one that must negotiate through whatever structure happens to be there. Scott himself is careful about this, and anyone using his argument to defend "traditional arrangements" against "bureaucrats" should be asked whose interests those arrangements serve.

So the honest framing is not legibility versus freedom. It is this: legibility is necessary, and its characteristic failure is not that it simplifies but that it stops noticing that it has.

Tacit knowledge: knowing more than we can tell

The reason simplification loses things is that a great deal of what makes a system work is not written down anywhere, because it cannot be. Michael Polanyi's formulation, from The Tacit Dimension (1966), is the one to remember: we can know more than we can tell. You can recognise a thousand faces and describe none of them adequately. You can ride a bicycle, read a room, spot a crack in a beam a millimetre wide, judge when dough is ready, and none of that knowledge exists in a transferable form.

Scott builds on a Greek distinction that maps onto this cleanly:

Technē Mētis
Form Universal, codified, teachable Local, practical, learned by doing
Carrier Manuals, curricula, formulas Practitioners, apprenticeships, community
Example Navigation by tables and geometry Reading the water, the sky, the birds
Transfers by Instruction Immersion and imitation
Scales by Copying Cannot be copied — must be regenerated

Mētis is not folk superstition. It is often more accurate than the codified version, because it is continuously tested against a real environment by people who bear the consequences. The farmer knows which corner of the field floods. The nurse knows which patient is deteriorating despite normal numbers. The operator knows the machine's sound before it fails. None of it is in the manual, and the manual is not thereby wrong — it is simply a projection, in exactly the sense the map chapter used.

Tacit knowledge is invisible to measurement and lossy under interruption. Because it is transmitted by contact and practice rather than by documents, it is destroyed easily and rebuilt slowly. Remove the practitioners and you do not merely lose staff; you lose the thing the org chart never recorded.

Four conditions for a high-modernist disaster

Scott's most useful contribution is that the catastrophes he studies are not the result of simplifying. Simplification is universal. They require a specific combination, and the absence of any one ingredient means the outcome is usually a costly mess rather than a disaster.

Ingredient What it supplies
Administrative ordering of nature and society The grid: cadastres, standard units, planned populations, uniform parcels
High modernism A confident ideology that scientific planning can redesign society from scratch — the future as a drawing
An authoritarian state willing to use coercion The power to enforce the grid against local resistance
A prostrate civil society The absence of the organised bodies that could contest, adapt, or reinterpret the plan

Note what is missing from the list: stupidity, cruelty, or bad intentions. High modernism is a faith in the model, sincerely held by people who wanted to improve lives. The danger is the conjunction, which is why the chapter on complexity keeps insisting that decomposable problems and interactive ones require opposite tools. The high modernist applies the jet-engine toolkit to the rainforest.

Worked example: the forest that was only a revenue stream

The paradigm case is scientific forestry, developed in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Germany. The state's need was clear: a predictable annual yield of timber for mines, ships, and revenue. To get it, foresters abstracted the forest into a normal forest — standardised stands, uniform age classes, an annual allowable cut, every tree counted by volume.

It worked, spectacularly, by its own metric. Wild, mixed, uneven woodland became a regular grid of single-species spruce and pine, growing at predictable rates, harvestable on schedule. Measured as a wood factory, it was a triumph.

The second rotation told a different story. The mixed undergrowth, the deciduous species, the soil fauna, the nitrogen-fixing plants, the fungi, the browsing animals, the fire and pest dynamics — none of which appear in a volume table — had been doing real work. Removed, the simplified forest lost soil fertility, became vulnerable to windthrow and insect outbreaks, and in some places failed to regenerate on the same ground. The foresters had optimised a metric and lost the system. The loop is familiar by now:

The mechanism is identical to the Goodhart, second-order-effect, and feedback material elsewhere in this book — this is the same structural failure in a different costume, and the costume is instructive: no measure was corrupted, no one cheated, and no incentive was perverse. The metric was faithfully optimised. The loss was everything the metric was never designed to see.

Worked example: the scripted support desk

A modern version, smaller in scale and closer to home. A support organisation standardises on a call script, a knowledge base, and an average-handling-time target, so that thousands of interactions become comparable and stafﬁng becomes predictable. The first quarter is excellent: variance collapses, throughput rises, the dashboard is unambiguous.

What disappears is the operators' mētis — knowing which customer needs the script abandoned, which problem resembles a known bug despite the wrong symptom, when to spend twelve minutes and prevent three future calls. Scripts crowd out that knowledge because the metric rewards adherence and speed, and because judgement that cannot be shown on a dashboard is invisible to performance review. Within a year, handle times are excellent and repeat contacts are rising; the repeat rate is a different team's metric, so no single owner sees the whole loop.

The lesson is not "don't write scripts". It is that the script replaced a system it did not fully understand, and the parts it removed were the parts that had been silently holding quality together.

Design: thin interfaces, thick interiors

The practical synthesis is a division of labour rather than a choice of side.

Standardise what must interoperate — that is where the gains are real and comparative, and where a common unit genuinely beats a hundred dialects. Leave the interior alone: let the people with local knowledge decide how the work is done, because that knowledge is not available to the centre at any price. Then measure coarsely and check the ground, because the moment a metric becomes a target, the chapter on Goodhart's Law is already running in the background.

Three specific habits fall out of this:

  • Do not standardise the part you cannot see. If your model of the work has no representation of judgement, the model will not protect it. Assume the gap exists and ask what fills it.
  • Keep an unreduced channel open. Site visits, verbatim complaints, ride-alongs, reading the raw tickets. These are expensive per unit and are the only thing that corrects a model rather than reshuffling it.
  • Protect the apprenticeship. Tacit knowledge is regenerated by contact, not by documentation. If experienced practitioners are too busy to work alongside newcomers — because that time doesn't show on the dashboard — you are consuming a stock you cannot restock. This is where lock-in logic and institutional memory meet.

Where it bites

  • Development and aid. Villagisation schemes, resettlement programmes, and "modernising" agriculture that replaced the farmer's judgment with the agronomist's plan, at enormous cost to yields and people.
  • Urban planning. Brasília, Chandigarh, and similar projects were built to a legible plan and quickly grew unplanned satellite settlements around themselves, because the plan could not encode how cities actually function — the informal street life and fine-grained mixing that Jane Jacobs argued were the city's substance.
  • Organisations. ERP rollouts, CRM mandates, reorganisation by org chart, and metric systems that homogenise work whose value lies in its variation.
  • Software and platforms. APIs, protocol standards, and data schemas are legibility machinery; so are moderation policies and ranking algorithms, which encode a simplified model of a community and are gamed the moment they become load-bearing.
  • Your own systems. Any dashboard you trust more than you trust a visit to the floor is the problem in miniature.

Practical rules

  1. Ask what the grid cannot see. Name two things your categories, units, or metrics systematically omit. If you can't, you haven't looked.
  2. Distinguish legibility from truth. A practice being unmeasurable does not make it irrational; a practice being measurable does not make it important.
  3. Standardise the boundary, not the middle. Interoperability is where uniformity pays.
  4. Treat resistance as information. When practitioners ignore the process, they are usually reporting a discrepancy your model cannot represent. The exception, as always, is when they are protecting their own position — which is a question about incentives, not knowledge.
  5. Watch for the high-modernist conjunction. Confidence plus coercion plus no organised opposition is the pattern, and it looks exactly like efficiency from the inside.
  6. Budget for regeneration. Apprenticeship, rotation, and slack are how tacit knowledge survives a generation. They will never appear on the business case as a benefit.

Terminology

Legibility — The condition of a population, practice, or landscape being rendered countable, comparable, and actionable from the centre through simplification into standard categories.

State simplification — Scott's term for the abstracting operations (maps, cadastres, censuses, standard units, codes) by which a state makes society visible to itself.

Grid — The set of categories, units, and identifiers through which an organisation perceives the world it governs; what is off the grid is effectively invisible to it.

Tacit knowledge — Knowing that is embodied in practice and cannot be fully articulated or transferred by documentation; Polanyi's "we can know more than we can tell".

Mētis — Practical, local, context-bound knowledge, learned by immersion and constantly tested against reality; contrasts with technē.

Technē — Universal, codifiable, teachable knowledge of the kind carried in manuals, curricula, and formulas.

High modernism — The confident belief that scientific planning can redesign society from first principles, treating the future as a drawing to be executed.

Authoritarian high modernism — Scott's term for the conjunction of high modernism with a state willing to use coercion and facing no organised opposition; the condition under which simplifications become catastrophes.

Normal forest — The idealised model of a forest as uniform age classes and predictable annual yield; the classic legibility grid for woodland.

Prostrate civil society — The absence of organised institutions capable of contesting, adapting, or reinterpreting a central plan; the fourth ingredient in Scott's catastrophe recipe.

Thin interface, thick interior — A design pattern that standardises what must interoperate while leaving local discretion intact where tacit knowledge lives.

Residue (the illegible remainder) — The parts of a system left out by a simplification, which continue to operate unmeasured and reappear as surprise.

Regeneration cost — The time and contact required to rebuild tacit knowledge lost when practitioners are removed or interrupted; a cost that appears on no budget line.
