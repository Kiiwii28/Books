5-6 Social construction

Part of 5 Act IV — The Coordination Problem (us)

Some things are real because they are made of matter, and some are real because enough people treat them as real. Money, borders, marriage, corporations, race, weekends, and the value of a signature all fall into the second group. This subsection is about that second group — what it means for something to be socially constructed, why the phrase is both indispensable and routinely misused, and how constructions acquire the power to shape a world full of physical things.

The practical stakes are high, because the answer to "is this constructed?" is usually assumed to settle "is this real?" or "should we keep it?" Neither follows. Getting the logic right is what lets you see, in the same glance, why a currency can collapse overnight and why no amount of disbelief will let you walk through a wall.

Three kinds of fact

John Searle's division is the cleanest place to start.

Kind of fact Depends on Example Stops existing if everyone stops believing?
Brute fact Physics, chemistry, biology Water boils at altitude; arsenic is toxic No
Social fact Widespread agreement, expectation, practice Language, queueing norms, a handshake Yes, gradually
Institutional fact Agreement plus an assigned status that carries powers Money, property, marriage, citizenship, a corporation Yes, immediately

Brute facts are indifferent to your opinion. Institutional facts are made of opinion — but not the opinion of any one person, and not the kind of opinion you can revoke privately.

Searle's formula compresses the mechanism into a sentence: X counts as Y in context C. A rectangle of polymerised cotton counts as twenty pounds in the United Kingdom. A person standing in a particular box on a particular day counts as a voter. A group of people filing a document counts as a legal person that can own assets and be sued. The physical substrate is doing something, but it is not doing the important thing.

The distinction that saves the concept from nonsense

Ian Hacking's The Social Construction of What? (1999) sorted the muddle by noticing that "constructed" answers at least two different questions, and people slide between them mid-sentence.

Causal construction. The thing was shaped by social forces — history, power, custom, economics. Almost everything human is causally constructed in this sense. Childbirth practices, maths curricula, and the layout of your city all qualify.

Constitutive construction. The thing exists only because of the agreement. Money is constitutively constructed; the fact that a banknote is worth something is not a fact about paper.

Both can be true of the same object, and neither implies that the object is unreal, arbitrary, or bad. The word "constructed" is a claim about origin, not about status. This is precisely the same mistake as reading "emergent" as "good" — an origin story doing work that only a value premise can do.

Three questions turn the vague phrase into an actual claim:

  1. What is the physical substrate? There is always one. There is no construction floating free of matter.
  2. What agreement does the thing require? Whose, how broad, how maintained?
  3. Is the construction constitutive or merely causal? What would remain if the agreement evaporated?

How constructions get made: coordination, not conspiracy

Nobody decided that money would be money, and nobody voted on which side of the road to drive on. Constructions are a coordination phenomenon, and the mechanism is the same one that produces focal points.

The essential ingredient is not agreement in the sense of approval. It is common knowledge — I expect it, I expect you to expect it, I expect you to expect me to expect it. That mutual expectation is what makes the behaviour self-sustaining without any enforcer.

This is why pointing out that something is constructed almost never changes it. The construction does not rest on your belief; it rests on everyone's expectation of everyone's behaviour. Explaining to a queue that the queue is a mere convention does not dissolve the queue.

Two corollaries follow. First, constructions can be designed — time zones, patents, the metre — but once adopted they behave like grown order, with all the knowledge-dispersal and adaptation properties that entails. Second, changing a construction requires changing what people expect others to do, which is a coordination problem, not an argument. That is where law, mandate, focal events, and shocks come in — the same toolbox that gets a locked-in standard unstuck.

Institutional facts and the powers they carry

What makes institutional facts special is that they do not merely describe; they assign. Searle calls these deontic powers: rights, duties, permissions, obligations, authorisations.

Notice the loop closing. The powers are real because people act on them; people act on them because the powers are real; and the loop is stabilised by a shared expectation rather than by force, though force is often available as a backstop. Declarations are the act that starts the loop — "I pronounce you…", "I resign", "the meeting is adjourned" — a speech act that changes the world rather than describing it.

This is the connection to legibility. States are in the business of manufacturing institutional facts at scale: surnames, cadastral maps, standard weights, identity documents, official categories. These are not neutral descriptions of what was already there; they are instruments that make a population administrable, and they reshape the people they classify.

Worked example: money

Start with the substrate. A banknote is a printed slip. Apply the formula: X (this slip) counts as Y (a claim on real goods) in C (the issuing jurisdiction and its trading partners).

Now ask the diagnostic question — what happens if everyone stops believing? Everything. Nothing about the paper changes; the purchasing power is gone. Hyperinflations are this happening in real time. The Weimar marks, the Zimbabwean dollars, and the Venezuelan bolívars were not destroyed; the mutual expectation collapsed, and the substrate was revealed as substrate.

But do not over-read the lesson. The construction is not arbitrary. It is backed by things that are far less constructed: the state's power to tax in that currency, productive capacity, the willingness of armed people to enforce contracts. Meanwhile some of the most consequential constructions in finance are ones nobody consciously agreed to. A credit rating is a status function assigned by a private agency, which then triggers regulatory rules that treat the rating as a fact about risk — powers, in Searle's sense, granted by an agreement nobody remembers making. In 2008 the loop turned violent in the ordinary direction: when enough participants stopped expecting the AAA designation to mean what it said, the designation stopped meaning it, and assets priced on the assumption had to be repriced.

Worked example: standard time

Before 1883, "noon" meant the moment the sun crossed the meridian where you stood. That was a brute fact about astronomy, and it worked fine until railways made it possible to travel far enough, fast enough, for the discrepancy to matter. Rail timetables in the United States listed dozens of local times, and near-simultaneous trains could collide because two stations disagreed about what time it was.

On 18 November 1883, American railroads imposed standard time by fiat — five zones, one day, announced rather than legislated. Congress ratified it in law in 1918. Within a generation the convention felt natural.

Look at what happened. A coordination problem (everyone needs to mean the same thing by "3 p.m.") was solved by a focal solution (the railroads), adopted because non-adoption was worse, and then hardened into a fact nobody experiences as a choice. Today, jet lag is a real physiological symptom of an administrative decision, and no individual can opt out. The construction is not less real for being constructed — it is in your body.

Worked example: race in medicine

This one shows why the crude version of the concept fails. Race is not a biological taxonomy: human genetic variation is clinal and does not partition into the folk categories. But race is not therefore nothing.

Three distinct claims are usually tangled together, and separating them is the whole exercise:

  • The classification is constructed. "Black" and "White" as mutually exclusive administrative boxes were assembled historically through slavery, colonialism, census categories, and law (the one-drop rule is a construction visible in the plumbing).
  • The consequences are brute. Exposure to discrimination, differential policing, environmental burden, wealth gaps, and stress physiology produce real, measurable differences in health. Racism is a physical exposure.
  • The clinical shortcuts built on it were bad medicine. Race-based algorithms — the eGFR kidney-function correction, spirometry reference values, some risk scores — treated a social category as a biological parameter. A 2021 task force convened by the National Kidney Foundation and the American Society of Nephrology recommended removing the race coefficient from eGFR estimation, and similar corrections have been dropped elsewhere.

The lesson is not "race is fake, so ignore it." It is that a constructed category can carry enormous causal weight, and can also be a bad proxy for the thing you actually want (ancestry, exposure, circumstance) — and that the only way to tell which is happening is to ask what work the category is doing.

This is Hacking's looping effect at work: classifications of people are not indifferent to the people classified. Apply a label, build institutions around it, and people respond to the label — forming identities, adjusting behaviour, generating new statistics, which then appear to confirm the label.

Self-fulfilling and self-undermining constructions

Because constructions run on belief and behaviour, they can be made true by being believed — or made false by the attempt to prevent them. The Thomas theorem states the general case: situations defined as real are real in their consequences.

A bank is solvent as long as depositors expect it to be; a rumour that it is not turns it insolvent, which then confirms the rumour. A rating of a neighbourhood as high-risk raises the cost of credit there, which suppresses investment, which produces the conditions the rating predicted. A school labelled failing loses the pupils whose parents can move, which makes it fail.

The mirror image matters too. A published forecast of a shortage can cause hoarding, or can cause conservation. A credible warning of a riot can cause police mobilisation that prevents it, so the prediction looks like a false alarm. Constructions do not just sit there being believed; they are inputs to the systems described elsewhere in this act.

The three ways the claim goes wrong

The concept is powerful and therefore attracts sloppy use. Four failure modes are worth naming explicitly, because catching them is most of the skill.

"Constructed, therefore arbitrary." Constructions are contingent — they could have been otherwise — which is not the same as random or unmotivated. Standard time solved a real problem. Money solves a real problem. Language solves a real problem. Contingency tells you the thing is a choice, not that it is a bad one.

"Constructed, therefore unjust." This is the is/ought gap wearing a lab coat. That something was assembled by historical forces is a descriptive claim; that it should be dismantled is a normative one, and it needs a bridge premise. Note that the bridge usually exists and is usually reasonable — but it must be stated, because otherwise the argument is doing an illegitimate slide, and it loses against anyone who shares your facts but not your values.

"Constructed, therefore changeable by explanation." Pointing out that a construction is a construction changes nothing, because it was never resting on individual belief. Constructions change when expectations change — via mandate, focal event, incentive redesign, or collapse — which is coordination work, not rhetoric.

"Everything is constructed." If the word applies to everything, it discriminates nothing. The test is the disbelief experiment: stop everyone believing, and see what survives. Gravity, arsenic, and the tide survive. Money, weekends, national borders, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average do not. And note that many of the most interesting cases are mixed — race, disease categories, "intelligence", wilderness — where a substrate is real but the boundaries are drawn by us.

Claim What it really asserts Common error
"Money is constructed" Its value exists only by shared expectation Reading it as "money is worthless"
"Race is constructed" The categorisation is social, not biological Reading it as "racism has no effects"
"Childhood is constructed" The age boundaries and expectations are historical Reading it as "children are imaginary"
"The economy is constructed" The aggregates and rules are conventions Reading it as "nothing constrains markets"

The last row points at a subtle and important asymmetry. Some constructions are ontologically shallow — a fad, a hashtag — and some are ontologically deep, having been layered into law, capital, bodies, and habit over centuries. Depth is why dismantling a shallow construction takes a fortnight and dismantling a deep one takes generations, even when everyone agrees it should go. That is path dependence applied to meanings.

Why the concept earns its place

Strip the misuse away and social construction does four jobs that nothing else in this act does.

It explains how order emerges without a designer. Constructions are the mechanism by which coordination happens among people who never met — a genuinely grown order with a deontic edge.

It explains why institutions have teeth. Rights, duties, and authority are not physical forces, yet they move armies. Searle's answer — status functions assigning deontic powers — is the bridge between coordination and the machinery of institutions.

It supplies the origin story for genuine constraints. Social constructions, once deep enough, behave like brute facts for everyone inside them. The price of bread, the value of a degree, the seriousness of a criminal record: none are physical, all are binding.

And it supplies the diagnostic vocabulary for asking what is contingent. "Which of the constraints on my options are physics, and which are agreements?" is one of the highest-yield questions available, and it is unanswerable without this distinction. Most things that feel like laws of nature are agreements that outlived the people who agreed.

A short diagnostic

When you meet the phrase in the wild — from a colleague or from yourself — run this:

  1. What is the substrate? Name the physical thing or act underneath the status.
  2. Who must agree, and how do they know the others agree? Find the common knowledge.
  3. Constitutive or merely causal? Run the disbelief test.
  4. What powers does it assign? Rights, duties, permissions, authorisations — the deontic layer.
  5. How deep is it? Weeks to change, or centuries? What has been built on top?
  6. What is it doing that something else could do better? A category can be constructed, consequential, and still a bad proxy for what you care about.
  7. If you want it changed, what is the coordination mechanism? Mandate, incentive, focal event, or collapse. Argument alone is not on the list.

Question 6 is the one that turns the concept from a debating weapon into an instrument. The useful version of "X is socially constructed" is never the bare observation. It is the follow-up: constructed out of what, held up by whom, doing which work, and replaceable with what?

Terminology

Social construction — A thing that exists or has its character because of widespread agreement, expectation, and practice, rather than because of its physical properties alone.

Brute fact — A fact that holds independently of anyone's beliefs; the class against which constructions are contrasted.

Institutional fact (Searle) — A fact created by assigning a status to something that carries powers; the formula is "X counts as Y in context C."

Status function — The assignment by which a physical thing or act takes on a social role — a slip of paper counting as money, a person counting as a judge.

Deontic powers — The rights, duties, permissions, and authorisations that a status function confers, and which thereby change behaviour.

Causal construction — The claim that a thing was shaped by social and historical forces; true of nearly everything human, and not by itself a claim about its reality.

Constitutive construction — The stronger claim that a thing exists only by agreement, so that dissolving the agreement dissolves the thing.

Common knowledge — The condition in which everyone knows, and knows that everyone knows, and knows that everyone knows that everyone knows; the ingredient that makes a convention self-sustaining without enforcement.

Looping effect (Hacking) — The process by which classifying people changes them, so that the classified respond to the label and generate evidence that appears to confirm it.

Self-fulfilling prophecy (Merton) — A belief that produces the behaviour which makes the belief true; the Thomas theorem ("situations defined as real are real in their consequences") is its general statement.

Ontological depth — How far a construction has been layered into law, capital, bodies, and habit; deep constructions behave like brute facts for those inside them and resist rapid change.

Disbelief test — The diagnostic of asking what would happen if everyone stopped believing in a thing: nothing survives for brute facts, everything for constitutive ones, and something for mixed cases.

Mixed case — A thing with a real substrate and socially drawn boundaries — race, disease categories, "intelligence" — where the substrate is not negotiable and the boundaries are.

Ontological gerrymandering — Declaring other people's beliefs to be constructed while presenting one's own analysis as neutral description; a rhetorical move that immunises the analyst's claims from the very treatment they apply to others.

Reification (of a construction) — Treating an agreement as a natural fact; the mirror error of treating a natural fact as an agreement, and the one that makes contingent arrangements look unchangeable.
