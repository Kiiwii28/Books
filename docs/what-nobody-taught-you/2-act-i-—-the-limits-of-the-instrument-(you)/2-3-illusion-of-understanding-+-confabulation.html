2-3 Illusion of understanding + confabulation

Part of 2 Act I — The Limits of the Instrument (you)

Familiarity is not comprehension, but the mind rarely signals the difference. Ask someone to rate how well they understand how a zipper works, then ask them to explain it step by step — the rating collapses. This is the illusion of explanatory depth: we mistake the ability to recognise, name, and use a thing for the ability to describe its mechanism. Fluency does the work. A concept we've heard about many times feels known, and that feeling of knowing is produced by processing ease, not by any stored causal model we could actually unpack.

Confabulation is what fills the gap when the machinery runs without the evidence. The brain has no direct readout of its own causes, so it manufactures a plausible story after the fact and presents it as introspection. Split-brain patients asked why they performed an action invented an explanation they could not possibly have had; participants in choice-blindness studies defend choices they never made. This is not lying — the storyteller believes the story. Damage to memory or to self-monitoring makes the effect dramatic, but the ordinary version is continuous with everyday life: you decide, then you explain the decision, and moral and political reasoning is especially prone to it.

The practical consequence is that introspection is a poor instrument for auditing your own understanding. The reliable test is generation, not recognition: try to write the explanation out, or teach it to a novice, and see where the sentence stalls. "Could I explain this to someone else?" is a far stricter question than "Do I get it?" — and the stalling point is exactly where real learning starts.

Terminology

  • Illusion of explanatory depth — the systematic overestimation of how well you understand a mechanism, revealed when you are asked to explain it in detail.
  • Confabulation — the production of a plausible but fabricated explanation for one's own behaviour, offered sincerely and without awareness of the fabrication.
  • Introspection illusion — the mistaken belief that introspection gives privileged, reliable access to the causes of one's own judgments and actions.
  • Processing fluency — the ease with which information is processed; it is misread as a signal of truth, familiarity, or understanding.
  • Choice blindness — the failure to notice that a choice one is asked to justify is not the choice one actually made.
  • Generative test — evaluating understanding by producing an explanation or prediction from scratch rather than by recognising a correct one.
