6-3 The map of mental models (how to think about thinking)

Part of 6 Act V — Living With It (synthesis)

A mental model is close to worthless in the form it is usually held: a name, a definition, and a memorable anecdote. That form survives exams and does nothing in a meeting. The gap is not knowledge — you can recite Goodhart's Law and still ship a target next quarter — it is that the model was never attached to anything that would make it fire. A map of mental models is not a catalogue of concepts. It is a retrieval index, a small set of distinctions for choosing between models, and a maintenance regime. And like every other map in this book, it compresses, and its failures live in what it discards.

Two ways to get this wrong

The catalogue failure. You accumulate thirty named concepts, can define each one, and reach for none. This is the illusion of explanatory depth wearing a syllabus: recognition is mistaken for capability. The test is the one from Act I — generation, not recognition. Try to run the model out loud on a live problem and see where the sentence stalls.

The single-lens failure. A model applied to everything stops being a model and becomes an ideology. "It's all incentives." "Everything is a feedback loop." "That's just Moloch." The tell is that the lens has become unfalsifiable: no observation could make you put it down. A tool that explains every case has left the empirical game, exactly as an immunizing stratagem does.

Both failures have the same root. In the first, the model never acquired a trigger. In the second, it never acquired a falsifier.

Three levels, and the failure at each

There is a territory, there are maps of it, and there is a map of the maps. This subsection is an object at the third level, which means it inherits the third level's characteristic disease.

Level 2 fails by reification — the model gets confused with the thing. Level 3 fails by collecting: the library grows, the confidence grows with it, and nothing in the system ever goes back outside. Notice what this map cannot show you: when to reach for which model, how a model feels when it is about to be misapplied, and whether the person holding the library is calibrated. Those live in practice, not in the diagram.

Organise by trigger, not by topic

The decisive design choice is what you index on. A library sorted by discipline, alphabet, or chapter is a reference work — useful for looking things up, useless under pressure, because you cannot look things up in the middle of a decision. Sort by presenting symptom instead, the way a clinician sorts a differential. The question is never "what do I know about systems?" but "what does this situation look and feel like?"

When you notice this Reach for these
Everyone is behaving sensibly and the outcome is terrible Moloch, commons, collective action, 1/N problem
The number improved and the underlying thing got worse Goodhart, distortion, legibility, Campbell's Law
We fixed it and the problem came back worse Second-order effects, fixes that fail, feedback delay
It worked here, so we assume it will work there Survivorship bias, base rates, reference class
They just don't get it Legibility vs. tacit knowledge, is/ought gap, disagreement
This will keep growing at this rate Exponential intuition, constraint, S-curve, doubling time
Competent people disagree and nobody is lying Prior, reference class, calibration, crux-finding
We've come too far to stop now Sunk cost, lock-in, ergodicity, absorbing barrier
It has lasted this long, so it must be good Lindy, path dependence, survivorship, robustness vs. merit
Everyone is being asked to try harder Collective action, enforcement cost, institutional design

Two habits make this table work. First, the right-hand column is a shortlist, not a verdict — its job is to get three candidates onto the table, not one. Second, whenever you add a model, add the symptom row with it, or you have added a fact rather than a tool.

Three kinds of thing, with different rules

Not everything in the library behaves the same way, and confusing the kinds is a common source of nonsense.

Kind Example How it earns its place Characteristic misuse
Lens Feedback loops, emergence, path dependence As a perceptual habit; by making you notice something you'd have missed Applied everywhere, becomes unfalsifiable
Rule Rule of 72, base rates, 1/N dilution By being checkable in its domain Applied outside its domain, silently wrong
Drill MAPS, veil of ignorance, pre-mortem, reversal test By being run, on a schedule, with an output Never scheduled, so never run

A lens cannot be falsified case by case and shouldn't be. A rule can be, and must be, and its domain matters — the rule of 72 is excellent arithmetic and useless for a population growing logistically. A drill is not a belief at all; it is a procedure, and its only failure mode is not happening.

What to do when two models disagree

Beginners assume that holding several models means averaging them. It does not. Models rarely contradict each other — they usually partition a situation, each describing a different mechanism that may be operating simultaneously. The work is not to pick a winner but to find which mechanism is binding right now, and that requires an observation that can tell them apart.

A short procedure:

  1. Check the kind. A lens and a rule cannot contradict each other; they answer different questions.
  2. Localise the crux. Convert the disagreement into the smallest sub-claim on which it turns.
  3. Name a discriminating observation. What would you see if mechanism A were binding rather than B? If nothing would tell them apart, you are not choosing between models — you are narrating.
  4. Ask about dominance. For most structural pairs (reinforcing vs. balancing loop, growth vs. constraint), the system's behaviour is a story about which one is currently winning.
  5. If it stays irreducible, weight by evidence and move — and state a settlement date, so the choice becomes scoreable.

Worked example: the churn spike

Monthly churn rose from 2.1% to 3.4% after you added an identity-verification step at signup. Here is the shortlist the trigger table produces, and what each one predicts:

  • Second-order effects. Friction at signup filters out exactly the marginal-but-legitimate users. Predicts: the effect appears in cohorts created after the change, is not seasonal, and is larger for users acquired through paid channels.
  • Goodhart. If activation is your North Star, support staff are pre-filling fields to keep it up. Predicts: activation holds steady while downstream retention falls, and the gap is concentrated in one team or region.
  • Survivorship / denominator drift. Churn is a ratio, and the denominator may have been redefined alongside the flow. Predicts: the break is an artefact — recompute under both definitions and the discontinuity softens.
  • Base rates. Churn rose in Q1 for the past three years. Predicts: this cohort looks like every other Q1; pull the reference class before doing anything.
  • Legibility. Manual review carried judgment the scripted flow cannot encode; the residue reappears somewhere the funnel does not measure. Predicts: chargebacks or support escalations rise while churn alone looks stable.
  • Ergodicity. 3.4% monthly compounds to roughly 34% annual attrition, and a small tail of accounts carries most of the revenue. Predicts: the mean churn is not the story; check the distribution and the top decile specifically.
  • Moloch. A competitor's price cut set a floor. Predicts: the rise is uniform across cohorts and correlates with their announcement, not with your change.

Note what the map did. It converted one alarming number into seven predictions with different signatures, and it named the observations that would separate them. That is the whole point: models earn their keep by generating discriminating observations, not by being true in general.

Installing a model rather than acquiring it

Knowing a model's name and being able to run it under load are different states, and the transition is practice, not reading.

Four specifics do most of the work. Name it out loud in the moment — "this looks like a fix that fails" — because labelling forces retrieval rather than recognition. Apply it to something with a settlement date, so the model's use, not just its content, can be scored. Run an after-action review with the model as the frame, asking which one would have caught what actually happened. And teach it, because generation is the only reliable test; the sentence that stalls is where your understanding stops.

Keep the active set small — somewhere between ten and twenty-five, each with a trigger, a failure case, and a use from the last year. Everything else is a library, and libraries are for lookup, not for thinking.

Falsifying your own library

A tool collection needs the same discipline as a belief. Four questions, run annually, take twenty minutes.

  • Which model did you apply in the last year and make things worse? If none, you have not been applying them under stakes.
  • Which model can you not describe a failure case for? That one is a candidate for reification or for rhetoric.
  • Which model do you reach for because it is flattering? Structural compulsion is real, and so is Moloch laundering — invoking the structure to justify what you wanted anyway.
  • Which model do you have a rhetorical use for and no operational use? That is a slogan with a bibliography.

The Lindy logic applies here as it does elsewhere, with the same caveat: old models have survived real selection pressure, which is evidence of durability rather than of quality. Keep them, and keep asking whether the terrain that selected them has moved.

What the map cannot show

Three things, and naming them is the honest close of a subsection about metacognition.

Timing and feel. Which model to reach for in the first thirty seconds of a conversation is tacit knowledge, learned by contact, not retrievable from any index. This is why the map is thin compared to the practice.

Your blind families. Everyone has a cluster they never use. The useful diagnostic is the one from disagreement theory: look at where your predictions have repeatedly gone wrong, and see which family of models would have caught it. That is your gap, and it is invisible from the inside.

Whether you are calibrated. The library can tell you to use base rates. It cannot tell you whether your priors are any good, whether your 80% is really 80%, or whether the class you chose was the flattering one. That requires scorekeeping, which is a practice rather than a page.

The map of mental models is therefore less an achievement than a regime — something run rather than possessed. Its health is measured by one thing: whether something outside it can still change what you think.

Terminology

Map of mental models — A structured, indexed collection of models and drills held for use in live decisions; distinguished from a catalogue of concepts by the presence of triggers, failure cases, and a maintenance routine.

Trigger (retrieval cue) — The symptom or situation pattern that causes a model to be recalled; the indexing key for a usable library.

Catalogue failure — Holding many named models and applying none; recognition mistaken for capability, the collective form of the illusion of explanatory depth.

Single-lens failure — Treating one model as universally applicable, so that it becomes unfalsifiable and ceases to be a model; Maslow's hammer in its epistemic form.

Lens / rule / drill — The three kinds of entry in a library: perceptual habits that cannot be falsified case by case, checkable heuristics with domains, and procedures that must be scheduled and run.

Discriminating observation — An observation whose outcome differs depending on which of two candidate models is binding; the mechanism by which multi-model reasoning is settled rather than averaged.

Dominance question — Asking which of several simultaneously operating mechanisms is currently determining the system's behaviour, rather than treating them as competing explanations.

Partition, not contradiction — The usual relationship between models: they describe different mechanisms operating at once, rather than incompatible accounts of the same one.

Multi-model reasoning — Using several models on one problem to generate a shortlist, localise the crux, and produce a discriminating test; never the averaging of their answers.

Installation (of a model) — The transition from recognising a model to running it under load, accomplished through live naming, application, scoring, and teaching rather than through reading.

Retrieval under load — Recalling and applying a model in the moment of a real decision, when the pressure and the missing information are what make it hard; the condition under which a model is genuinely held.

Blind family — The cluster of models a person systematically fails to reach for; detectable from a pattern of past prediction errors rather than from introspection.

Moloch laundering — Invoking structural compulsion to justify a preference that would otherwise require defending; the failure mode of a powerful model in the hands of someone who already knows what they want to do.

Library maintenance — The periodic audit of a model collection: which models were used, which made things worse, which have no failure case, and which are held for rhetoric rather than for use.

Third-level error — Confusing the map of models with either the models or the territory; the characteristic failure of a section about thinking about thinking, and the reason this one ends by naming what it cannot show.
