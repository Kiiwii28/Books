22-1 Connecting Every Factor- How the Pieces Build Toward Profit and Strategy

Part of 22 Tying It All Together

You've reached the final subsection of the book, and it's the one place where nothing new gets added. No new factor, no new definition, no new diagram of a market. This is where the twenty things you've studied stop being a list and become a system — and where you finally find out what the list was for.

Because something happens to almost everyone who learns about business one factor at a time. You finish reading about suppliers and you think about suppliers. You finish reading about tax and you think about tax. Each chapter feels like its own small world with its own vocabulary. Then you sit down to actually run the business and discover the factors refuse to stay in their boxes. A supplier raises their price, which becomes a pricing decision, which becomes a customer-service problem, which becomes a cash problem, which becomes a staffing problem — all inside six weeks, and none of it announced.

Nothing happens in isolation. That's the whole message of this subsection.

Everything is a lever on one of three things

Strip the language away and every business, from a market stall to a multinational, is doing the same arithmetic:

Profit = Revenue − Costs

Everything else is machinery for moving one of those two numbers. That's not a simplification for beginners — it's the actual structure. When you hear a strategist say "this business has a good position," they mean the factors are lined up in a way that raises revenue, lowers costs, or makes both more reliable.

So here is the whole book compressed into one table. Every factor you've studied points at profit somehow:

What the factor does The factors How it reaches profit
Creates revenue Market, Product, Sales, Stores, Distribution, Customer Service More buyers, higher price, more repeat purchases
Sets the revenue ceiling Competitors, Market, Regulations Caps what anyone in the market can charge
Adds cost Suppliers, Raw Materials, Labour, Manufacturing, Distribution, Stores, Tax Comes straight out of the margin
Converts revenue into cash Financials, Revenue, Costs Explains why profitable businesses can still run out of money
Decides how hard you can pull Company, Management Risk ceiling, decision quality, staying power
Sets the rules of the game Economy, Tax, Regulations What's allowed, what's taxed, what's scarce

Notice the two factors sitting in the "how hard you can pull" row — company and management. They're the odd ones out, and the earlier chapters made the case: company is the frame that decides how much risk you're allowed to carry, and management is the hand on the levers. Everything else is a lever or a condition.

Notice, too, that several factors appear twice. Market creates revenue and caps it. Tax adds cost and sets rules. Distribution earns revenue and burns cash. That's not sloppy bookkeeping — it's the first real proof that the factors overlap, and it's why reading any one chapter alone gave you an honest but partial picture.


Read the solid arrows as the part you control and the dotted arrows as the part you don't. Management picks and pulls; the factors produce an offer; the market decides what that offer is worth; the leftover profit gets ploughed back into the factors. The dotted lines are the reason no business is ever fully in charge of its own story — and the reason strategy exists at all.

Two loops, not a straight line

If this were a list, you'd work through it once and be done. It's not a list. There are two loops running simultaneously, and both of them compound.


Photo by Alex Agrico on Pexels

The profit loop. Profit isn't the finish line — it's fuel. Money left over funds better suppliers, better equipment, better training, a second van, a new website. Those improve the offer, which raises revenue or cuts cost, which produces more profit. Run this loop well and the business gets a little stronger every quarter. Run it badly and it gets a little weaker every quarter — so slowly that nobody notices until the bad month arrives and there's nothing left to absorb it.

The market's verdict loop. You don't grade your own homework. Customers decide whether what you built was worth what you charged, and they decide it every single day. That verdict arrives as revenue. And — this is the part people miss — it doesn't just judge your product. It judges the whole system. A brilliant loaf at a bad location, sold by a bored assistant, delivered late, is a poor product as far as the customer is concerned. The market grades the system, not the parts. That's why the Product chapter insisted a product is the total bundle of benefits a customer receives, not the thing in the box.

Which means the two loops are the same loop seen from different ends. Profit funds better levers, better levers produce a better offer, the market grades the offer, the grade shows up as revenue, and revenue produces profit. Around and around.

And here is where the Financials, Revenue and Costs chapters earn their place in the synthesis rather than sitting off to one side. They're not the engine — they're the instrument panel. Revenue tells you what the market just said. Costs tell you what the system consumed to earn that verdict. Financials tell you whether the verdict arrived as cash or as a promise. Without that panel you're driving the whole machine blind, pulling levers with no idea whether the wheel is turning.

Advantage compounds — and so does disadvantage

Here's where all that connects to something the opening chapter promised. Remember the two sandwich shops on the same street, both taking £1,000 a day, one keeping £170 and the other £30? Nothing about their ingredients or their customers differed. What differed was a hundred small management decisions, each one shaving or adding a percentage point.

That's compounding, and it works on every factor at once. The well-run shop negotiates a slightly better flour price (suppliers, raw materials). Its staff stay longer, so it wastes less and trains less (labour, staff). Its delivery is reliable, so customers come back (distribution, customer service). Its books are clean, so the bank lends it money for a second oven at a better rate (financials). Its reputation means it can charge 20p more and still fill the place (product, competitors).

None of those is decisive alone. Together they're the difference between £62,000 a year and £11,000.

And the reverse is the trap. A thin margin means you can't afford training, which means higher staff turnover, which means more mistakes and worse service, which loses customers, which thins the margin further. That's the spiral from the opening chapter, and it's the same mechanism running backwards. Advantage and disadvantage are both self-feeding. The strategist's job is largely to notice which direction the wheel is turning, and turn it.

One warning about the working-capital edge of this, straight out of the Financials and Distribution chapters. Growth is the most dangerous thing that can happen to a business running this loop badly. A growing business buys stock and pays wages before it collects from customers, so every extra sale can widen the cash gap. A struggling business that suddenly wins a large order can go under on the strength of its own success. Growth amplifies whichever loop you're already in — up or down. That's why the sticky-versus-flexible discipline below matters so much.

Not all levers respond to the same touch

There's a crucial asymmetry hiding inside all this, and it's the most practical idea in the subsection.

Some factors you can change on Monday morning. Prices, promotions, the rota, the order quantity, the response time to a complaint. Those are the flexible factors. They're how you handle today.

Some factors you change once and live with for years. The legal form of the company. A shop lease. A factory. A distribution network. The design of your core product. A long-term supplier contract. A senior hire. Those are the sticky factors. They're how you handle the next five years.

Flexible (adjust in days) Sticky (adjust in years)
Price and promotion Company structure and legal form
Staff rota and shift patterns Store locations and leases
Order quantities and stock levels Manufacturing plant and capacity
Service standards and scripts Product design and core range
Which customers you chase Distribution network and channel commitments
Which supplier you buy from this month Long-term supplier contracts and exclusivity

Here's the thing that separates a good manager from a good strategist: most people spend almost all their time on the flexible factors, and almost none on the sticky ones. That's backwards. A flexible decision is reversible next week, so getting it slightly wrong costs you little. A sticky decision is a bet on the future that you'll be paying for long after the world has changed around you.

There's a second dimension worth noticing: stickiness and time to pay off aren't the same thing. A marketing campaign is cheap to stop but slow to work. A lease is expensive to stop and certain to bill you monthly. The Economy chapter adds a third — sticky decisions made when interest rates are low can look brilliant for two years and ruinous for the next five, because the price of the money outlives the decision that borrowed it.

So the discipline is this. Ask of every decision: how long will I be living with this? The longer the answer, the more of your thinking time it deserves. That single habit will do more for you than any framework in this book.

You cannot win every row — and you shouldn't try

Once you accept that all twenty factors connect, a temptation appears: to try to be excellent at all of them. Cheaper than everyone and better and faster and broader.

That's not a strategy, it's a wish. Every factor you push on costs you something elsewhere. Being cheaper means thinner margins, which means less to spend on service. Being broader means more complexity, which means more cost and more things to get wrong. Being faster means holding more stock, which ties up cash. The Costs chapter's fixed-versus-variable split explains why the broader range hurts so much more than it looks: every extra product adds fixed complexity — training, shelf space, ordering, forecasting — whether or not it sells.

Remember Priya's scorecard against the café two doors down — roughly as many weaknesses as strengths, and yet a perfectly viable business. That's not a failure of the exercise. That's the lesson. A strategy is a choice about which rows to win, which rows to merely survive, and which rows to deliberately lose.


Photo by DS stories on Pexels

The test of a strategy isn't "are we good at things?" It's "do our choices fit together?" A bakery that charges premium prices, uses the cheapest ingredients, and opens at unpredictable hours has a set of choices that fight each other. The customer feels the contradiction even if they can't name it. A bakery that charges premium prices, sources carefully, knows its regulars by name, and opens reliably at 7am has choices that reinforce each other — and it feels like something, even to a first-time visitor.

Coherence beats excellence. It's also much cheaper to achieve.

And coherence is what makes the Competitors chapter usable. Rivals are what your choices are being compared against. A coherent set of choices gives you a position — a reason to be chosen that a rival can't copy without dismantling their own coherent set. Incoherent choices just make you a slightly different version of whoever's already there, and then the only thing that's left to compete on is price.

Management is the only factor that sees all the others

Here's the final piece, and it closes the loop the book opened with.

Nineteen factors are things you have, do, use, face or trade with. The twentieth — management — is the one that can look at all nineteen at once. That's not a nice compliment, it's a structural fact. Nobody else in the business sees the whole picture: the baker sees the bake, the salesperson sees the customer, the accountant sees the numbers. Only the manager — and in a small business, that's usually you — has the vantage point to notice that a supplier problem is about to become a pricing problem, or that a hiring decision is about to become a quality problem.


Photo by Jacob Yavin on Pexels

Which means the four jobs from the opening chapter aren't abstract theory. They're the mechanism:

  • Planning is choosing which rows to win and what you'll pay for them.
  • Organizing is putting people, money and materials where the plan needs them.
  • Leading is getting humans to actually want to do it — because none of this happens on its own.
  • Controlling is checking what actually happened against what you intended, and correcting. Which sends you straight back to planning.

That loop is the whole book in four words. And notice what it implies: strategy is not a document you write once. It's a habit of looking at all twenty factors, deciding which ones matter most right now, pulling the levers you can reach, and checking whether the wheel turned the way you expected.

Worked example: Priya's second site, viewed through all twenty factors

You've met Priya's bakery throughout this book. Right now it's one shop, a market stall, a wholesale contract with three cafés, and four staff. She's wondering about a second shop two miles away.

Watch how one decision touches everything. This is the synthesis in action — not a list of topics, but a single choice lighting up twenty dials at once.

The frame.

  • Company. A second lease means a second set of obligations. If she's still a sole trader, her house is on the line for both. The limited company from earlier in the book starts looking less like paperwork and more like an obvious move.
  • Management. She can't run two shops from one body. The real question isn't whether there are customers — it's whether she can find and trust someone to run the second. If she can't, the decision is dead regardless of the numbers. What is she really buying? Another shop, or her own job back as a baker?
  • Financials. Can the fit-out come out of cash, or does she need to borrow? Borrowing costs money monthly whether the second shop sells anything or not. And if she borrows, the Economy chapter's interest rate is now a permanent line in her costs.

The verdict.

  • Market. Are there enough buyers within walking distance of the second site? Commuters, offices, a school, a housing estate — the market is the catchment, not the postcode. Supply and demand set the price here just as they do anywhere: she's a price-taker for the first year and a price-setter only once she's established.
  • Competitors. Who's already there, how strong, and how will they respond? If a chain opened a branch on that corner last year, the answer changes everything.
  • Product. The same loaf, or something tailored to a different crowd? Either way the range has to be manageable from one production kitchen without doubling the complexity — and remember the life cycle: a product that's mature in shop one may be brand new to shop two's customers.

The inputs.

  • Suppliers. Can the mill deliver to two addresses at the same total price, or does the discount she's earned on volume now depend on the second site succeeding?
  • Raw Materials. Two shops double the flour order — genuine buying power, and a genuine risk if site two underperforms. Flour also has a shelf life, which turns a big order into waste if demand doesn't materialise.
  • Labour. A second team, a second rota, and the awkward problem of covering holidays across two sites instead of one. Skilled bakers are scarcer than shop assistants, and the Labour chapter's point holds: some labour you can hire in a week, some takes a year to train.
  • Manufacturing. One production kitchen supplying two shops, or bake on both sites? One is efficient but means a van run every morning and less freshness. Two is fresh but doubles the equipment and the skilled bakers you need. This single choice quietly determines her distribution cost, her product quality and her cost structure at the same time.

The route to the customer.

  • Stores. A lease is the stickiest decision on the list. Five years, rent due every month, rates, insurance, and a landlord who doesn't care how the croissants sold.
  • Distribution. Something has to move stock between the sites — a van, a driver, a schedule, and every one of those is a cost you didn't have last month.
  • Sales. Does the second shop take customers from the first, or add new ones? If it cannibalises, she's doubled her costs to stand still. Reach, not volume, is the thing she's actually buying.
  • Customer Service. Standards have to hold at both, because the brand is one brand. A rude assistant two miles away costs the original shop customers too.

The people.

  • Staff. The culture that makes the original shop work — the banter, the standards, the habit of noticing — doesn't replicate automatically. It has to be deliberately built, and that's exactly what her second manager is for.

The rules.

  • Economy. Is borrowing cheap or expensive this year? Is the town growing or shrinking? Is disposable income rising or being squeezed?
  • Tax. Business rates on a second premises. Payroll obligations for a bigger team, which scale with headcount rather than sales. Possibly a VAT threshold crossed — and crossing it changes her prices overnight.
  • Regulations. Food hygiene registration for a new site, employment law for more staff, and the paperwork that scales with headcount rather than revenue.

Read that list and notice something. There isn't a single factor that gives a clean yes or no. Every row is a consideration, a trade-off, a cost against a benefit. That's what strategy actually feels like. Anyone promising you a decision with one clean answer is selling you something.

Which means the honest question isn't "is a second site good?" It's:

Does this one decision make the whole system better — and can I survive the version where it doesn't?

The first half is strategy. The second half is why the company and financials chapters exist.

Where to look first when something feels wrong

One last piece of practical synthesis, because a strategist who can see twenty factors also needs to know where to point the torch.

The factors aren't equally good at telling you something. They're much better at different jobs. When something feels off but you can't name it, work in this order:

  1. Financials first. They're the instrument panel. Is the problem revenue, cost, or cash? Cash problems and profit problems feel identical in your stomach and are completely different in their cure. A profitable business short of cash needs payment terms changed, not more customers.
  2. Market and competitors second. Is the verdict loop turning? Falling revenue with stable costs points outward, at the customer and the alternative they've chosen.
  3. Costs third. Walk the cost lines one at a time — suppliers, raw materials, labour, manufacturing, distribution, stores, tax. Costs drift quietly and blame the wrong things.
  4. The people factors last, but not least. Bad service, rising mistakes and quiet resignations are usually symptoms, not causes. They're what a problem looks like once it has reached the humans.

That order isn't a rule. But it stops you doing what almost everyone does — noticing the symptom that shouts loudest, which is nearly always a staff or service problem, and treating it as the cause.

What this means for you as a strategist

Seven habits, and they're the practical yield of the whole book.

Read your business twice. Once as a list of factors — costs, staff, suppliers, product. Once as a comparison against rivals, factor by factor. The first view tells you what you have. The second tells you whether any of it is worth anything.

Follow every decision across the whole table. When you change something, ask what it does to revenue, to costs, to cash, to the people, to the market's verdict. Not to be paralysed — to be deliberate. Most expensive mistakes aren't wrong decisions, they're right decisions with unexamined consequences.

Spend your thinking time in proportion to stickiness. Flexible factors get a decision. Sticky factors get a project.

Choose coherence over excellence. You cannot win every row. Pick the two or three where you'll genuinely be better than the alternative your customer could choose, aim for "good enough" elsewhere, and accept being worse on the rows nobody pays for.

Watch the direction of the wheel. Is your margin compounding up or down? Small drifts are invisible month to month and decisive over years. That's what the controlling half of the management loop is for.

Treat profit as fuel, not a score. The number that matters isn't this year's profit — it's what this year's profit lets you do to the factors next year.

And remember which factor you actually are. You're not the flour, the van or the spreadsheet. You're the hand on the levers, the person who can see all twenty dials at once. That's the scarcest input in any business, and it's the one nobody else can supply.

Everything in this book has been a description of one thing or another. What you do with the descriptions — which rows you choose, which loops you feed, which levers you pull — is not in any of them. That part is you.

Summary

  • Every factor in this book is a lever on revenue, a lever on cost, or a condition that frames both — and profit is simply the difference.
  • The factors divide into six roles: those that create revenue, those that cap it, those that add cost, those that convert revenue into cash, those that decide how hard you can pull, and those that set the rules. Several factors play more than one role.
  • Company and management are different in kind: the frame and the hand. Everything else is a lever or a condition.
  • Two loops run permanently: the profit loop (profit funds better levers) and the market's verdict loop (customers grade the whole system, not the parts). Both compound — advantage and disadvantage equally, and growth amplifies whichever one you're in.
  • Financials, Revenue and Costs are the instrument panel: they don't drive the machine, they tell you what it's doing.
  • Factors differ by stickiness. Flexible ones you adjust in days; sticky ones you live with for years. Spend your thinking time in proportion to how long you'll be living with the answer.
  • Strategy is not being good at everything. It's coherence — a set of choices that reinforce each other, with deliberate decisions about which rows to win and which to concede.
  • Management is the only factor that can see all the others, which makes planning, organizing, leading and controlling the mechanism by which the whole system actually moves.
  • The strategist's recurring question: does this decision improve the whole system — and can I survive the version where it doesn't?
