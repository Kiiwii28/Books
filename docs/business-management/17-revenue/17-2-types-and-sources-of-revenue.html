17-2 Types and Sources of Revenue

Part of 17 Revenue

Two businesses can report exactly the same revenue and be in completely different health. One is coasting on a contract it signed two years ago and gets paid whether anyone lifts a finger today or not. The other starts every single morning at zero and has to go out and win the whole month again from scratch. Same number on the top line. Very different business.

You've already met revenue as price × the number of completed sales. This subsection is about the fact that revenue isn't one river — it's a set of streams, and the mix of those streams tells you more about a business than the total ever will.

Two questions, not one

Before we sort anything, get this split straight, because most confusion about revenue comes from muddling it.

The type of revenue is about how you're charged. Is it a one-off sale? A monthly subscription? A commission on somebody else's transaction?

The source of revenue is about where the money comes from. Which product line? Which customer? Which channel? Which country?


Both matter, for different reasons. The type tells you how predictable the money is and how much it costs to keep coming. The source tells you how exposed you are — because if 60% of your revenue comes from one customer, one product or one shop, you don't really have a business; you have a bet.

The types of revenue

One-off transactional revenue

The familiar one. You sell a thing once, and the money arrives at or near the moment of the sale. A loaf over the counter, a haircut, a sofa, a plumbing repair.

Its virtues are simplicity and speed — the cash lands quickly and there's no commitment to honour. Its weakness is that it leaves nothing behind. You start the next day at zero, and the whole amount has to be won again from scratch. This is the world of transactional selling from the Types of Sales section, and it's the natural fit for convenience products, which is why the bakers, barbers and cafés of the world live and die by footfall.

Recurring revenue

Subscriptions, contracts, retainers, memberships, maintenance agreements, a standing weekly bread order. You win it once and it repeats — until the customer cancels.

This is the single most valuable type of revenue a business can have, and it's worth being explicit about why. Every pound of recurring revenue was sold once and collected many times. Compare that with one-off revenue, where every pound must be chased down individually. The Sales section made the point that hunting new customers is expensive and farming existing ones is cheap; recurring revenue is what happens when you build the business around farming.

The catch is that it isn't really yours. It's rented from the customer, month by month, and the rent is paid in service. That's why the Customer Service chapter is, in a very real sense, a revenue chapter in disguise. Recurring revenue is protected by how the customer feels about you, not by the contract.

Usage-based and metered revenue

Here you charge for what's consumed rather than for access: electricity by the kilowatt-hour, cloud computing by the gigabyte, a card platform by the transaction, a delivery firm by the mile.

It's an elegant type, because the customer pays in proportion to the value they got, and your revenue grows automatically as their use grows — no renegotiation required. But it's also the least predictable of the trading types, because it depends entirely on somebody else's behaviour. A single large customer cutting back their usage can move your month without anybody cancelling anything. Watch the link to Costs here too: metered revenue usually pairs with metered costs, which is comfortable, but only if your cost per unit stays below your price per unit.

Commission, brokerage and advertising revenue

This is the middleman's cut you met in the Market chapter — you're paid for closing a gap rather than for making a product. A sales agent earns a percentage, a marketplace takes a slice, a broker matches a buyer to a seller. Advertising revenue belongs here too: a newspaper, a website or a delivery app sells attention, and the size of that stream depends on the size of the audience, which is a completely different asset from anything on a shelf.

The trade-off is familiar. Commission revenue scales beautifully with no stock and no capital, but you rarely control the price, and your income depends on somebody else's volume passing through your hands.

Rent, licence and royalty revenue

Charging for an asset instead of a product. Renting out the function room above the shop. Licensing a recipe to another bakery. A franchise fee. A royalty on a patent or a brand name.

The distinctive feature here is that an extra licence sold costs you almost nothing to deliver — the asset already exists. That makes this the highest-margin type of revenue in the book, at the margin. It's also the classic way to earn from something you already own but aren't using: a skill, a recipe, a brand, an empty room, a piece of software.

Revenue that isn't trading

Interest on cash sitting in the bank. The sale of an old van. A government grant, a training subsidy, an insurance payout. A bakery selling day-old bread to a café, or a sawmill selling sawdust.

These are real money, and you should absolutely collect them — but keep them mentally separate from trading revenue. They don't repeat on their own, they rarely respond to effort, and they can make a struggling business look like a growing one. The Financials chapter draws the line as operating versus non-operating income, and the Tax chapter often treats the two differently. A grant also usually arrives with strings attached, which is the Regulations chapter arriving in your bank account.

The sources of revenue

Now slice the same money a different way. A source is a stream you can point at, measure, and decide to grow or drop — and the discipline of naming your sources is what turns "we made £12,000" into something you can actually manage.

By product or line. Bread, cakes, classes, flour. The trap here is that revenue is a flattering number. A line can be your biggest seller and still contribute almost nothing after its direct costs, because revenue says nothing about what's left. Whenever you rank sources, rank them by contribution — the Costs chapter's measure of what a sale leaves behind once its direct costs are paid — not by revenue alone.

By customer or segment. Consumers, other businesses, the public sector. You've met these buyers in the Types of Products and Types of Sales sections; here the point is concentration. One customer worth 40% of your revenue is a business you don't fully control. If they leave, or decide to make it themselves, you have a very bad quarter. The Suppliers chapter asked how easily you could walk away from a supplier; the mirror question is how easily your biggest customer could walk away from you.

By channel. The same loaf sold over a counter, through a delivery app, and wholesale to a café is three different revenue sources with three different prices, three different costs and three different sets of competitors. This is the Distribution chapter's central lesson: channel changes your margin and your rivals. In the accounts they all look like revenue. In reality they are not the same money at all.

By geography and by contract type. Domestic versus export brings currency, duties and different regulations along with the sales. Spot revenue (whoever walks in today) versus contracted revenue (agreed volumes at agreed prices) is the difference between guessing and knowing.

Two businesses, same revenue

Here's why the mix matters more than the total.

Same revenue, same industry size, same effort on a good day. But one of these businesses is predictable, and predictable revenue can be planned around: you can hire, lease premises and buy stock with confidence. The other is a permanent treadmill, and every quiet week is a cash problem. No prizes for guessing which one sleeps better.

Worked example: Priya's revenue mix

Priya's bakery, whom you've followed through this whole book, brings in roughly £12,820 a month. But look at where it comes from.

Source Type Monthly revenue Direct costs Contribution
Counter sales One-off, self-service £8,000 £2,800 £5,200
Delivery app orders One-off, commission £1,800 £1,320 £480
Café wholesale contracts Recurring, contracted £1,040 £480 £560
Bread-making classes One-off, booked ahead £1,440 £240 £1,200
Celebration cakes One-off, made to order £540 £165 £375
Total £12,820 £5,005 £7,815

Three things jump out, and each one is a lesson you can use on your own business.

Revenue and contribution tell different stories. The delivery app brings 14% of revenue but only 6% of contribution, because the platform's commission and the ingredients eat most of it. The classes bring 11% of revenue but 15% of contribution. If Priya ranked her sources by revenue alone, she'd invest in entirely the wrong one.

She's heavily concentrated. Nearly two-thirds of her revenue comes from one source — the counter — which depends on a single location, a single street and a single footfall pattern. If a big employer nearby closes, that stream drops and takes the business with it. This is the Competitors chapter's indirect-rival problem arriving as a revenue risk.

Not all revenue is new revenue. Before celebrating the app, Priya has to ask an uncomfortable question: are those app customers genuinely new, or are they people who used to walk into the shop and now order at home? A new source that simply moves existing sales isn't growth — it's the same revenue with an extra 25% commission attached.

Finally, note that £12,820 of revenue and £7,815 of contribution still has to carry around £7,000 of fixed costs — rent, wages, energy, insurance. That leaves roughly £800 of profit before tax, a margin of about 6%. Revenue is where the story starts, not where it ends.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • List your revenue types and sources, with numbers. You cannot manage a mix you've never written down. Most owners find one stream they'd forgotten and one they'd overestimated.
  • Rank sources by contribution, never by revenue. A big, busy, low-margin source is a good way to work hard and stay poor.
  • Prize recurring revenue, and price it accordingly. It's worth more than one-off revenue even when the totals are identical, because it's cheaper to keep than to win. Just remember it's rented from the customer, and service pays the rent.
  • Watch concentration. One customer, one product or one channel holding more than a third of revenue is a strategic risk, not a compliment.
  • Check that new revenue is genuinely new. Diversifying into a channel that cannibalises your existing sales just adds cost and complexity.
  • Run the loop. Set a target mix, organize the channels and people who deliver it, lead the team through the slow months, and then control it with four numbers: revenue by source, contribution by source, share of recurring revenue, and your largest customer's percentage of the total. Review them monthly — the mix drifts faster than you'd think.

Summary

  • Revenue has types (how you're charged) and sources (where the money comes from), and the two questions are separate.
  • The main types are one-off transactional, recurring, usage-based, commission or advertising, licensing or rental, plus non-trading income like interest, grants and asset sales.
  • Recurring revenue is the most valuable type, because it's won once and collected repeatedly — and it's protected by service, not by the contract.
  • Sources are best sliced by product line, by customer segment, by channel, and by geography or contract type — and each slice changes your margin, your rivals and your risk.
  • Rank sources by contribution, not revenue. Revenue flatters; contribution tells the truth.
  • Predictability and concentration are what separate a business from a bet. A predictable mix lets you plan, hire and invest; a concentrated one leaves you exposed to a single decision made by somebody else.
  • Build a deliberate mix — several sources, a healthy share of recurring revenue, no single customer or channel holding the whole thing up — and measure it on a rhythm, the same way you'd control any other part of the business.
