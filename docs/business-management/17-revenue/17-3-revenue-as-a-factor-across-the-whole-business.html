17-3 Revenue as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 17 Revenue

Most owners have one number they check before anything else in the day, and it's almost always revenue. Which is a bit like judging a football team purely by how much possession it had. Useful, yes. Decisive, no — and dangerously misleading on its own.

But here's the thing worth holding onto for this whole subsection: revenue isn't just the outcome at the end of the business. It's a factor that runs through all of it. It funds what you can afford to do, it decides which of the other chapters in this book even get to happen, and it's the earliest signal you'll get that your strategy is working or quietly failing.

Same revenue, two completely different businesses

Two firms. Both turn over £200,000 a year. That single sentence makes them sound like twins. They aren't.

Firm A Firm B
Revenue £200,000 £200,000
Where it comes from One customer = 70% of it 400 customers, none above 3%
Contribution margin 12% 45%
Repeatability Re-tendered every year Weekly habit
Money left to cover fixed costs £24,000 £90,000

Firm A is doing £200,000 of work and keeping £24,000 to run a building, pay salaries and survive a bad month. Firm B is doing the same £200,000 and keeping nearly four times as much — with a customer base that can't walk out in a single meeting.

Same top line. Two entirely different businesses. So the question a strategist asks is never "how much revenue do we have?" — it's "what kind of revenue is it?"

Revenue funds every other factor

Remember the loop that's run through this book: profit funds the next round of capability, and capability produces the next round of sales. Revenue is the point where that loop gets its oxygen.


Everything you've read about so far is paid for out of this flow. Labour is wages — which come from revenue. Raw materials and suppliers are paid from revenue, and usually before the customer pays you back. Manufacturing capacity, stores, vans, distribution routes, software, insurance, the accountant — revenue again.

That's why a fall in revenue is so much more serious than it looks on paper. It isn't one line moving down. It's the flow that keeps every other factor alive, and it's the thing that decides whether you can reinvest in being better next year or start cutting the things that made you good in the first place.

Revenue has a ceiling, and you don't set it

Here's a hard truth from the Market chapter: revenue can never exceed the size of your market multiplied by the share you can realistically win.

If your market is worth £2 million a year and you hold 10%, you're doing £200,000. To double revenue you must either take share from competitors (a fight, with weapons, against people who won't sit still) or find a bigger market (a new region, a new customer segment, a new channel).

Both are legitimate. What isn't legitimate is setting a revenue target that ignores the ceiling. A goal of "double revenue" in a flat market with three entrenched rivals isn't a plan — it's a wish with a spreadsheet attached.

And remember from Types of Competition: the structure of your market decides how much power you have over the price half of the revenue equation. In a near-commodity market, price is handed to you and every rival matches your discount within a week. In a differentiated one, you have a little room to charge more. Revenue won't grow the same way in both.

The number you actually manage is smaller

Revenue is price × quantity. Break quantity down and you get four levers you can genuinely pull:

price × customers × how often they buy × how much they buy each time

That's it. If revenue is growing, one of those four moved — and knowing which one is the difference between an achievement and an accident. A revenue rise caused by more customers is a different beast from one caused by a price rise that's slowly driving customers away.

There's a second useful trick: revenue per something. Revenue per labour hour (the Labour chapter's productivity lens). Revenue per square metre (from Stores). Revenue per delivery or per channel (from Distribution). Revenue per product line (the Product life cycle's stage detector). These ratios show you where your revenue is really coming from — and they often reveal that a chunk of it is being bought at a price you can't afford, once you count the effort it took to earn.

Quantity is not quality

Before you celebrate a rise in revenue, run it through four tests:

  • Is it profitable? Revenue without contribution is just activity. The Costs chapter's arithmetic — price minus variable cost — decides whether more sales make you richer or busier.
  • Is it reliable? Recurring revenue from a subscription, a contract or a weekly habit behaves nothing like a scatter of one-off sales. Predictable revenue is worth more than lumpy revenue of the same size, because you can plan around it.
  • Is it diverse? Concentrated revenue is borrowed revenue. If one customer is a quarter of your income, they set your prices, your payment terms and your future.
  • Is it real? During inflation, revenue rises simply because prices did. A 5% revenue increase in a 6% inflation year is a decline in disguise — your costs went up faster than your sales did. The Economy chapter isn't background noise here; it's the lens that turns a nominal number into a true one.

And one more: revenue isn't all yours. Sales tax and VAT are collected on it and passed to the state, which is the Tax chapter sitting quietly inside every invoice you send.

Worked example: Priya's supermarket contract

Priya's bakery currently turns over about £150,000 a year, drawn from her counter, a few wholesale café accounts, the Saturday market and occasional classes. Contribution runs at roughly 40%, fixed costs are about £48,000, and profit lands around £12,000 — a thin but real 8%.

Then a supermarket chain offers a supply contract worth £60,000 a year. A 40% jump in revenue, from one handshake.

Tempting. Let's price it honestly. The contract is won on price and reliability, not on craft, so contribution on that work is nearer 15%. It also needs a bigger oven on finance, a second van, and an extra part-time baker — about £8,000 a year in additional fixed costs. And it fills the same early-morning hours as the loaves that earn her 45% at the counter.

Per year Now With the contract
Revenue £150,000 £210,000
Contribution £60,000 £69,000
Fixed costs £48,000 £56,000
Profit £12,000 £13,000
Largest customer as share of revenue ~8% ~29%

Revenue up 40%. Profit up about 8%. And the business is now nearly a third dependent on a single customer who can move the contract to a rival at the next review.

That doesn't automatically make the answer "no". The supermarket might open doors, fill the ovens in quiet hours, and stabilise cash flow. But look at what had to be weighed: the Market ceiling and pricing power, the Costs contribution, the Manufacturing capacity, the Distribution route, the Labour rota, the Stores space, the Financials cash, and the Competitors reaction. One revenue decision, touching eight chapters.

That's what "a factor across the whole business" means.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Judge revenue by quality, not size. Margin, repeatability, diversity and realism. A smaller, sturdier top line beats a bigger, fragile one almost every time.
  • Always ask what a pound of revenue leaves behind. Growth that doesn't cover its own cost of winning and serving isn't growth — it's a slow leak wearing a good mood.
  • Know your ceiling. Market size × achievable share. Then decide whether growth means taking share, finding a new market, or going deeper with the customers you already have.
  • Watch the timing, not just the total. Revenue is a promise; cash is the delivery. Payment terms, seasonality and stock all sit between the two — that's the Financials chapter doing its work.
  • Treat the revenue line as your instrument panel. It's the first number to move, long before anything reaches your accounts. A dip in a growing market means you're losing share; a dip in one product line is the Product life cycle talking.
  • Close the loop. Set revenue as a goal, organise the channels and people to chase it, lead the team through the weeks it doesn't come, and control it with a handful of numbers — conversion, basket size, revenue per hour, and revenue per customer. That's the management cycle from the opening of this book, pointed at the top line.

Summary

  • Revenue isn't the scoreboard at the end of the business — it's the flow that funds every other factor, from raw materials and labour to capacity, stock and distribution.
  • Identical revenue can describe completely different businesses. Quality — margin, reliability, diversity, realism — matters at least as much as quantity.
  • Revenue has a ceiling: market size × achievable share. Market structure decides how much of the price half of the equation you actually control.
  • Revenue is price × customers × frequency × basket size — four levers, each owned by a different chapter, and worth checking per labour hour, per square metre and per channel.
  • Growth and profit can move in opposite directions. Every extra pound of revenue carries a cost of winning and serving it.
  • Concentrated revenue is borrowed revenue. One large customer quietly sets your prices, your terms and your future.
  • In an inflationary year, a revenue rise can be a decline in disguise — and a slice of every invoice belongs to the tax authority, not to you.
  • Strategically: chase profitable, repeatable, spread-out revenue, and treat the top line as the earliest warning system you have.
