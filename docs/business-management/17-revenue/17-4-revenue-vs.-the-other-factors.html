17-4 Revenue vs. The Other Factors

Part of 17 Revenue

Revenue Is Where All the Arrows Point

Here's a way to think about this chapter's factor that will pay off for the rest of the book.

Revenue is the money that comes in from customers. Everything else in this book is doing one of four things: creating that money, spending it, limiting it, or regulating it. So comparing revenue with the other factors isn't really a list of twenty separate comparisons — it's one question asked twenty ways: which direction does the arrow point between revenue and this factor?

Sometimes the arrow points into revenue. Your product, your sales team, your store's location, a supplier who actually delivers — these push money in the door. Sometimes the arrow points out of revenue. Staff wages, raw materials, rent, tax — these are paid with the money you collected. And sometimes the factor just sets the boundaries: the market decides how much you can charge, and regulations decide whether you're allowed to sell the thing at all.

Hold on to that mental model and the rest of this section becomes a map rather than a memory test.

Revenue Is a Result, Not an Activity

The single most important comparison is the one with Management and Company, because it frames all the others.

You cannot "do" revenue. It's a symptom. When a manager says "we need more revenue this quarter," they haven't actually said anything actionable. What they mean is one of a handful of concrete things: sell to more customers, sell more to each customer, charge more, sell more often, or stop losing the customers you already have. Revenue is the output of decisions made everywhere else in the business.

That has a strategic consequence you'll meet again and again: revenue is a lagging indicator. Today's revenue is the harvest of decisions taken months or years ago — the product you built, the price you set, the salesperson you hired, the supplier relationship you built. If revenue is falling now, the cause is probably upstream and old. A strategist reading a revenue line is reading history; a strategist reading their factors is reading the future.

Revenue vs. the Factors That Create It

Company and Management: who you are and what you're allowed to chase

Company sets the container. A two-person sole trader has a natural revenue ceiling of two people's working hours. A limited company with backing can chase a contract worth more than its annual revenue, because it can hire and borrow to deliver it. The legal form, the brand, the reputation — these determine which customers will even take your call.

Management decides the revenue architecture: which products to push, which markets to enter, which price point to defend, where to spend the money revenue generates. Management is also the factor that decides what revenue is for. A business that chases revenue to look impressive while margins quietly collapse has a management problem, not a revenue problem — and we'll come back to that when we talk about Costs.

Product: revenue lives inside a life cycle

Here's where the Product chapter earns its keep. A product doesn't produce flat revenue forever. In the growth stage, revenue climbs fast as word spreads. In maturity, it plateaus and then competition grinds the price down. In decline, it slides.

This means a healthy business's revenue is never one number — it's a portfolio of curves at different stages. A bakery might have sourdough in maturity (steady, dependable), a new pastry line in growth (climbing), and last year's fad cookie in decline. Total revenue can look flat while underneath, everything is moving. That's why "revenue is up 5%" is almost never a complete sentence.

Competitors: revenue is relative

Revenue has a twin that never appears on your own accounts: market share. If the market grew 20% and your revenue grew 5%, you didn't win — you lost ground. The Competitors chapter matters here because a rival's new product, price cut, or clever promotion doesn't just dent your revenue, it tells you something about your pricing power. Businesses with a real competitive advantage can raise prices without losing customers. Businesses without one can only win on volume, which is an expensive, exhausting way to grow.

Market and Economy: the size of the room and the weather

The Market sets the ceiling. No amount of brilliant management lets you sell 50,000 units a month in a town that buys 3,000. And remember the supply-and-demand mechanics: the market sets the price you can charge, not just the quantity you can sell. Since revenue is price × quantity, the market is effectively shaping both halves of your top line at once.

The Economy is the weather in that room. In an expansion, customers buy more and tolerate higher prices. In a contraction, they trade down, delay purchases, and haggle. Two companies with identical products and identical skills can post wildly different revenue in the same year purely because of which economy they're selling into. You can't control the weather — but you can decide how exposed you are to it.

Sales, Distribution, Stores and Customer Service: the last mile

These four are revenue's delivery system, and each one leaks if you're careless.

Sales is the most direct lever: pipeline, conversion, average deal size. Notice how a sales team's incentives quietly shape your revenue mix — pay commission on total deal value and you'll sell cheap volume; pay on margin and you'll sell fewer, better deals.

Distribution changes revenue per unit, not just reach. Selling wholesale to a café chain at £4 a bag is a very different revenue line from selling direct to consumers at £9 a bag — same product, same customer drinking it, half the revenue to you. Channel choice is a revenue decision.

Stores is the physical version of the same idea. Revenue per square foot, foot traffic, location — a great product in a bad location generates a fraction of the revenue it deserves.

Customer Service is the quietest revenue factor of all, because it works through repeats. A customer who buys once is a transaction; a customer who buys for ten years is an annuity. Service is what converts the first into the second. Every unresolved complaint is revenue walking out the door — often taking two or three referred friends with it.

Manufacturing, Raw Materials and Suppliers: the ceiling you can't sell through

These three usually get discussed as cost factors, and they are — but they also hard-cap revenue.

If your manufacturing can only produce 500 units a week, you cannot sell 800, however much demand exists. If a raw material runs short or a supplier fails to deliver, you have nothing to sell and revenue stops. And factory quality matters too: defective output doesn't just cost you the sale, it costs you the refund, the replacement, and sometimes the customer forever.

The nuance worth remembering: supply problems hit costs first and revenue second. A supplier raising prices squeezes your margin immediately but barely touches revenue. A supplier failing destroys revenue overnight. Same factor, two very different risks.

Revenue vs. the Factors That Spend It

Costs and Financials: the ceiling and the scoreboard

Costs and revenue are the two numbers that matter most to each other, because profit is simply the gap between them. Revenue sets the ceiling on what you can possibly spend. That's why growing revenue is so attractive: it buys you room to invest, hire, and experiment.

But be careful — this is where a lot of businesses quietly die. Revenue growth that arrives with proportionally bigger costs isn't progress. Adding £10,000 of revenue at 12% margin while straining your staff, your delivery fleet, and your patience is often worse than adding £4,000 at 50% margin with room to breathe. Profitable revenue is the goal; revenue alone is a vanity number.

Financials is the bookkeeping lens on all of this. Two things it teaches: revenue is recognised under rules, not whenever cash arrives — so a big month can look fantastic on the income statement while your bank account is empty because the customer pays in ninety days. Revenue is not cash. Never confuse the two.

Revenue vs. the Rules of the Game

Tax and Regulations: what you collect isn't always yours

Tax catches out more beginners than any other factor. Sales tax or VAT collected on your sales is not your revenue — you're acting as an unpaid tax collector, passing it to the government. Counting it as your money is a painful rite of passage. Beyond that, tax shapes behaviour: tax your product heavily (excise duties on alcohol, tobacco, sugar) and the price rises, volumes fall, and your revenue base shrinks without you changing a thing.

Regulations decide whether the revenue is legal at all. A licence to operate, a product safety standard, a restriction on advertising to children — these can open a revenue stream, close one overnight, or make one so expensive to comply with that it stops being worth it. Regulations are rarely revenue-driven, but they are frequently revenue-defining.

The Loop: Revenue Funds the Factors That Create Revenue

The comparisons above look one-directional, but the real system is circular. The factors create revenue, and revenue pays for the factors that will create tomorrow's revenue.


Read that loop forwards and you get growth: revenue funds capacity, capacity enables volume, volume generates more revenue. Read it backwards and you get the death spiral every struggling business knows: thin revenue starves capacity, weak capacity loses customers, lost customers cut revenue further. Same loop, opposite direction.

This is why strategists obsess over revenue quality — not just how much, but what kind. Recurring subscription revenue is far more valuable than one-off sales, because it makes the loop predictable and lets you invest with confidence. Revenue concentrated in one huge customer is fragile; if they leave, the loop snaps. Revenue with healthy margins feeds the loop faster than revenue without.

A Worked Example: The Coffee Roaster

Say you run a small coffee roastery with three revenue streams in a month:

Stream How it's calculated Revenue
Subscriptions 1,000 subscribers × £20 £20,000
Wholesale to cafés 40 cafés × £400 £16,000
Own café walk-ins Foot traffic × average spend £9,000
Total £45,000

Now change one factor at a time and watch how differently revenue responds.

  • Raise prices 10% across the board: revenue goes to £49,500, and because your beans, staff and delivery costs are unchanged, nearly all of that extra £4,500 lands as profit. Risk: some subscribers cancel.
  • Sell 10% more volume instead: also about £49,500 — but you now need 10% more green beans, roasting time, packing and delivery. Revenue rises, profit rises far less.
  • Lose your biggest wholesale account (say a chain worth £6,000): revenue drops 13% instantly, and the beans you'd bought for them may spoil.
  • Open a second café: revenue may rise — but only once you've paid rent, staff wages and fit-out costs long before the customers show up.

Same revenue target, four completely different businesses. That's the strategist's lesson from this subsection: revenue tells you what happened; the other factors tell you why, and what to do next.

Summary

  • Revenue is an output, not an action. You create it by managing price, volume, mix and retention — the other factors — not by wanting it harder.
  • Factors split into four roles: those that create revenue (product, sales, distribution, stores, service, competitors' weakness), those that spend it (costs, staff, materials, tax), those that cap it (market size, manufacturing capacity, supply, economy), and those that rule on it (regulations, tax law).
  • Price × volume is the whole game, and the market plus your competitive advantage set much of both. A price rise is usually far more profitable than an equal percentage volume rise.
  • Revenue is not cash and not profit. It's the top line, the ceiling on spending, and the fuel for the loop that builds tomorrow's capacity. Judge it by quality — recurring, diversified, high-margin, and collectable — not by size.
  • Read revenue as history. Read the factors as the future. The strategist who watches the factors gets a head start on the number everyone else is staring at.
