17-1 What Revenue Really Means

Part of 17 Revenue

The number everyone quotes

Every business conversation eventually reaches for the same figure. "We did £40k last month." "Revenue was up 12%." "We need a million in revenue." It's the headline number — the one investors ask about first, the one that goes on the slide, the one that makes a small business feel real.

It's also the number most people misunderstand. Because revenue, on its own, tells you almost nothing about whether a business is any good. A company can double its revenue and go bankrupt in the same year. Both of those facts are true, and they aren't a contradiction — they're a definition problem.

So let's fix the definition.

The definition worth keeping

Revenue is the total value of what a business has sold in a period — its ordinary earnings from doing the thing it exists to do, before any costs are taken out.

Three pieces of that sentence are doing the work.

Sold. Not "offered", not "planned", not "had a really promising meeting about". Revenue counts completed exchanges. The Sales chapter called the sale the moment the loop closes; revenue is the scoreboard that keeps the tally.

In a period. Revenue is always measured over a stretch of time — a day, a week, a quarter, a year. It's a flow, not a pile. "We have £40,000 of revenue" means nothing until you attach "a month" or "a year", and the difference between those two is the difference between a thriving bakery and a catastrophe.

Before any costs. This is the part that matters most. Revenue sits at the very top of the income statement, which is why it's called the top line. Everything you've met in this book so far — the flour, the wages, the oven, the rent, the van, the platform's commission — is deducted below it. Revenue is the size of the pot. Profit is what's still in the pot after everyone else has been paid.

Revenue is price × units

Strip revenue back to its bones and there's only one formula:

Revenue = price × number of units sold

Two numbers, multiplied. Every revenue conversation in business is really a conversation about one of them, or both. But look at what each one costs you to move, because they are not symmetrical.

Units are hard work. Another hundred loaves means more flour, more oven time, more hours, more packaging, more delivery. Every extra unit drags a variable cost along with it — the direct cost from the Costs chapter that rises with every sale. Volume grows revenue and grows costs alongside it.

Price is nearly free. Raise your price by 50p and you don't buy any extra flour or pay any extra wages — the extra money drops straight through to the bottom of the page. That's why the Market chapter's obsession with elasticity matters so much here: a price rise is the cheapest revenue you will ever find, if your customers stay. If they don't, you've shrunk your revenue while keeping every cost exactly where it was.

Hold those two levers in mind, because nearly every decision in the rest of this chapter is one of them in disguise.

What revenue is not

It is not profit. Profit is what's left after the costs of earning that revenue. A bakery turning over £2,000 a week with £1,950 of costs is not a £2,000 business. A £20,000 car and £20,000 of bread are not remotely the same achievement.

It is not cash. Revenue is recorded when you earn it; cash arrives when the customer pays. Sell £5,000 to a café chain on 60-day terms and you have £5,000 of revenue and £0 of the money. That gap — the working-capital problem from the Financials chapter — is why so many fast-growing businesses die while their revenue charts look magnificent.

It isn't all yours. Sales tax, VAT and similar charges are collected on behalf of the government. That money passes through your accounts on its way to somebody else, and the Tax chapter will tell you exactly what you owe and when. A £12 sale including £2 of VAT is £10 of your revenue, not £12.

It isn't the same as an order. A signed contract, a booked wedding, a pre-order — all wonderful, and none of them revenue yet. They become revenue when you've delivered what you promised.

It has to be earned, not just announced

There's a whole body of accounting rules about when revenue counts, and the principle underneath them is refreshingly simple: revenue is recognised when you've done the thing you were paid to do.

Priya takes a £180 booking for a wedding cake in March. The cake is delivered in June. She hasn't earned £180 in March — she's earned a promise and an obligation. The revenue lands in June, when the cake leaves the building and the customer takes it away.

This isn't pedantry. It's the reason a business can look rich on paper and be broke in the bank, and it's why a manager who watches only the order book gets badly surprised. Orders are hope. Revenue is achievement.

And it's usually smaller than it looks

Most businesses quote a gross figure and quietly earn a net one. Between the two sit three things.

  • Discounts. A 10% discount is a 10% cut in revenue — and, as the Sales chapter worked out, potentially most of your profit.
  • Returns and refunds. Sold and then given back isn't revenue. It's a cost of doing business with a return attached.
  • Commission and channel cuts. When a delivery platform takes 25%, your revenue on that order is what's left — £3.00 from a £4.00 loaf. That's the Distribution chapter's lesson in one line.

Net revenue — what you actually earned — is the figure worth planning around. Gross revenue is the figure worth telling stories about, and the two are often several percent apart.

Worked example: one week at Priya's bakery

Let's put real numbers on it.

Stream Price Units Revenue
Loaves over the counter £4.00 600 £2,400
Pastries and cakes £2.50 400 £1,000
Wholesale bread to cafés £2.60 400 £1,040
Bread-making class (8 seats) £45.00 6 £270
Gross revenue £4,710

Now the honest corrections. A delivery app sold 90 of those loaves at £4.00 and kept a quarter of the money. Two wholesale orders came back unsold at the end of the week. And £785 of the total is VAT she's collecting for the tax office rather than earning for herself.

Correction Effect
Platform commission (90 × £1.00) −£90
Returns — unsold wholesale loaves −£45
VAT collected on behalf of the state −£785
Net revenue £3,790

So the headline is £4,710 and what she actually earned is £3,790 — a gap of around 20%. Then the costs come out of that: ingredients, wages, rent, energy, the van. On a good week roughly £3,600 of it goes straight back out of the door, and the few hundred pounds left is profit.

Notice what happened there. Priya has four different ways of earning revenue, and they leave very different amounts behind. The £45 class earns far more per hour of her time than the wholesale loaf. The platform loaf earns less than the counter loaf for exactly the same bread. Same revenue line on paper; completely different value to the business. That's the thread the next subsection pulls on.

And notice what hasn't happened yet. The cafés get 30 days to pay, so a chunk of that £3,790 is sitting in somebody else's bank account until next month. Revenue banked, cash pending.

Why revenue still matters

After all that, is revenue just vanity? No — and it's worth being clear about why.

Revenue is the ceiling on everything else. Profit is what's left of revenue after costs. There's a hard limit to how much you can cut; there's no fixed ceiling on what you can sell. A business with strong revenue and flabby costs has options. A business with no revenue has none.

Revenue is what pays your fixed costs. Those costs from the Costs chapter — the rent, the oven lease, the salaried manager — don't care how you feel. Revenue is the only thing that covers them, and it has to cover them before a single pound becomes profit.

Revenue is evidence. It proves somebody out there genuinely wants what you do, at the price you asked. That's the Market chapter's entire apparatus, confirmed one transaction at a time.

Revenue is a sensor. A softening order book usually shows up in the top line long before it reaches your profit — which makes it one of the earliest signals you'll get that the wider Economy is turning.

So the right attitude isn't "ignore revenue, only profit matters". It's this: revenue is the input, profit is the output, and cash is whether the machine keeps running. Manage all three, and always know which one a decision is actually moving.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Always say which revenue you mean. Gross, net, recognised, or cash collected — four different numbers answering four different questions. Most muddled business conversations are two people quoting different ones.
  • Ask which lever a decision pulls. Price or volume? The Market chapter's elasticity tells you which one your customers will tolerate.
  • Judge revenue by what it leaves behind. A pound from a wholesale contract and a pound from the counter are not equivalent once costs follow them. That's the Costs chapter's contribution, applied to your revenue mix.
  • Watch the gap between revenue and cash. Growing revenue on long credit terms is how busy, profitable businesses run out of money.
  • Read revenue against the product life cycle. The Product chapter showed that profit tends to peak before sales do — so a rising revenue line isn't automatically good news.
  • The cheapest revenue is repeat revenue. Winning a new customer costs far more than keeping one, which makes the Customer Service chapter a revenue strategy in disguise.
  • Close the loop. Forecasting revenue is the planning job from the opening chapter, and it feeds nearly every other plan in the business — how much stock to hold, how many people to roster, what to order from suppliers, and how much cash will be in the bank.

Summary

  • Revenue is the total value of what a business sold in a period — its ordinary earnings, before any costs are deducted. It's the top line.
  • It is price × units sold, and the two levers behave very differently: volume drags variable costs along with it, while price doesn't.
  • Revenue is not profit, not cash, and not all yours. Sales tax passes straight through, and earnings are counted when they're earned, not when they're ordered or announced.
  • Gross revenue is bigger than net revenue. Discounts, returns and channel commissions all come off before you've earned anything.
  • Revenue is nonetheless the ceiling on profit and the only thing that pays your fixed costs — so it matters enormously, as long as you treat it as an input rather than a score.
  • The strategist's habit: know which revenue you're quoting, know which lever you're pulling, and always judge revenue by what it leaves behind.
