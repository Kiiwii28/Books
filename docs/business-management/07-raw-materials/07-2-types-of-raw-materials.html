07-2 Types of Raw Materials

Part of 07 Raw Materials

Here's the thing about raw materials: the word "raw" makes them sound uniform, like a single sack of stuff sitting at the back of the warehouse. In reality, a business's raw materials might be iron ore, live cattle, fresh basil, sand, crude oil, and recycled cardboard — all at once. Grouping them isn't academic box-ticking. Each type behaves differently, and that behaviour ripples straight through your costs, your suppliers, your factories, and your bottom line.

Think of it like sorting a crowd of people. You can sort by age, by job, by hometown, or by how early they wake up. None of those is the right answer — each one tells you something different, and which lens you reach for depends on the question you're asking. The same is true here. Let's walk through the most useful lenses.

By Origin: Where the Material Comes From

This is the most intuitive way to carve things up, and it's a good starting point.

Plant and agricultural materials. Cotton, timber, wheat, cocoa, rubber, sugar beet, palm oil. They're grown, and growth depends on weather, seasons, and soil. That means supply wobbles — a drought in one region moves prices worldwide. (Your Market chapter explained how supply and demand set price; agricultural raw materials are the clearest, most brutal example of that mechanism in action.)

Animal materials. Hides and leather, wool, dairy, tallow. Niche but important for fashion, furniture, food, and some cosmetics.

Mined and mineral materials. Iron ore, bauxite, copper, lithium, cobalt, potash, gravel. You can't grow more of these on a useful timescale, and they tend to sit in specific countries — which is exactly why so many Regulations and trade disputes revolve around them. Note the weight consideration here too: nobody ships gravel across an ocean to save a few pennies, because the Distribution cost dwarfs the material cost.

Fossil and energy-derived materials. Crude oil, natural gas, and coal aren't just fuels — they're the starting point for plastics, fertilisers, synthetic fibres, and a huge range of chemicals. Buy a plastic bottle and you've indirectly bought petrochemicals.

Water and industrial gases. Easy to forget, but breweries, soft-drink makers, and food processors treat water as a primary raw material, and they care enormously about its quality and consistency. Welders and hospitals buy oxygen and nitrogen by the tank.

By Renewability: Will It Grow Back?

A second lens, and one that now carries real commercial weight:

  • Renewable — timber, crops, wool. Replaceable within a human lifetime, if managed well.
  • Non-renewable — metals, minerals, fossil feedstocks. Use it and it's gone.
  • Secondary (recycled) — scrap steel, recovered paper, recycled plastic, reclaimed glass.

That third category is the fastest-growing one in many industries, and it's a beautiful illustration of how chapters interlock. A recycled input can cut raw material Costs, reduce exposure to commodity price spikes, and satisfy tightening environmental Regulations and packaging rules. It can also become a marketing asset that lifts Revenue — customers will pay more for a product whose story they like. Skipping this category isn't just old-fashioned; it's ignoring a lever.

By How They Enter the Product: Direct vs Indirect

Ask a simple question: does this material end up inside the thing we sell?

Direct materials do. Flour in bread, steel in a bicycle frame, cotton in a T-shirt, glass in a bottle.

Indirect materials don't, but production grinds to a halt without them — machine lubricants, cleaning chemicals, welding gases, safety gloves, replacement drill bits.

Why care? Because this split maps almost perfectly onto the direct and indirect Costs you met earlier, and it shapes how you track inventory in your Financials. Direct materials scale with output: make twice as much, buy twice as much. Indirect materials often behave more like fixed overhead — a factory uses roughly the same drum of lubricant whether it runs at 70% or 100% capacity. Get that distinction wrong in your costing and you'll misjudge which products are actually profitable.

By Stage of Processing: The Ladder from Dirt to Part

"Raw" is a matter of where you stand on the supply chain. The same substance is raw material to one business and finished input to another.


A bakery typically buys at the "commodity" or "intermediate" rung — flour and sugar are already refined. A carmaker buys at the "component" rung — stamped panels, wiring looms, tyres. A mining company sells at the very first rung.

Where you choose to buy isn't a detail. It decides which Suppliers you deal with, how much of the Manufacturing work you take on yourself, and what your cost structure looks like. Buying lower down the ladder is usually cheaper per unit but demands more capability; buying higher up costs more but is faster and simpler. That's a genuine strategic choice, and it's one Competitors are making at the same time — often in the opposite direction.

By Behaviour: How They Misbehave in Storage

Finally, sort by how the material forces you to handle it. This is where raw materials meet your Stores and Distribution chapters.

Perishable. Fresh milk, cut flowers, berries, live yeast. They have a shelf life measured in hours or days, which forces fast production, cold chain logistics, and ruthless stock rotation — first in, first out. Waste here is pure lost margin.

Bulky or heavy. Sand, cement, gravel, coal. Low value per kilogram, so transport distance is the enemy. Plants that use them tend to sit right next to the source.

Hazardous. Acids, solvents, flammable liquids. Extra storage rules, extra insurance, extra regulation, extra cost — but unavoidable in many chemical and metalworking businesses.

Standardised vs critical. A standardised commodity like copper wire trades on global exchanges; you can buy it from almost anyone and hedge the price. A critical material — a rare-earth element, a specialised alloy, a single-origin botanical — might come from two suppliers on one continent. There, your supplier relationship is your risk management.

Two Quick Worked Examples

A small bakery. Direct materials: flour, butter, sugar, eggs, yeast, salt. Indirect materials: oven cleaner, pan grease, parchment paper, gloves. Classification by origin: mostly agricultural, highly perishable, water is a key input. By stage: buying at the intermediate rung. What should the owner worry about? Flour price volatility (a Market issue), spoilage (Stores and Distribution), and butter costs, which swing with dairy markets. Cheap, fast, simple — but not risk-free.

A phone assembler. Direct materials: aluminium, glass, copper, gold traces, lithium. Indirect: solder flux, cleaning solvents, isopropyl alcohol. Classification by origin: mineral-heavy. By renewability: almost entirely non-renewable, with a growing recycled stream. By criticality: cobalt and rare earths sourced from a handful of regions, exposed to geopolitics, tariffs, and conflict-mineral Regulations. Same word — "raw materials" — but the second business lives in a totally different world of risk.

Summary

Raw materials come in types, and the type tells you where you're exposed:

  • By origin — plant, animal, mineral, fossil-derived, water and gases.
  • By renewability — renewable, non-renewable, or secondary/recycled, with growing regulatory and reputational weight.
  • By role in the product — direct materials become the product; indirect materials keep the machines running.
  • By stage of processing — the ladder from extracted commodity to finished part, and the strategic choice of where you buy.
  • By behaviour — perishable, bulky, hazardous, or critical, each demanding different storage, transport, and supplier tactics.

Here's the payoff. Every one of those labels points to a lever you can pull: price risk points to contracts and hedging; perishability points to logistics and stock rotation; criticality points to supplier relationships; renewability points to regulation, cost savings, and brand. You don't need to memorise the categories — you need to look at your own material list and ask, honestly, which types am I actually dependent on? That question is where good business strategy starts, and it's the thread we'll pull on next, when we look at raw materials as a factor across the whole business.
