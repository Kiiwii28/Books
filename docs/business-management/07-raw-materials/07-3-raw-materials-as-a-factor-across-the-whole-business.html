07-3 Raw Materials as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 07 Raw Materials

Raw materials sit at the far end of the business — furthest from the customer, least visible on the shop floor, and easiest to treat as somebody else's problem. That's exactly why they deserve their own lens. You can run a beautiful operation and still watch your profit evaporate because the price of flour moved, because a harvest failed, or because a batch arrived a shade too damp. Nobody outside your business ever sees your raw materials. Everybody inside it feels them.

This subsection zooms out. Rather than looking at raw materials as a purchasing task, we'll follow the threads that run from them into every other part of the business — cost, product, operations, risk, people, rules — and see how each thread changes what a good strategist should do.

The ceiling on everything downstream

Start with the blunt truth: you cannot make a better product than your inputs allow. No amount of skill in the bakery makes a poor flour into good bread. No amount of polish on the shop floor makes up for a component that fails after six months.

This puts raw materials directly underneath the Product chapter. Remember the three levels of a product — core benefit, actual product, augmented product? Raw materials decide the quality and consistency of the actual product, and that in turn decides whether the promises wrapped around it hold up. A brand is a promise; the product is the keeping of it. A raw material that varies batch to batch quietly breaks that promise, and the customer experiences it as a complaint — which lands in Customer Service, not in the purchasing ledger where it started.

Two consequences follow:

  • Consistency beats brilliance. A predictable material lets you promise a predictable product. That's why the boring supplier with reliable spec control often beats the exciting one with better samples.
  • New materials create new products. Tastes and markets don't only pull products into existence; materials push them too. Cheaper solar cells, lighter alloys, better batteries — each one opened a market that couldn't have existed before. Watching materials is one way to spot a product opportunity before your rivals do.

Where the money actually is

For most product businesses, raw materials are the largest single line inside cost of goods sold. That's the costs that rise with every unit you sell, as opposed to the fixed ones — rent, salaried staff, insurance — that you pay whether you sell anything or not.

Costs come off profit pound for pound, which means a small change in material cost has an outsized effect at the bottom of the page. Watch what happens to a bakery with £1,000 of daily sales:

Per day Before After a 20% rise in flour cost
Sales £1,000 £1,000
Raw materials and ingredients £300 £360*
Everything else £650 £650
Profit £50 £-10

*Assuming flour is roughly a fifth of the ingredient bill.

A 6% rise in total costs wiped out the entire profit. That's the leverage: material costs are paid by the business, but only the profit line suffers. This is why purchasing is a profit centre, not an admin chore — and why the Costs and Financials chapters keep circling back to it.

There's a cash dimension too. Materials you've bought but not yet sold are money sitting in a warehouse doing nothing — stock, which is cash you can't spend on anything else. Raw materials are unusually demanding here: they tie up cash, they may need paying for before you've sold the finished goods, and if you don't use them, they rot, rust or go out of fashion. A material price rise you can usually survive. A material price rise during a cash squeeze is how businesses die.

Materials shape how you make, move and store

Manufacturing. The material dictates the process. Flour needs ovens and proving time; steel needs furnaces and presses; chemicals need tanks and precise temperatures. The whole vocabulary of the Manufacturing chapter — job, batch, mass, continuous production — grows out of what you're working with. Change your raw material and you may have to rebuild your factory.

Stores. Materials carry their own storage rules. Grain keeps for months; milk keeps for days. Bulk materials need space, dangerous ones need containment, valuable ones need security. Every gram that spoils or walks out the door is a material cost with no revenue attached — pure loss, invisible in the accounts unless somebody is counting.

Distribution. Weight and volume are paid for twice. A cheap heavy material can cost more to move than to buy, which is why businesses that make weight-losing products historically located near their source — mills by rivers, breweries by water. Add long lead times from overseas and your Distribution planning gets longer, and the stock you must hold gets bigger.

Labour. Materials decide who you need, what you must train them to do, and how safe the work is. Hazardous inputs mean protective equipment, procedures, insurance and Regulations compliance. Fine tolerances mean skilled hands. This is the Labour thread running straight out of the material you chose.

The shocks come from outside your four walls

Raw materials are where the wider world reaches directly into your cash flow. Almost every big swing has an external cause:

  • Harvests, weather and geology. A bad season lifts prices for everyone, including your rivals — but not equally, because the well-supplied firm has stock and contracts while the other one is buying at the spot price.
  • Commodity markets. Materials traded in bulk get a going price set by supply and demand, exactly as the Market chapter described. You're a price taker.
  • Currency. Buy in a foreign currency and an exchange rate move can change your material cost without a single supplier lifting a finger.
  • Tariffs, duties and taxes. Goods crossing borders pick up charges, dealt with in the Tax chapter.
  • Regulation. Restrictions on hazardous substances, sourcing rules, sustainability requirements and labelling all sit on materials rather than on finished products. If regulators ban an input, your production line stops until you reformulate.
  • Interruption. Strikes, port closures, a fire at a supplier you've never met. This is the Suppliers chapter's tier problem: your Tier 2's disaster is delivered to you as your Tier 1's apology.

And the quiet one: a rival who locks up supply. Control a scarce input — the best site, the only licensed processor, an exclusive contract — and you've built a barrier your competitors can't cross. In the language of the Competitors chapter, that's a structural advantage, and a durable one.

Worked example: the flour price comes to Priya's counter

Priya's bakery, from earlier in the book, sells 100 loaves a day at £4. Ingredients cost £1.20 a loaf; everything else — wages, rent, energy — comes to £230 a day.

Per day Normal Bad harvest (flour +20%)
Revenue (100 × £4) £400 £400
Ingredients £120 £132
Wages, rent, energy £230 £230
Profit £50 £38

A 3% rise in costs cut her profit by a quarter. Now watch her think. Raising the price to £4.10 would recover £10 a day if the same 100 customers stayed — but as the Market chapter showed, her bread has close substitutes and her demand is elastic, so some of them walk to the café next door. So instead of one blunt move, a strategist plays several small ones:

  • Cut waste. She counts what she throws away, finds 4% of her flour going in the bin, and gets it under 1%. Worth a few pounds a day, permanently.
  • Reformulate. One of her six lines moves to a cheaper blend of the same quality grade. The customers don't notice; the margin does.
  • Negotiate. She asks the mill for a small discount on a committed weekly volume — and gets it, because she's now a predictable customer.
  • Adjust selectively. She puts 5p on the sourdough, where she has the most loyal buyers, and leaves the everyday white loaf alone.

No single lever saves the day. Together they more than cover the £12. That's what raw materials as a factor across the business looks like in practice: not one heroic decision, but four ordinary ones in different corners of the company.

The five questions a strategist asks

You can apply this to any material, in any business, in about ten minutes:

  1. How much of my cost is it? A material that's 2% of your costs can be ignored; one that's 30% is a board-level topic. Know which is which.
  2. Could I substitute it? Flexibility — a second material, a second grade, a second specification — is insurance you buy before you need it.
  3. How easily could it be cut off, and by whom? One supplier, one country, one mine? Then you're managing risk, not price.
  4. How much do I lose? Waste, spoilage, shrinkage, over-ordering, expired stock. This is the cheapest saving available, because it needs no negotiation and no customer.
  5. Could it be an advantage rather than a cost? Better material, or exclusive access to it, can be the thing customers pay a premium for. That's a Product decision financed by a purchasing one.

Summary

  • Raw materials set the quality ceiling on your product and the consistency your brand depends on — no amount of downstream skill fixes bad inputs.
  • They are usually the biggest part of cost of goods sold, and because costs come off profit pound for pound, a small material price change swings profit hard.
  • They tie up cash in stock, which links them directly to the Financials chapter and to survival during a squeeze.
  • Materials shape your manufacturing process, storage needs, transport costs and labour requirements — they're an operations decision disguised as a purchase.
  • The biggest shocks are external: weather, commodity markets, currency, tariffs, regulation and interrupted supply chains. Most of them are predictable, which means most of them are planable.
  • The strategist's toolkit is a set of small moves — reduce waste, substitute, negotiate, design around the material, adjust prices selectively, hold buffer stock — rather than one heroic fix.
  • Finally, ask whether a material is a cost to be minimised or a capability to protect. Sometimes control of supply is the advantage your rivals can't copy.
