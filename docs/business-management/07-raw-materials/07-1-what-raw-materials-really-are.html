07-1 What Raw Materials Really Are

Part of 07 Raw Materials

You already use the phrase without thinking about it. "We need to order raw materials." "The raw materials got more expensive." It sounds obvious — the stuff you make things out of. But the word raw is doing something sneaky, and pulling it apart is worth ten minutes, because raw materials sit at the very bottom of your cost sheet, at the very start of your supply chain, and usually at the root of whatever is quietly eating your margin.

The definition worth keeping

A raw material is a basic physical input that a business buys and then transforms into the thing it sells.

Three words in that sentence are doing the work:

  • Buys — it came from outside. You didn't make it; somebody else did, and you paid for it. That makes it a Suppliers relationship, not an internal one.
  • Physical — you can weigh it, spill it, store it, and lose it. This is what separates materials from services, information, or labour.
  • Transforms — it doesn't sit next to your product. It becomes part of it. Flour doesn't accompany a loaf; flour is the loaf, rearranged.

That third word is the one people get wrong, and it's the one that decides everything else.

"Raw" is a job title, not a substance

Here's the idea that unlocks the whole chapter: nothing is a raw material on its own. It's a raw material to somebody.

The same bag of flour is a finished product to the mill that bagged it and a raw material to the bakery that bakes with it. Same bag. Same flour. Two completely different roles, depending on where you're standing.

The material A raw material to… A finished product for…
Flour A bakery A flour mill
Steel sheet A car plant A steel mill
Cocoa beans A chocolate maker A farmer or exporter
Cotton yarn A T-shirt maker A spinning mill
Silicon wafers A chipmaker A wafer fabrication plant

Two useful consequences follow. First, when someone says "we're a raw materials business," they mean they sell to other businesses that will transform what they produce — they're one step up from the ground. Second, and more practically, the same firm can be both things at once. A steel mill buys iron ore (raw material) and sells sheet steel (finished product), and it will describe itself either way depending on who it's talking to.

So always ask the locating question: whose product is this an input to?

Sorting the pile: materials, parts, supplies, stock

Once you accept that "raw material" is a role rather than a category, the pile of things you buy sorts itself into distinct types. Each one behaves differently on your accounts and in your warehouse.

Type What happens to it In a bakery In a bike workshop Where it lands
Raw materials Transformed into the product Flour, butter, yeast Steel tube, paint Direct cost of goods sold
Components Assembled onto the product Bought-in jam, paper cases Wheels, gears, saddles Direct cost of goods sold
Consumables / supplies Used up keeping the place running Oven gas, cleaning fluid, till rolls Machine oil, gloves Overhead (indirect cost)
Goods for resale Sold on unchanged Bottled drinks from a wholesaler Bought-in helmets Cost of goods sold (resale)
Capital goods Used for years, not consumed The oven The welding rig Fixed asset, depreciated

The distinction matters because it decides where a cost shows up, and therefore what you can do about it. Raw materials and components move with your output — make twice as many loaves, buy twice as much flour. That's a variable, direct cost, and it's covered properly in the Costs chapter. The gas bill and the machine oil barely twitch when output doubles; they're overhead, and they behave nothing like materials.

Where raw materials come from — and why that matters

Run your finger backwards along the chain and raw materials are always the furthest thing from you. You buy from a Tier 1 supplier. That supplier bought from a Tier 2. Somewhere at the far end, a farmer, a mine or a well produced the actual substance — and you will probably never meet them, never negotiate with them, and never see their name on a document.


Photo by Pixabay on Pexels

That distance has three real consequences.

You don't control the price. Steel, wheat, oil, cotton, cocoa and copper are traded on world markets. Their prices move with weather, harvests, energy costs, shipping rates and geopolitics — none of which you vote on. This is the Market chapter's supply curve, applied to your own shopping list: a bad harvest isn't a small event, it's a supply-side shift that lands on your cost base whether you like it or not. The Economy chapter is where these ripples get explained properly.

You don't fully control the quality, either. Raw materials arrive with natural variation. Wheat from one harvest behaves differently from the next; timber has knots; cocoa beans vary by origin. This sets a quality ceiling on your product, which is why the Product chapter and this one shake hands constantly. You can add skill, technology and care — but you cannot make a great loaf out of poor flour.

You carry the risk of them running out. A shortage upstream stops production downstream, and the further away the cause, the later you find out.

Worked example: a chocolate bar and a 20% price rise

Aurelia makes chocolate bars and sells them for £2.50 each. Her raw materials are cocoa beans, sugar, milk powder, cocoa butter and vanilla. Notice that the sugar is a raw material to her and a finished product to the refinery down the road. Notice too that her cocoa arrives via a trader, who bought it from an exporter, who bought it from farmers she'll never visit.

Say her figures look like this:

Per bar Normal After a 20% rise in cocoa prices
Selling price £2.50 £2.50
Cocoa £0.35 £0.42
Other materials £0.75 £0.75
Wages, rent, packaging, delivery £1.28 £1.28
Profit £0.12 £0.05

A cocoa price that moves 20% — entirely normal in that market — has just taken more than half her profit. She did nothing wrong. She changed no recipe, lost no customer, hired nobody. A weather event four thousand miles away reached into her till.

This is why raw materials matter so much more than their share of the invoice suggests. Every penny saved or lost on materials goes straight to profit. Shave 5p off the cost of a bar and her profit goes up by nearly half; win 5p more on price and she has to fight a competitor for it. Cost reduction on materials is the cheapest profit in business.

She has three honest responses, and they map neatly onto decisions from other chapters:

  1. Charge more. Possible — but only if her customers aren't easily tempted by the bar next to hers on the shelf. That's the elasticity question from the Market chapter, and it's decided by how differentiated her bar really is.
  2. Reformulate. Swap some cocoa butter for a cheaper alternative, or change the recipe. That's a Product decision with a quality consequence — and a Customer Service consequence if anyone notices.
  3. Absorb it. Accept the thinner margin, or hedge by buying cocoa forward at a fixed price. Either way, the Financials chapter is where the damage eventually shows.

A good strategist usually does a bit of all three — and, crucially, decides before the invoice arrives rather than after.

The management angle: materials are a loop, not a shopping list

Go back to the four jobs from the opening of this book and see how neatly they fit around a warehouse full of flour.

  • Planning means forecasting what you'll need — your sales forecast, plus expected waste, plus a buffer for the weeks when supply is unreliable.
  • Organizing means setting up the sourcing, the contracts, the delivery schedule and the storage.
  • Leading shows up in the buyer–supplier relationship; suppliers bend over backwards for people they like and trust, as the Suppliers chapter argues.
  • Controlling is the one businesses skip, and it's the one that finds money. Measure material usage per unit of output — kilos of flour per hundred loaves, grams of cocoa per bar. When that number creeps up, someone is wasting, spilling, over-portioning or stealing, and you'll know within a week instead of at year-end.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Know your material base. Usually two or three inputs carry most of the cost. Those are the ones worth your attention; the rest are noise.
  • Watch the market, not just the invoice. Your supplier's price is a result. The cause lives in a commodity market, a harvest, a shipping route or a currency — and it moves before the invoice does.
  • Remember the quality ceiling. Materials set the upper bound on what you can sell; process and skill only decide how close to the ceiling you get.
  • Treat materials as cash in a different form. Stock sitting in a warehouse is money you've spent and can't use. That's a Financials and Stores problem, and it deserves the same attention as the price you paid.
  • Look for substitutes before you're forced to. The best time to find an alternative material is while the current one is still cheap.

Summary

  • A raw material is a basic physical input a business buys and transforms into what it sells — bought in, physical, and turned into the product rather than merely used alongside it.
  • It is a role, not a substance. The same item is a raw material to one firm and a finished product to another, so always ask whose input it is.
  • Materials sort into raw materials, components, consumables, goods for resale and capital goods — and each lands in a different place on the cost sheet.
  • Raw materials come from the far end of the supply chain, which means you rarely control their price, their quality or their availability.
  • They are direct, variable costs, so savings and losses hit profit one-for-one — which is why materials are usually the most rewarding place to look for margin.
  • Managing them is the management loop applied to the warehouse: plan the forecast, organise the sourcing, control the usage per unit.

Next, we'll sort raw materials into their main types — and see why a cocoa bean, a barrel of crude oil and a sheet of steel behave nothing alike.
