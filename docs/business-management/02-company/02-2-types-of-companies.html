02-2 Types of Companies

Part of 02 Company

A bakery, a bank and a Premier League football club are all "companies" — yet one might be a single person with a rented kitchen, and another might have shareholders in forty countries. The word covers a huge range of legal animals, and which animal you pick decides who owns the business, who pays its debts, how it gets money, and how much paperwork lands on your desk.

This subsection walks through the main ways companies come in types: by legal structure (the big one), by size, by ownership, and by purpose.

The one idea that does most of the work: limited liability

Before the categories, meet the concept that sorts them. When a business is incorporated, the law treats it as a separate legal person. The company can own property, sign contracts, sue and be sued — in its own name, distinct from the humans behind it.

That has a huge consequence. If an incorporated company owes money and can't pay, the creditors generally come after the company's assets, not the owner's house or savings. This is limited liability: the owners' risk is capped at what they put in.

A business that isn't incorporated — a sole trader, a basic partnership — has no such wall. The owner is the business, so the business's debts are the owner's debts. Lose a court case, and you can lose your home.

Everything else in this subsection is, in one way or another, a variation on that single trade-off.

Sole traders and partnerships: simple, cheap, exposed

A sole trader is one person running a business. Set-up is minimal, accounts stay private, and all profit is yours. The catch is the one from above: unlimited liability, and the business dies with you or your enthusiasm.

A partnership is two or more people sharing ownership. It brings in extra money, skills and hours — and, in a general partnership, extra exposure. Partners are typically liable for the firm's debts jointly, meaning a bank can pursue any one of them for the lot. Fall out with a partner who runs up debts, and you may still be on the hook.

Two refinements worth knowing: a limited partnership has silent investors whose liability is capped at what they invested (but who can't manage the firm), and an LLP gives all the partners limited liability while keeping the flexible, partner-run feel. LLPs became the standard shape for professional firms for exactly that reason.

Limited companies: private and public

A private limited company — an Ltd in the UK, an LLC in the US, a GmbH in Germany — is the workhorse of the business world. It's a separate legal person, owners have limited liability, and shares are held privately. You can sell shares to a handful of investors, but you can't offer them to the general public.

A public limited company (a PLC, or a corporation like Inc.) can. It can list its shares on a stock exchange, where anyone can buy a slice. That's how a company raises serious money: instead of one bank loan, it sells ownership in thousands of small pieces.

The price is transparency and control. Public companies must publish detailed accounts, report to regulators, answer to shareholders, and can in principle be bought out by whoever accumulates enough shares. Owners of a private company get to keep their numbers to themselves; owners of a public one live in a fishbowl.

One more distinction you'll meet in the US: a C-corporation pays tax on its profits and then shareholders pay tax again on dividends (sometimes called double taxation), while an S-corporation passes profits straight through to owners' personal tax returns, but is capped in size and restricted to certain shareholders. Same idea, different tax plumbing.

Cooperatives, non-profits and state-owned firms

Not every company exists to enrich its owners.

A cooperative is owned and governed by the people who use it — the farmers who supply it, the customers who shop in it, or the workers who run it. The classic rule is one member, one vote, regardless of how much each has invested, and profits are shared according to how much you traded with the co-op rather than how many shares you hold.

A non-profit has no owners at all. Any surplus is ploughed back into the mission rather than distributed. Charities, foundations, most hospitals and many sports clubs work this way, usually with tax advantages and strict rules on what the money can be used for.

A state-owned enterprise is owned by government — often utilities, railways, or national oil companies. It may be run for profit, for public service, or a muddle of both.

Sorting companies by size

Size bands are mostly matters of definition, but they matter because governments write rules and support programmes around them. The European Union's version is a common one:

  • Micro — under 10 employees, up to about €2 million turnover
  • Small — under 50 employees, up to about €10 million
  • Medium — under 250 employees, up to about €50 million
  • Large — anything above that

Thresholds differ by country and industry (the US varies them sector by sector), so treat the numbers as illustrative. The pattern underneath is real, though: as a firm grows through these bands, the rules tighten — more reporting, more employment law, more tax complexity. Growth buys capability and costs simplicity.

Sorting companies by ownership

You can also slice companies by who holds them:

  • Privately held — shares held by founders, families or a small group of investors.
  • Publicly traded — shares listed on an exchange.
  • Family-owned — controlled by one family, which is the most common ownership form worldwide, from corner shops to giant conglomerates.
  • Employee-owned — owned by the staff, through a trust or an employee share scheme.
  • State-owned — owned by government.
  • Joint venture — a separate company created by two or more parent companies to do a specific job together.

Note that ownership and legal structure are independent questions. A family can own a multinational PLC; a cooperative can be a limited company. The two axes just answer different questions: "what legal animal is this?" and "whose is it?"

A worked example: Priya's bakery grows up

Priya starts as a sole trader, baking and selling at a weekend market. Cheap, simple, all hers. Then her oven needs replacing, and the bank asks a question she hadn't thought about: if the loan goes bad, what's the security? On a sole trader's loan, the answer is usually her own house.

Two years later her friend Dev joins. They form a private limited company. From then on, the company owns the oven, signs the lease, and carries the debt. If the bakery fails, Priya and Dev lose what they invested — not their homes. In exchange, they must file annual accounts and keep proper records of who owns what.

Five years on, they want three more sites and £400,000. Selling more shares privately gets them partway. Beyond that, they'd need to become a public limited company and float on a stock exchange — raising serious money at the cost of opening their books, and their control, to strangers.

Notice the pattern: each step up the ladder buys capability and costs simplicity.

Two honest caveats

Limited liability isn't a force field. Banks know the wall exists, so they often demand a personal guarantee from directors of small companies — which puts the owner's house back on the table by contract. And if a business trades recklessly or fraudulently, courts can sometimes reach through the company to the people behind it.

The fanciest structure isn't the best one. A one-person business doesn't need a board, and many large firms stay privately held for decades. The right type is the one that fits how much risk you can carry, how much money you need, and how much admin you're willing to live with.

Summary

  • The key dividing line is incorporation: an incorporated company is a separate legal person, so owners get limited liability and risk only what they invested. Sole traders and general partners do not.
  • Main legal structures: sole trader, partnership, LLP, private limited company, public limited company, cooperative, non-profit, state-owned enterprise.
  • Private companies can sell shares to a few investors and keep their accounts private; public companies can sell to anyone and must open their books.
  • Companies are also sorted by size (micro to large), which determines how much regulation applies, and by ownership (private, public, family, employee, state, joint venture).
  • Purpose is a fourth axis: for-profit, mission-driven, or public service.
  • Moving up the ladder — sole trader to PLC — buys you capital and protection, and costs you simplicity and control.
  • Limited liability is not absolute: personal guarantees and reckless trading can still reach the owners.
