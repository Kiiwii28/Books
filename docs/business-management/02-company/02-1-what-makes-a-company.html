02-1 What Makes a Company

Part of 02 Company

A word you already use loosely

"We're a company of about twelve." "I work for a big company." "The gas company." In everyday speech, company is just a fancier word for "business". In business and law, it means something much sharper — and it's worth getting straight now, because when later sections say a company has shareholders, a board, its own tax bill or its own debts, all of that flows from the one definition in this subsection.

Here it is: a company is a business that has been given its own legal existence, separate from the people who own it.

That's it. Everything else — shares, limited liability, directors, "Ltd", endless paperwork — is a consequence of that single idea.

The one big idea: the company is its own "person"

Most businesses start as an extension of a person. A plumber trades under her own name; if she owes money, it's her money. Her van is hers. If someone sues the business, they're suing her.

A company breaks that link. Once it's registered — incorporated — the law treats the company as a distinct entity that can do most of the things a person can:

  • It owns things in its own name. The ovens, the van, the cash in the bank account. Not the owners' ovens — the company's.
  • It owes things in its own name. Debts, rent, tax, wages. If the company can't pay, that's the company's problem, not automatically yours.
  • It can sign contracts, sue, and be sued. Your name isn't on the contract; the company's is.
  • It outlives its owners. Shareholders can die, retire, divorce, or sell up, and the company carries on regardless. Lawyers call this perpetual succession, which just means "nobody's funeral closes the business".
  • Its ownership can be sliced. Because ownership is divided into shares, you can sell 20% of a company without selling the building or the brand. That's the trick that lets companies raise money from strangers.


The headline benefit is limited liability. If the company collapses, shareholders normally lose only what they put in — their shares go to zero, but their house stays theirs. That's a huge deal: it's what lets cautious people invest in risky ventures.

Be honest about the limits, though. Limited liability isn't a magic shield. Directors can be personally on the hook for fraud, for trading while knowingly insolvent, or for unpaid taxes. Banks lending to small companies usually demand a personal guarantee from the owner, which quietly puts the house back on the table. And plenty of people run companies their whole lives without ever testing the shield. It's a safety net, not a suit of armour.

The skeleton and the body

A company needs two quite different sets of ingredients, and beginners often notice only one of them.

The legal skeleton is what makes it a company rather than a person trading under a name:

Ingredient What it means in practice
Registration A filing with the state, a name, a registration number
Owners One or more shareholders holding shares
Capital Money or assets the owners put in
Rules A constitution or articles saying how decisions get made
Directors People appointed to act for the company
An official address Where legal letters go

The commercial body is what makes it a business rather than a shell:

  • something to sell,
  • customers who actually pay for it,
  • people and equipment to deliver it,
  • money coming in that exceeds money going out.

You can build the skeleton with nothing inside it — that's a shell company, entirely legal and sometimes useful, but not a business. You can also have a thriving business with no skeleton at all, which is what a sole trader has. A company that lasts has both.

Worked example: Nadia incorporates

Nadia has run a bakery for six years as a sole trader. Her name is on the lease, her name is on the loan, and when a customer slipped on a wet floor last winter, the claim landed on her personally.

Now her sister wants to invest, and they want a second site. So they incorporate: Nadia's Bakery Ltd. They issue 1,000 shares — Nadia takes 700, her sister 300 — and the company buys the ovens from Nadia, signs a new lease, and employs the staff.

What changed?

  • The lease and the ovens now belong to the company. If someone sues over that wet floor tomorrow, the claim is against the company's assets, not Nadia's savings.
  • Nadia's sister now owns 30% of a thing, not 30% of a promise. She can sell those shares to someone else if she wants out.
  • The company files its own tax return and pays its own tax.
  • If Nadia dies, the bakery keeps trading. Her shares pass to her estate; the business doesn't skip a beat.
  • Books, filings and annual returns now have to be done properly, every year, whether Nadia feels like it or not.

What didn't change?

The bread still has to be good. The staff still need rostering. Customers still have to walk through the door. Incorporating changes who owns the business and who carries the risk — it doesn't sell a single loaf.

Three things people mix up

A company isn't the same as a business. Business is the broad word for organised effort to create and sell value. Company is one particular legal form that a business can take. Every company is a business; plenty of businesses aren't companies. (The next section unpacks the options.)

Owners aren't managers. Shareholders own the company; directors run it; managers and staff do the work. In a tiny company those are often the same three people in one body, which is exactly why the distinction feels academic — until the company grows and it suddenly isn't.

A company isn't its brand, its building, or its website. Companies rename themselves, move offices, and sell premises all the time and remain the same legal entity. The thing that persists is the entity, not the shopfront.

The test

If you want to know whether something is a company, ask one question: is there an entity here that owns things, owes things, and can keep going, independently of the people who own it?

Yes — company. No — you're looking at a sole trader or a partnership, which we get to next.

Summary

  • A company is a business with its own legal existence, separate from the people who own it.
  • That single idea produces the features: it owns assets, owes debts, signs contracts, employs people, and outlives its owners.
  • Ownership is split into shares, which is what allows ownership to be sold in slices and money to be raised from outsiders.
  • Limited liability means shareholders normally risk only what they invested — with real exceptions like fraud, insolvency, and personal guarantees.
  • A company needs a legal skeleton (registration, owners, capital, rules, directors) and a commercial body (something to sell, customers, people, cash flow). One without the other isn't much.
  • Owners, directors, and staff are three different roles, even when one person wears all three hats.
  • The test: is it a separate entity that can own, owe, and endure on its own? If yes, it's a company.
