04-4 Market vs. The Other Factors

Part of 04 Market

Every factor in this book is, at heart, a lever. Product, price, staff, stores, suppliers, manufacturing — you decide, you spend, you adjust. The market is different. The market is the verdict. It is the only factor in the whole list that tells you whether pulling all those other levers actually worked, and it is the only one that can change its mind without asking you first.

Before we start comparing, one quick recall from earlier in this chapter: a market is not "the shoppers" and it is not "the country." It's a space where buyers and sellers meet and where supply and demand grind out a price. Hold on to those three pieces — buyers, sellers, and the space between them — because almost every comparison below lands on one of them.

Market vs. the factors inside the company

Company and Management

A company is a machine assembled to serve a market. That sounds obvious until you ask the founding question every strategist eventually asks: what business are we actually in? That question has no answer inside the company. It only has an answer in the market. A firm that thinks it's in the "typewriter business" dies; one that realises it's in the "office document business" survives. The market redefines the company, not the reverse.

The relationship runs both ways, though — and this is where strategy gets interesting. A big enough company can shape the market it sells into. Enough marketing, enough distribution reach, enough price power, and you stop responding to demand and start creating it. That's the difference between being a market-taker (you accept the going price) and a market-maker (you help set it). Almost every business starts as a taker. The good ones earn a little market-making power over time — through brand, scale, patents, or sheer ubiquity.

Management is the translator in the middle. The market speaks in broad, messy signals — a shift in taste, a new rival, a younger demographic — and management's job is to turn those signals into decisions about the other eighteen factors. Ask yourself what "good management" even means without a market to define it. Efficient at what? Fast at what? The market supplies the target that management aims at. A brilliant management team aimed at the wrong market loses to a mediocre team aimed at the right one.

Staff and Labour

Notice the phrase labour market. It isn't a coincidence. Labour is bought and sold, wages are a price, and that price is set by supply and demand exactly like the price of a coffee. So the market — your market — reaches inside your building and sets what you must pay your people.

If your market demands baristas, you're hiring from a shallow, local pool and paying what that pool costs. If it demands machine-learning engineers, you're hiring from a global pool and paying a very different number. The market you serve decides which skills you need; the labour market decides what those skills cost. Those two forces squeeze your margin from opposite sides.

Staff are also the market's eyes and ears. Your frontline people hear what buyers complain about, what they ask for, what makes them walk out. A business that treats staff as an internal cost centre throws away its cheapest market research.

Financials, Revenue and Costs

Here is the sharpest comparison in the book. Financials are the market's report card, written in numbers. Revenue is simply the market's verdict converted into money: demand at a price. Costs are what you spent trying to earn that verdict.

The trap is getting the direction of causation backwards. A lot of new business owners build a product, add up what it cost, tack on a margin, and call that the price. The market doesn't care what it cost you. Price is set by supply and demand — by what buyers will pay and what rivals are charging. Your cost structure isn't the source of your price; it's a constraint you must fit underneath it. The market sets the ceiling, and everything in the Costs chapter is about living below it.

This is why the same product can be a great business in one market and a terrible one in another. Not because the costs changed, but because the market's willingness to pay did.

Market vs. the factors that build and move things

Suppliers, Raw Materials and Manufacturing

Suppliers, raw materials and manufacturing are all upstream. They exist because a market somewhere downstream wants something.

  • Suppliers. Your demand for a supplier is derived demand — you only need their cardboard because buyers want your boxes. If the market shrinks, your supplier orders shrink with it, and suddenly your bargaining power with that supplier changes. The supplier chapter's advice about choosing and managing suppliers is really advice about managing market risk upstream.
  • Raw materials. Which materials you use is a market question dressed up as an engineering one. Cotton or polyester? Glass or plastic? The answer depends on what buyers will pay for, what they'll accept, and what regulators will allow — all market-side facts. And when a raw material gets scarce, its price rises, and that rise has to come out of your margin unless the market lets you raise your own price. Usually it won't.
  • Manufacturing. Scale is set by market volume. A factory tooled for 100,000 units is a liability if the market only wants 5,000. On the flip side, manufacturing efficiency can lower your price, and a lower price can expand the market by pulling in buyers who previously couldn't afford you. Manufacturing can therefore create market, not just serve it — one of the few factors with that power.

Distribution and Stores

Where your buyers are determines where your stuff has to go. If the market is spread across a continent, you need warehouses and carriers; if it's a two-mile radius, you might need one van. Distribution is geography plus habit, and both of those are market facts.

Stores are the most visible expression of this. A shop is a bet on a market: it says "enough buyers who want this, at this price, will pass this spot." Wrong about the market, and no amount of beautiful shopfitting saves you. Right about it, and an ugly shop on a busy corner beats a gorgeous one on a quiet street. The location decision is 80% market analysis and 20% rent.

Market vs. the factors that face the buyer

Product

The market defines what a product is — not its ingredients, but its meaning. A watch tells the time; a watch in a certain market means status, or durability, or nostalgia. The same object serves different markets, and the market decides which features are worth paying for and which are worthless gold-plating.

Two directions of influence matter here. Normally the market tells you what to build. But occasionally a product creates its own market — nobody was asking for smartphones, streaming, or ride-hailing until they existed. Even then, the market gets the final say: plenty of products that invented a category also failed because the market arrived too early, too late, or never.

Competitors

Competitors are a market phenomenon. No market, no competitors. And the structure of your market — how many buyers, how many sellers, how easy to enter — determines what kind of competition you face. A market with thousands of near-identical sellers and free entry produces brutal price competition and razor-thin margins. A market with three big players produces something else entirely: advertising wars, tacit pricing, and comfortable profits.

So when you size up your rivals, you're really reading the market's structure. Market share is a market measurement. Every move a competitor makes changes the space between buyers and sellers that you all live in.

Sales and Customer Service

Sales is the moment of contact between the market and your business — the handshake where demand turns into revenue. It follows the market rather than leading it: you can't persuade a market that has no need, and you can't sell at a price the market won't pay, no matter how good your closing technique.

Customer service works on the market's memory. Service shapes what buyers believe about you, and that belief becomes part of the market's perception of your whole category. Great service turns a one-time buyer into repeat demand and turns buyers into salespeople. Bad service doesn't just lose a customer — in a small market, it shrinks your market.

Market vs. the forces nobody negotiates with

Economy

The economy is the market's weather. It sets the total level of demand, the cost of borrowing, and how confident people feel about spending. A recession doesn't change your product or your staff, but it can halve your market overnight. Conversely, a boom can make a mediocre business look brilliant for a few years.

The strategist's habit here is to separate the two: how much of my sales is my skill, and how much is just the tide? Businesses that mistake a rising economy for their own genius tend to be the ones that drown when it turns.

Tax and Regulations

These two are market-shapers with a legal pen. A tax on a good raises its effective price, which reduces demand — the market shrinks without a single buyer changing their mind. A subsidy does the reverse and can create a market out of nothing (think electric vehicles in their early years).

Regulations decide who may sell what, to whom, at what standard, and where. Licensing requirements fence a market off from newcomers. Safety and quality rules can protect existing players or wipe them out. Whole markets have been created and destroyed by a single rule change. This is why the Regulations and Tax chapters sit so close to the Market chapter: they don't just constrain your business, they redraw the arena.

And one more comparison worth making: Market vs. market. Your market sits next to other markets, and buyers drift between them. A customer who buys your sandwich today might buy a salad tomorrow. Competitors, substitutes and changing tastes all move the boundaries. The market you're in this year is not guaranteed to be the market you're in next year.

Worked example: Priya's lunch boxes

Priya makes plant-based ready meals and sells them to office workers in a city centre. Watch how the market drives everything else.

  • Market first. She notices thousands of office workers with 30 minutes for lunch and a growing interest in eating less meat. Buyers exist, sellers are scarce, and the space between them is a short walk from the office.
  • Product follows. She builds meals that reheat in 90 seconds, because the market's constraint isn't taste, it's time.
  • Manufacturing follows. Volume is predictable — weekday lunch, roughly 400 units — so she tooled for that, not for 40,000.
  • Distribution and stores follow. One small unit near the offices beats a bigger, cheaper one two miles away. The market is the location.
  • Suppliers and raw materials follow. She picks ingredients the market recognises and will pay for, from suppliers who can deliver daily.
  • Labour and staff follow. She hires two part-time cooks from the local labour market and pays what that market charges.
  • Costs follow from price. The market will pay about £7. Her cost ceiling is therefore £5-ish, and every cost decision is a negotiation with that ceiling. She did not add up her costs and then declare a price.
  • Sales and service follow. A loyalty card and a friendly face at the counter turn one-off buyers into regulars.
  • Financials and revenue follow. These tell her, monthly, whether her read on the market was right.

Now move the market. The economy dips and the offices go hybrid — two days a week, not five. Nothing about her product, staff, suppliers or factory changed, yet her market has shrunk by 60%. That is the whole lesson of this subsection in one paragraph: the market can rewrite your business without touching a single thing inside it.

Her strategic options are all market options — find another market (residential neighbourhoods, hospitals), change the product to fit a new market (family-sized meals), change the channel (delivery apps), or exit. Notice she doesn't fix this by being better at manufacturing.

Three traps that come from confusing the market with everything else

  1. Build it and they will come. Building the product before confirming the market. Every other factor is easy to obsess over because it's visible and controllable. The market is invisible and stubborn, which is exactly why it deserves the first look, not the last.
  2. Cost-plus pricing. Deriving price from your costs rather than from the market. This works only until a competitor prices below you and the market proves your cost structure was your problem, not its.
  3. "Our market is everyone." A market is buyers with a need, money, and access. Everyone is not a market; it's a wish. Narrow markets are where small businesses win, because narrow markets can be understood and served better than anyone else is willing to.

Summary

  • The market is the only factor in this book that you mostly can't control, and the only one that gives a verdict rather than taking an instruction.
  • Every other factor is downstream of it: product, manufacturing, distribution, stores, sales and service all exist to satisfy demand the market supplies.
  • Financials, revenue and costs are the market's report card — the market sets the price ceiling, and your cost structure has to fit beneath it.
  • Suppliers, raw materials and labour are themselves markets, which is why the word keeps appearing: anywhere buyers and sellers meet, the same supply-and-demand logic applies.
  • The economy, tax and regulations are the market's weather and its rulebook — they can grow, shrink, create or abolish your market without touching anything inside your business.
  • For profit, the strategist's job is simple to state and hard to do: align every controllable factor to an uncontrollable market, then keep re-reading the market before it re-reads you.
