04-3 How Supply and Demand Set the Price

Part of 04 Market

In the last subsection you met the cast: buyers, sellers, and the space between them. Price is what fills that space. It's the number that decides whether a seller bothers to open, whether a buyer bothers to walk in, and — as you'll see — whether your business makes money or just makes noise. This subsection is about where that number actually comes from.

Here's the short version, and the rest of this page unpacks it: price isn't set by you. It's set by the meeting point between what buyers want and what sellers are willing to provide. Your job as a strategist is not to announce a price but to understand that meeting point well enough to make money inside it.

Two curves, one picture

Start with the two sides of the space between buyers and sellers.

Demand is the relationship between a price and the quantity buyers will take at that price. It slopes downwards: as the price falls, more people buy, and the people already buying buy more. This isn't a moral statement, it's arithmetic. At £2 a loaf, Sam buys two and freezes one. At £8, she buys none and makes toast at home. Nothing changed about her appetite — only the price.

Supply is the relationship between a price and the quantity sellers are willing to offer. It slopes upwards, because making more usually costs more. The first hundred loaves come out of the oven during a normal shift. The next hundred need overtime, a second baker, or flour bought at short notice from a pricier supplier. This is the costs connection: each extra unit has its own cost, and a seller will only supply it if the price covers it.

That's why a bakery happily bakes 200 loaves at £4 each and quietly turns the oven off at £2. Turn the price too low and the seller doesn't compete harder — they simply leave.

Where the two meet

Put the two curves together and something clean happens. At prices that are too high, sellers produce more than buyers want and stock sits unsold. At prices that are too low, buyers want more than sellers care to provide and the shelves empty. Somewhere between the two is a price where the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer. That's the equilibrium price, and at that price the market clears — everything made gets sold, and everyone who wanted one got one.

Notice the word clears, not fair. Equilibrium says nothing about whether the price feels reasonable. It only says the two sides match.


Read that loop carefully, because it's the engine of everything that follows. It isn't a committee deciding a price — it's thousands of small decisions correcting each other. Unsold bread gets discounted on Saturday afternoon. A sold-out favourite gets a price rise on Monday, or a bigger batch. The market finds the price by repeatedly overshooting and correcting. Equilibrium is a moving target, not a resting place.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Here's where beginners trip, and where a lot of bad business decisions come from. There are two completely different things that can happen to a market, and people constantly confuse them:

  • A movement along the curve happens when only the price itself changes. Lower the price and buyers take more; that's the same demand, described at a different point.
  • A shift of the whole curve happens when something other than price changes — incomes, costs, tastes, taxes. Now the entire relationship changes, and the market settles at a genuinely new price and quantity.

The distinction matters because only a shift creates a new opportunity or a new threat. A discount on Saturday doesn't change the market; a new office block opening down the street does.

What shifts demand

On the buyers' side, the curve moves when any of these change:

  • Incomes. When the local economy booms, people buy more of almost everything. That's the Economy chapter showing up in your till.
  • Taste and fashion. Sourdough becomes a status symbol; white sliced becomes a joke. Demand moves for reasons no accountant can predict.
  • Prices of substitutes and complements. This is straight out of the Competitors chapter. If the café across the road raises its prices, your demand rises — you're substitutes. If coffee pods get cheaper, the milk people buy goes up — you're complements.
  • The number of buyers. A new office block, a new housing estate, a cruise ship in port.
  • Expectations. If customers think prices are about to rise, they buy now and borrow demand from next month.

What shifts supply

On the sellers' side, the curve moves when the cost of supplying changes:

  • Input costs. A bad harvest raises the price of flour. A fuel spike raises delivery costs. Wage rises lift the cost of every hour worked. Here the Raw Materials, Labour and Suppliers chapters all feed directly into price.
  • Technology. A better oven means more loaves from the same hours — supply shifts out, and prices tend to fall. This is one of the quiet engines of long-run prosperity.
  • Taxes and subsidies. A new tax on sugary drinks raises the cost of supplying them; a subsidy on apprentices lowers the cost of labour. That's the Tax chapter, arriving via the supply curve.
  • Regulation. New hygiene rules, labelling rules or licences add cost, and cost is what supply responds to. Straight from the Regulations chapter.
  • The number of sellers. New entrants add supply. When your industry looks profitable, supply arrives and prices sag — which is exactly why good margins attract competitors.

Worked example: two shocks at Priya's bakery

Priya sells sourdough at £4 a loaf and bakes 100 a day, which sell out by noon. Now two things happen in one month.

Shock one: a new office block opens nearby. Three hundred workers now walk past her door at lunchtime. Nothing about her bakery changed — her bread, her costs and her prices are identical. But demand has shifted. At £4, she now has a queue at 11am and sells out at 11:30. She has three options: raise the price to £4.50 and sell the same 100 to the people who really want them; bake more; or keep everything the same and disappoint customers who then try the café next door. The shift in demand gave her a decision to make — that's the whole point.

Shock two: a poor harvest pushes flour up 20%. Now supply has shifted. Her cost per loaf has risen, so the price that made 100 loaves worth baking no longer does the job. She can't wish the harvest back. Her realistic choices are to charge more, shrink the loaf, cut other costs, or accept a thinner margin.

Notice that neither shock came from her baking. One came from the local economy and the number of buyers; the other from raw materials and her suppliers. That's the honest lesson of supply and demand: the most important movements in your business happen outside your four walls, and the strategist's job is to see them early.

Elasticity: how much does quantity actually move?

There's one more idea worth carrying, because it decides whether a price rise makes you richer or poorer. Elasticity is how much the quantity bought responds to a change in price.

  • Inelastic demand — people barely change what they buy. Insulin, salt, a train ticket to work. Raise the price and sales dip only a little.
  • Elastic demand — people change a lot. Anything with a close substitute: bread from the bakery next door, a mid-range hotel room, a taxi versus a bus.

And here's where supply and demand stops being theory and starts being your profit and loss account. Remember from the Costs chapter that each loaf has a variable cost — flour, butter, the bag — and that what's left after paying it is the contribution toward rent, wages and profit.

Priya's loaf sells for £4 and costs £1.20 in ingredients. She sells 100 a day: contribution = £2.80 × 100 = £280.

She tries a 10% price rise to £4.40, and 15 customers vanish:

Price £4 Price £4.40
Loaves sold 100 85
Revenue £400 £374
Ingredient cost £120 £102
Contribution £280 £272

Both revenue and contribution fell. Her demand was elastic, so the price rise lost more than it gained. On a more inelastic product — say a unique celebration cake nobody else in town makes — the same 10% rise might cost her only two orders, and margin would jump. Same bakery, same arithmetic: elasticity is what decides. Cross-reference this with the Revenue chapter, because revenue is price × quantity, and they move in opposite directions.

What this means for you as a strategist

Pull it together and a few practical rules fall out.

  • You rarely set the price; you discover it. Nearly every business is a price taker in some markets and a price maker in others. The structural reasons sit in the Types of Competition chapter; the day-to-day version is that a rival's price sets a ceiling above your head.
  • You earn margin by shifting your own cost curve, not by arguing with the market price. In highly competitive markets, the baker who shaves two pence off the cost of a loaf keeps that two pence. Everyone else is stuck.
  • You can change your own elasticity. Differentiate — better service, a location, a brand, a loyalty scheme — and your customers stop treating you as a straight swap for the shop next door. That's how you move away from "price is the only thing that matters".
  • Forecast, then plan, then check. Demand shifts can be anticipated, because they leave traces: job adverts, planning applications, harvest reports, interest rate news. That's the planning and controlling loop from the management chapter applied to price.
  • Watch the whole chain. Your suppliers' costs, your staff's wages, the taxes and rules you pay — every one of them is a supply-side shift waiting to happen.

Summary

  • Demand slopes down and supply slopes up; price is where the two meet. Nothing about either curve is a moral judgement — it's just how buyers and sellers respond.
  • The meeting point is the equilibrium price, where the market clears. It's a moving target that markets find by repeatedly overshooting and correcting.
  • Distinguish a movement along a curve (only the price changed) from a shift of the curve (income, costs, tastes, taxes, rivals, regulation changed). Only shifts create new opportunities and threats.
  • Demand shifters: incomes, taste, substitute and complement prices, number of buyers, expectations. Supply shifters: input costs, technology, taxes, regulation, number of sellers.
  • Elasticity decides whether a price rise helps you. Elastic demand means customers leave; inelastic demand means they grumble and stay.
  • The profitability connection is direct: revenue is price × quantity, and profit depends on the contribution left after variable costs. Elasticity and cost control are what you actually control.
  • Strategy in one line: you don't argue with the market price — you lower your costs, sharpen your differentiation, and move your own curves.
