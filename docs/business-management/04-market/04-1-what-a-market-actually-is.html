04-1 What a Market Actually Is

Part of 04 Market

Ask someone to point at "the market" and they'll usually point at a building — the covered hall with the fruit stalls, the trading floor, the website. That instinct isn't wrong, exactly. It's just too narrow, and getting the definition right changes what you can see for the rest of this chapter.

The market isn't the place — it's the arrangement

Here's the definition worth keeping:

A market is the whole set of buyers and sellers who could trade a particular thing with each other, plus the channel and the rules that let them do it.

Five pieces, and only one of them is a location:

  • The thing being traded — a product or a service, from a loaf of bread to an hour of legal advice. (This is why the Product chapter and this one lean on each other.)
  • Buyers — the people or firms who want it, and have the money and the willingness to pay.
  • Sellers — the people or firms who can supply it.
  • The channel — how buyer and seller actually find each other and complete the deal. A shop, a website, an app, a broker, a wholesaler, a phone call.
  • The rules — the going price, the currency, the contract law, the quality standards, and the plain old trust that lets a stranger hand over money and expect goods in return.

A farmers' market has all five. So does a stock exchange. So does a B2B contract negotiated over email for 20,000 ball bearings. The hall with the stalls is just one possible channel — the market itself is the arrangement.

Why the word trips people up

"Market" is doing at least four jobs in everyday speech, and mixing them up causes real confusion:

  • A place. "I'm going to the market." A physical building or street.
  • A set of buyers and sellers. "The housing market." No building required — just everyone willing to trade houses.
  • Demand only. "There's no market for that." Here it means buyers, or the lack of them.
  • A competitive arena. "The UK coffee market." Now it means the whole contest — buyers, sellers and the fight between them.

When a report says "the market grew 4%", it usually means sales rose. When your colleague says "the market won't pay that", they mean buyers won't. Same word, different meaning. Ask which one is meant and half the confusion evaporates.

Markets without walls

Once you accept that a market is an arrangement rather than a place, a lot of things fall into line:

  • A stock exchange is a market with no shopfront at all. Buyers and sellers never meet; a computer matches them and a price appears.
  • The labour market — the subject of the Labour chapter — is a market where the thing traded is people's time and skill.
  • Online marketplaces connect a seller in one country to a buyer in another, with the platform supplying the rules and the trust.
  • A wholesale market for raw materials is what the Suppliers and Raw Materials chapters sit inside.

The test is simple: can a buyer and a seller actually complete an exchange here — and do enough of them do it regularly that a going price exists? If yes, you have a market. Walls optional.

The tricky part: which market are you actually in?

This is where the definition earns its keep. A market isn't defined by what the sellers look like — it's defined by what the buyers treat as a realistic alternative.

That connects straight to the Competitors chapter. If you remember the test from there — if my customer couldn't buy from me, where would this money go instead? — you already know how to draw a market's boundary. The sellers who answer that question are, by definition, in your market.

So the boundary question is really a substitution test:

  • A pizza takeaway and a sushi restaurant are in the same market if enough customers genuinely choose between them on a Friday night.
  • Two firms with identical industry codes are in different markets if they sell to completely different buyers at completely different prices.
  • And a supermarket meal deal is in your market if it's taking the same lunch money — no matter what the trade directory says.

Draw the line too tightly ("my market is bakeries on this street") and you'll miss the rival that's actually eating your lunch. Draw it too loosely ("my market is everything anyone could spend £5 on") and you'll paralyse yourself. The useful boundary is where customers genuinely pause and choose.

The market shapes everything else you'll decide

Once you can name your market, four big things stop being mysteries:


Each of those ties into a chapter of its own. Customers and their numbers feed directly into Revenue — your sales can never exceed the market's size multiplied by the share you can win, so a small market puts a hard ceiling on the business. Rivals and their intensity come back to structure — a crowded market of identical sellers behaves nothing like a market with three giants in it. Price is set by the market's buyers and sellers together, which is exactly what the next two subsections cover. And channel is the Distribution question: the same product sold in a shop and sold wholesale sits in two different markets, with two different sets of rivals.

This is where management comes back in. Management, as we saw at the start of the book, is setting goals and coordinating resources towards them. Choosing which markets to play in — and how to win in them — is the biggest goal-setting decision a business ever makes. Everything downstream (what you make, who you hire, what you charge, where you sell) is a consequence of that choice. A brilliant team in a shrinking market will lose to a mediocre team in a growing one.

Worked example: which market is Priya in?

Priya runs a bakery. Ask her what market she's in and she might say "the bakery market". Let's see how unhelpful that is.

  • On Saturdays at the farmers' market, she sells loaves against four other bread stalls. Buyers compare on price and freshness. Tight, local, price-sensitive.
  • On weekdays in her shop, she's selling breakfast and a mid-morning break to office workers. Her real alternatives aren't other bakeries — they're the supermarket meal deal, the vending machine and the coffee chain. Wider market, less about bread.
  • For the cafés she supplies wholesale, she's in a completely different market: three big bakeries bidding on delivery contracts where reliability and price per unit matter far more than crust.

Same ovens, same recipes, three markets. Each one has different buyers, different rivals, a different going price, and a different thing that wins. If Priya manages all three with one strategy, she'll be mediocre in all three.

Three mistakes to avoid

Confusing your market with your industry. Your industry is the group of firms that make similar things. Your market is the group of buyers and sellers who could trade with each other. They overlap, but a rival from outside your industry can still be stealing your customers — which is how whole trades get ambushed.

Assuming the market is fixed. Markets appear, shrink, split and get regulated. Ride-hailing created a market that barely existed; streaming hollowed out the market for DVD rentals. A good strategist watches the market's edges, because that's where the next threat or the next opening will come from. The Regulations chapter is largely about what happens when rules reshape a market overnight.

Forgetting that a market needs buyers with money and willingness. Demand isn't the same as desire. Plenty of people would like a handmade £40 loaf; almost none will buy one. That gap between "people want it" and "people will pay for it" is where a lot of hopeful businesses die.

Summary

  • A market is the set of buyers and sellers who could trade a particular thing, plus the channel and rules that let them do it. The place is optional; the arrangement is not.
  • The word carries four everyday meanings — a place, a set of traders, demand, and a competitive arena. Knowing which one is meant prevents a lot of muddle.
  • Markets exist without walls: stock exchanges, labour markets, online platforms, wholesale supply.
  • A market's boundary is set by the substitution test — who customers genuinely treat as an alternative — not by industry labels or what sellers happen to look like.
  • Your market determines your customers, rivals, realistic price and channel — which makes choosing markets the single biggest strategic decision management makes.
  • One business can sit in several markets at once, each with its own rivals and its own winning move.
  • Markets are not fixed. Watch their edges: that's where threats and opportunities arrive.
