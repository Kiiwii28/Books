04-2 Buyers, Sellers, and the Space Between Them

Part of 04 Market

You've already met the market as an arena where a good or service gets bought and sold. But an arena isn't just a floor — it's full of people. Two kinds, in fact: the people who want to buy, the people who want to sell, and a surprising amount of machinery standing in between them. That in-between part is where most of the money, and most of the strategy, actually lives.

The two obvious parties

Start with the buyer. A buyer has a need, some money, limited time, limited information, and a set of alternatives. Their private question is simple: is this worth it, compared with everything else I could do with this money?

Now the seller. A seller has something to offer, a cost base, a capacity limit, and — this is the part beginners skip — a need to sell enough of it to cover the costs of making it at all. Their private question is: can I sell this at a price that leaves something over?

Both sides are trying to read a person they can't fully see. Economists call this information asymmetry, and it's the reason markets are never as clean as the diagrams suggest. Priya knows exactly when her bread came out of the oven; you don't. You know exactly how much you're willing to pay; she doesn't.

One more thing worth noticing early: almost everyone is both. Priya sells loaves and buys flour, butter, electricity and cardboard boxes. So does the industrial baker down the road, and so does the supermarket she sells to. Whenever you think about a market, ask which side of it am I standing on right now? — because the same person can be a seller in one transaction and a buyer in the next one, five minutes later.

The gap nobody sees

If buying and selling were simple, a hungry person with £4 and a baker with a loaf would just find each other and swap. Often they can't, and the reasons are worth naming, because each one is a business opportunity in disguise.

  • Space. The bakery is four miles from the office where the hunger is.
  • Time. The flour mill harvests once a year and sells continuously; the bakery buys every week.
  • Information. You don't know which of the twelve nearby bakeries is any good, and the good one doesn't know you exist.
  • Trust. Will you actually pay? Will they actually deliver what was promised?
  • Quantity and matching. The mill sells flour by the tonne. Priya needs four 16kg sacks. Neither of them wants to change their size for the other.
  • Risk. Somebody has to buy stock before anyone has confirmed they want it — and swallow the loss if the loaves don't sell.

Those gaps are the reason a market is more than a place. A market is a system that bridges them, over and over, at a cost low enough for everyone to come out ahead.


Every arrow in that chain is a place where a deal can stall. Bridge all of them cheaply and you have a working market.

Who fills the gap — and why they get paid

The bridges have names. Wholesalers buy in bulk and break it into smaller amounts. Retailers put goods where buyers already are. Distributors move stock between them. Agents and brokers match buyers to sellers without ever owning the goods. Marketplaces and platforms host the whole exchange and take a cut. Banks and payment providers solve the "will you actually pay?" gap. Insurers carry the risk that something goes wrong. Logistics firms close the space gap, one van at a time.

Notice that none of them make the product. They exist because closing a gap is worth money — and the reason they get paid is worth saying plainly: the middleman must be cheaper than the gap they close. If a wholesaler's markup is bigger than the cost of Priya driving to the mill herself and storing a pallet of flour in her back room, she'll drive. Middlemen are not parasites or heroes; they're a cost you accept because the alternative costs more. (The Suppliers and Distribution chapters dig into choosing and managing them properly.)

This works in both directions over time. When the internet made it cheap for a bakery to reach customers directly, some intermediaries got squeezed out — disintermediation. Then a new wave appeared: delivery platforms, online marketplaces, review sites. Re-intermediation. The gap rarely closes; it just gets filled by somebody new.

The connective tissue: trust, rules, and reputation

Here's the puzzle at the heart of every market: you hand over money to a stranger and expect bread in return. Why does that work?

Partly reputation. Brands, reviews, star ratings, "family run since 1974", a logo you recognise on a van. All of it is compressed evidence that the seller has something to lose by cheating you.

Partly rules and enforcement. Contract law, consumer protection, food safety standards, weights and measures, licences, and the threat of a fine or a lawsuit. This is regulation doing its quiet job — it makes strangers safe to trade with. (The Regulations chapter takes this further; here, just note that a market without rules gets smaller, because people stop trusting it.)

And partly the price, which is the most remarkable piece of information-compression ever invented: a single number that tells you, all at once, roughly how scarce something is, how much others value it, and whether you should bother. How that number gets set is the subject of the next subsection — but you can already see that it only works because all this trust and infrastructure is holding it up.

Worked example: Priya, on both sides of the same market

Priya's bakery can make 3,000 loaves a month. Here's how she sells them, and what each route costs her.

Route Loaves Price Priya receives Revenue
Her own shop counter 2,000 £4.00 £8,000
A delivery app 600 £3.00 (after 25% commission) £1,800
Wholesale to a café chain 400 £2.60 £1,040
Total 3,000 £10,840

Look at the app and the wholesaler and you might think: they're skimming £1.00 and £1.40 a loaf — close them down. But try it. Her shop counter only closes the "space" gap for people who live nearby; she physically cannot sell 3,000 loaves over one counter in one month, because there aren't enough passers-by. The app closes the space and convenience gaps for customers across town; the wholesaler closes the matching and volume gaps in one meeting. Both charge for it. Both buy her something she doesn't have: reach.

Now flip her to the buying side. Priya needs flour. The mill sells by the tonne at roughly £17 per 16kg sack's worth; a wholesaler delivers four sacks a week at £24 each. On paper the mill is far cheaper — but the tonne minimum would tie up about £1,000 of her cash and fill her back room with flour she'd be paying insurance on. The wholesaler's £7-per-sack premium buys her cash, space and time, and lets her order again next week at whatever volume her sales actually justify.

So the honest test, in both directions, is never "is there a middleman?" It's "which gap is this middleman closing, and is it cheaper than closing it myself?"

What a strategist does with this

This subsection is short, but it changes how you look at your own business.

First: you can compete on the gap, not just the product. Plenty of very profitable companies never make anything better than their rivals. They simply make buying easier — faster delivery, painless returns, clearer information, a stronger guarantee, a shelf in a more convenient place. Those aren't marketing gimmicks. They're real reductions in the buyer's cost of doing business with you, and customers pay for them. Keep this in mind when you reach the Stores, Distribution and Customer Service chapters; it's the thread that ties them together.

Second: whoever stands between you and your customer shapes your whole business. Sell through your own shop and you keep more of the £4, control the experience, and face one set of competitors. Sell through an app or a wholesaler and you get reach, but you hand over margin and some control over pricing and service — and, as the Competitors chapter pointed out, your competitive set changes completely. Wholesale Priya is fighting industrial bakers on price and reliability, not competing with the café two doors down on atmosphere.

Third: the gap is your map of opportunities. Walk through the six gaps above and ask, honestly, which one is being badly closed in your market right now. Slow delivery? Confusing information? Nobody willing to sell in the small quantities people actually want? That's usually where a small business can out-position a bigger one — not by being cheaper, but by being easier.

And fourth: none of it happens by itself. Someone has to choose the channels, work out what each one really costs after commissions, check that they're producing what you expected, and change course when they aren't. That's the management loop from the opening chapter, applied to your place in the market. Good strategists don't drift into a channel because everyone else uses it — they pick it deliberately and measure what it earns.

One caution before we move on. Not every gap is worth closing, and not every middleman earns their keep. Same-day delivery sounds wonderful until you work out that customers won't pay enough to cover it. A distributor you can't attach to any specific gap is probably just a cost line. The discipline is to name the gap, name the price of closing it, and compare that with what buyers will actually pay.

Summary

  • Every market has buyers (needs, money, limited time and information) and sellers (a product, a cost base, a capacity limit) — and most people are both, depending on the transaction.
  • Buyers and sellers are separated by gaps: space, time, information, trust, quantity and matching, and risk.
  • A market is the system that bridges those gaps cheaply enough for both sides to benefit.
  • Intermediaries — wholesalers, retailers, distributors, agents, platforms, banks, insurers, logistics — exist to close specific gaps, and they earn their keep only when they cost less than the gap they close.
  • Trust, reputation and rules are the connective tissue that makes trading with strangers safe; prices compress all of it into one number.
  • Strategically, you can win by closing a gap better than anyone else, and your choice of who stands between you and the buyer changes your margins, your control, and who your competitors are.
  • The standing test: name the gap, price the fix, and check what buyers will pay. An intermediary you can't attach to a gap is just a cost.

Next, we'll look at how that single number — the price — gets set by supply and demand.
