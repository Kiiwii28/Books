19-3 Economy as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 19 Economy

Every other factor in this book has a name, an address and a phone number. You can renegotiate with a supplier, retrain a worker, redesign a product, re-site a shop. The economy has none of that. You can't ring it up, you can't switch to a cheaper one, and it will not take your call. It simply arrives — in your flour bill, your customers' confidence, your loan repayments and the wage you have to offer — whether or not you planned for it.

So this subsection isn't about predicting it. Nobody reliably can, and the people who claim to are usually selling something. It's about seeing how it travels through a business, so that when it moves you already know where it's going to land.

The weather your whole business stands in

Here's the reframe worth carrying. Every factor you've studied in this book is priced inside the economy.

Your suppliers buy their energy, pay their staff and borrow their money in the same economy you do — which means their costs move whether or not they change anything. Your customers earn, borrow and spend inside it. Your wages are set by it. The interest rate on your oven is decided by it. The Economy chapter isn't a topic that sits alongside suppliers, labour and financials; it's the weather all four of them live in.

Which also tells you what you can and can't do about it. You cannot change the forecast. You can change how exposed you are to it, and that is a management decision — planning, organizing and controlling, from the opening chapter, applied to something you don't control.

Four dials that move almost every business

Macroeconomics has hundreds of variables. Four of them do most of the damage.

Dial What it is What it does to you
Growth Total spending in the economy Moves your volume — how many people walk through the door
Inflation The general rise in prices Moves your costs, often faster than you can move your prices
Interest rates The price of borrowed money Decides what investments you can afford, and how much spare cash your customers have
Employment and wages How many people are working, and at what pay Sets your wage bill and how hard hiring is

Add a fifth if you buy or sell across a border: exchange rates. A currency that moves 10% changes your cost of goods overnight without a single supplier lifting a finger. Same logic, different dial.

Notice something about that table. Growth and employment tend to help revenue; inflation and interest rates tend to hurt costs and cash. Most painful periods are a combination — which is exactly the scenario the worked example below walks through.

How one shock travels

Here's the shape of it. A single economic shift doesn't hit one chapter of your business. It fans out through all of them and meets again at the profit line.


That last arrow is the one to watch. When profit falls, businesses cut investment, hiring and stock — and if enough of them do it at once, that is the recession, feeding itself. You're not just standing in the weather; at scale, businesses like yours are the weather.

Upstream: suppliers and raw materials

Most economic shocks reach you first through what you buy. Commodity prices — wheat, steel, oil, cocoa — are set on world markets, which is the Raw Materials chapter's point about distance: you don't control the price, you inherit it.

Three economic forces do the inheriting:

  • Inflation raises your suppliers' own costs, and they pass on what they can. There's usually a lag of weeks or months between the world moving and your invoice changing — which is why watching the market beats watching your in-tray.
  • Currency moves the price of anything imported, duties and freight included, long before your supplier bothers to reissue a price list.
  • Growth cuts both ways. A boom can leave suppliers short of capacity, so lead times stretch and Distribution gets slower just when you need it most.

The strategic move here is boring and effective: know which two or three inputs carry most of your cost, and follow them. Ignore the rest.

The middle: labour, capacity and investment

The economy runs through your people and your machines too.

Wages follow the labour market, as the Labour chapter set out. In a boom, skilled staff are scarce, your best baker gets poached by the café down the road, and the market rate for a replacement rises. In a downturn, applications flood in — but so do your customers' reasons to spend less.

Interest rates decide your capital spending. A second oven, a bigger van, a second site — all of them are bets that only pay off at a certain cost of borrowing. The Manufacturing chapter's fixed-versus-variable arithmetic doesn't change with the interest rate, but the decision does. That's why the same investment can be sensible in March and reckless in September without anything about the oven changing.

And the economy shifts the automation question. When wages rise and borrowing is cheap, replacing hours with a machine looks attractive. When wages are flat and money is expensive, flexible labour wins. The Costs chapter's fixed-versus-variable trade gets re-priced every few years by forces entirely outside your control.

Downstream: the market, your price and your rivals

Then it reaches your customers — and this is where the Market chapter's elasticity stops being theory.

In a squeeze, customers don't just buy less. They become more price-sensitive. The same loaf that sold comfortably at £4.40 last year may sit unsold at £4.20 now, because the alternatives — the supermarket meal deal, making it at home, skipping it — have all become more attractive. Your demand curve hasn't moved along; it has shifted. The Types of Competition chapter adds the second half of the story: when a whole market shrinks, rivals get desperate, and desperate rivals in an oligopoly start price wars. Margins get shredded for everybody.

And the reverse is true in a boom, which is worth remembering because it's where the real mistakes get made. Demand is easy, so you stop being sharp. You hire in a hurry, you sign a long lease, you add capacity you'll regret. Booms don't just make businesses richer — they make them fragile.

The financial plumbing underneath all of it

For most businesses, the economy shows up hardest in cash, not profit.

  • Interest rates change what your overdraft, lease or loan costs — and a rate rise on a business with thin margins can be the difference between a small profit and a small loss.
  • Inflation makes holding stock a hedge and makes borrowing expensive at the same time. The Stores chapter's carrying-cost arithmetic shifts, and with it your ideal stock level.
  • The cash conversion gap — you pay your suppliers before your customers pay you — gets wider in a downturn, because your customers start paying late. The Financials chapter covers this properly; the economic version is that a squeeze squeezes twice, once on demand and once on cash.

Worked example: Priya's squeeze year

Nothing catastrophic happens to Priya's bakery. No fire, no lost contract, no rival opening next door. The economy just moves four dials a little.

Annual Normal year Squeeze year What moved
Revenue £120,000 £110,400 Demand down 8%
Materials £36,000 £37,100 Flour up 12%, volume down 8%
Wages £41,000 £43,500 Local wages up 6%
Rent, energy, other £28,000 £29,400 Costs up 5%
Loan interest £2,000 £3,200 Interest rate rise
Profit £13,000 −£2,800

A £13,000 profit has become a £2,800 loss. Look at how modest each individual move was: 8%, 12%, 6%, 5%. None of them would make a headline. Together they wiped out a whole year's earnings and more — which is precisely why the economy is so easy to underrate until it's already happened.

Now watch how she responds, because the shape of the response is the lesson:

  • Cut waste. Her spoilage sits at 5% of materials. Getting it to 2% is worth about £1,100 — no negotiation, no customer needed. The cheapest profit in the building, exactly as the Raw Materials chapter argued.
  • Raise one price, not all of them. She adds 15p to the counter loaf, where her regulars are least likely to switch, and leaves the wholesale price alone because the café contract is elastic. Worth roughly £2,000, and it's the same elasticity arithmetic from the Market chapter.
  • Defer the second oven. The capex plan made sense at 5% interest. At 8%, the break-even volume — 25 extra loaves a day, in the Manufacturing chapter's sums — looks a lot further away.
  • Hold less stock. Freeing cash out of the storeroom is worth more, not less, when borrowing is dear.
  • Renegotiate. She asks the mill for a committed-volume price, and gets a little, because she's a predictable customer.

Those two small operational moves recover about £3,100 — enough to get back to roughly break-even, not back to profit. Full recovery needs the structural decisions. That's honest, and it's typical: the economy takes a year off you, and it takes more than one clever fix to get it back.

Not every business feels the same weather

Two businesses on the same street can have completely different economic exposures, and knowing which one you run is strategy.

  • Cyclical businesses sell things people can postpone — holidays, new kitchens, cars, weddings. They swing hard with growth.
  • Defensive businesses sell things people can't skip — bread, electricity, basic healthcare, repairs. They barely move.
  • Elastic products (commodity bread, budget hotels) lose volume fast when customers have less to spend.
  • Inelastic products (a unique prescription, the only bakery in a village, a specialist tool) hold up far better.

If your product is cyclical and elastic, you are running a business that will be tested hard roughly every decade, and your cost structure needs to reflect that. If you're defensive and inelastic, your risk is complacency, not collapse.

What a strategist does with it

  • Know which two dials move you most. A bakery watches wheat prices, local wages and consumer confidence. A van hire firm watches fuel and interest rates. Write yours down.
  • Build buffers before you need them. Cash reserves, a second supplier, a flexible layer of labour rather than all-permanent contracts. Buffers cost a little every good year and save the business in a bad one.
  • Watch leading signals, not headlines. Your own sales pipeline, job adverts in your sector, commodity prices, rate announcements, your suppliers' lead times. These are controlling, from the management loop — and they arrive weeks before your accounts do.
  • Match your cost structure to your volatility. The more the economy can swing your sales, the more you want costs that move with them.
  • Don't over-react to one quarter, and don't ignore three. Two data points in a row is noise; a trend across several is a signal.
  • Use downturns as well as surviving them. Good staff become available, premises get cheaper, weaker rivals exit. The Competitors chapter's advice holds: a market shake-out is a repositioning opportunity for whoever is still standing.

And the thread that runs through all of it: the economy is the factor you can't manage, which makes it the factor that defines good management. Anyone can run a business through easy conditions. The strategist's edge is being positioned for the other kind — with a margin that can absorb a shock, cash that can wait out a bad quarter, and a set of numbers honest enough to tell you it's coming.

Summary

  • The economy is the weather every other factor in this book operates inside. You can't change it; you can only change your exposure to it.
  • Four dials do most of the work: growth (your volume), inflation (your costs), interest rates (your investments and cash) and employment and wages (your wage bill). Add exchange rates if you trade across a border.
  • A single economic shift fans out through suppliers, labour, market and financials at once, meeting again at the profit line — and your resulting cutbacks become part of the economy itself.
  • It reaches you upstream through input prices and currency, in the middle through wages, hiring and the case for capital spending, and downstream through demand, elasticity and the behaviour of desperate rivals.
  • It hits cash harder than profit: interest costs, the stock-holding trade-off, and customers who start paying late.
  • Small numbers compound — in the worked example, moves of 8%, 12%, 6% and 5% turned a £13,000 profit into a £2,800 loss.
  • Businesses differ enormously in exposure: cyclical vs defensive, elastic vs inelastic. Know which you are, and set your cost structure accordingly.
  • The strategist's habit: know your two key dials, hold buffers, read leading signals, keep costs flexible where sales are volatile, and treat a downturn as a repositioning opportunity — all while running the same plan–organize–lead–control loop you'd use on anything else.
