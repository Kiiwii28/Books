19-4 Economy vs. Other Factors

Part of 19 Economy

Of every force in this book, the economy is the only one that shows up in every other factor's chapter. Competitors fight you. Customers choose you. Suppliers serve you. The economy doesn't do any of those things — it quietly changes the value of everything you already control. That's the whole relationship in one sentence, and it's worth understanding deeply, because most business owners get it backwards. They treat the economy as just another rival to beat, when really it's the set of conditions under which every other contest is played.

Why This Comparison Is Different From All the Others

Look back at the other "vs." sections and you'll spot a pattern. Company vs. competitors. Suppliers vs. raw materials. Each of those is a peer comparison: two forces on roughly similar footing, interacting directly.

Economy vs. anything is not a peer comparison. It's a layered one.

Think of it as a chessboard. Your company, your product, your staff, your prices — those are the pieces. The economy is the board itself: its size, its shape, the rules of movement. When the board changes, none of your pieces have moved, yet every one of them is suddenly worth something different. A strategy that wins on a small board loses on a big one. Same pieces, different game.

There's a second reason this comparison is unusual: control. You can negotiate with a supplier. You can out-design a competitor. You can fire a manager. You cannot negotiate with a recession. The economy sits at the far end of a spectrum that runs from "fully under your control" to "completely outside it," and knowing where each factor falls on that spectrum is arguably the single most useful strategic skill there is.

Position on the control spectrum Factors
Fully yours Product, Staff, Management, Stores, Customer Service, Distribution choices
Mostly yours Company, Manufacturing, Sales approach, Costs, Labour
Shared with others Suppliers, Raw Materials, Financials, Revenue
Barely yours Competitors, Market
Not yours at all Economy, Tax, Regulations

Notice that the three factors you can't touch — economy, tax, regulations — are exactly the three that appear last in this book, and exactly the three that set the boundaries inside which all the others operate.

The Transmission Belt: How the Economy Actually Reaches You

The economy never hits your business directly. It arrives through specific channels, and if you can name the channel, you can respond to it.


Read that diagram left to right and you've got the entire chapter in miniature. Growth, interest rates and inflation change how much people buy (market), what you pay for inputs and people (costs), and what the government takes and demands (tax, regulations). Those changes land on your sales and costs, which land on your financials, which determine whether you can invest in staff, stock and stores.

Now let's walk the comparisons factor by factor.

Economy vs. the Outside Forces: Market, Competitors, Tax, Regulations

Market. This is the tightest relationship of all, because a market is a slice of the economy. When the Economy chapter talks about growth and confidence, and the Market chapter talks about supply, demand and price, they're describing the same phenomenon at two zoom levels. A recession is simply the macro version of the demand curve shifting left. Your market chapter taught you that price and quantity move together; the economy is one of the biggest things that moves them.

The practical implication: market size is not a constant. A business plan that says "the market is worth $500 million" is really saying "the market is worth $500 million under these economic conditions." Strategists who survived 2008 or 2020 learned to model market size as a range, not a number.

Competitors. Here's the cruel bit. When the economy contracts, the market shrinks — but the number of competitors doesn't. Often it grows, because laid-off workers start side hustles and because struggling firms slash prices to survive. You get a smaller pie and more people at the table. Competition becomes price competition, margins collapse, and the strongest balance sheet wins. Conversely, in a boom, everyone looks like a genius; weak operators survive on rising tide alone. The economy doesn't create or destroy competitors, but it decides how brutal the fight between them is.

Tax. Tax and the economy are siblings — both are set by the same government, often at the same time, and they pull in opposite directions. A government trying to cool an overheating economy raises interest rates and may raise taxes; trying to stimulate, it cuts both. This means your tax chapter and your economy chapter should always be read together: a tax rise during a slowdown hits twice, once through the rate and once through the timing.

Regulations. Same story. Regulation often responds to the economy — heavier financial rules after a crash, looser planning rules in a housing slump. Regulation is also the mechanism through which the economy's side effects get managed: minimum wage laws track inflation, environmental rules track energy prices. When you model the economy, you're implicitly modelling the regulatory and tax environment that comes with it.

Economy vs. the Value Chain: Product, Suppliers, Raw Materials, Manufacturing, Distribution, Stores

This is where the economy stops being abstract and starts showing up on invoices.

Product. Every product has a cyclicality — how sensitive its sales are to the economic cycle. At one end: holidays, luxury cars, restaurant meals, business software. At the other: bread, soap, electricity, haircuts, cheap chocolate. Economists call the resilient ones "inferior" or "defensive" goods, which sounds insulting but is actually a compliment — when incomes fall, demand for them rises, because people trade down from premium to value.

This links straight back to the Product Life Cycle chapter. Product life cycles don't run on a clock; they run on a clock plus an economy. A product in maturity can be pushed back into growth by a downturn that makes customers switch to cheaper alternatives — or shoved into decline by a boom that makes them trade up and never come back. The economy is an accelerant you don't control. This is why smart firms run a portfolio: some products that shine in booms, some that shine in busts, so the business doesn't swing as hard as the economy does.

Suppliers. Two economic forces hit your suppliers simultaneously. First, their own costs move — energy, transport, wages — and they pass those on to you. Second, the cost of money moves. When interest rates rise, your supplier's overdraft gets more expensive, and suddenly they're pushing for shorter payment terms or cash on delivery. That hits your working capital, which is a Financials chapter problem. Notice how one macro change cascades through three chapters at once. This is the standing preference of this book in action: factors are never isolated.

Raw Materials. Raw materials are where the economy gets literal. Commodity prices are driven by global growth (more building in Asia means more steel demand), by currency movements (a weaker dollar makes oil more expensive for everyone else), and by speculative money flows chasing returns when interest rates are low. Your raw materials chapter taught you about types and sourcing. The economy chapter tells you why the price on the contract changes every quarter. The strategic response — long-term contracts, index clauses, hedging, dual sourcing — exists precisely because raw material prices are macro-driven.

Manufacturing. Manufacturing is capital-hungry, which makes it exquisitely sensitive to interest rates. A new production line financed at 5% versus 9% is a completely different investment decision; the machine costs the same, the money doesn't. Manufacturing is also where capacity utilisation bites. Factories have high fixed costs, so when volumes fall, unit costs rocket. This is the operating leverage point from the Costs chapter, and it's why manufacturers shed shifts so aggressively in recessions: every idle hour is money evaporating.

Distribution. The economy reshapes channels. In a downturn, customers migrate to cheaper routes — from your physical store to your website, from your website to a marketplace. Meanwhile distribution costs move with fuel prices, driver wages and interest rates on vehicle fleets. A long, elaborate channel that made sense at high volumes becomes a millstone at low ones. The Distribution chapter's question — which route to the customer? — is answered partly by economics, not just by strategy.

Stores. Retail is the most visible casualty of every downturn, because stores carry two economic burdens at once: rent (a fixed cost, often inflation-linked) and footfall (a variable that collapses). A retail lease signed in a boom becomes a liability in a bust. This is why store portfolios shrink and shift in recessions: fewer, better locations, shorter leases, more pop-ups. The economy doesn't close your stores; it changes whether the rent is affordable at the sales volume they generate.

Economy vs. the People: Labour, Staff, Management

Labour. The economy is the labour market. Unemployment, wage levels, and the availability of skills are all macro variables — you don't set the market rate for a welder, the economy does. In a boom, labour is scarce and expensive and you compete on perks; in a bust, labour is abundant and cheap and you can hire talent you couldn't touch last year. Cynical but true: recessions are the best recruiting opportunities most companies ever get.

There's a subtlety worth knowing — wages are sticky downwards. People resist pay cuts far more than they resist not getting a raise, so in a downturn firms cut headcount rather than rates. That's why unemployment rises sharply in recessions even when total wage bills fall only slightly.

Staff. Your staff chapter focused on roles and structures; the economy decides what those roles cost and whether you can afford them. Every hire is a long-term commitment priced at short-term rates. When inflation runs at 8% and your revenue grows at 3%, you are getting poorer every month with the same headcount — a silent squeeze that's easy to miss because nothing has visibly gone wrong.

Management. This is the factor that decides whether the economy hurts you or helps you. Management is your only real response mechanism. The economy hands out the same conditions to every firm in your industry; management is what differentiates the outcomes. A mediocre management team in a boom looks brilliant and then gets found out. A strong one in a downturn renegotiates supplier contracts, reallocates marketing spend to cheaper channels, buys a failed competitor's assets at 40 cents on the dollar, and comes out of the trough with more market share than it went in with.

The counter-cyclical playbook is the highest form of this: advertise when rivals cut, hire when rivals freeze, expand when rents fall, and hold cash when everyone else is leveraged. Buffett's line — be fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful — is really a statement about the economy.

Economy vs. the Money: Sales, Customer Service, Revenue, Costs, Financials

Sales. Sales is where the economy becomes measurable. Volume and price both move with the cycle, but rarely together: in a boom you sell more and charge more; in a bust you discount to hold volume, so revenue falls even when unit sales look fine. Watch that gap. It's the earliest reliable signal of a downturn in your own business — earlier than the GDP statistics, which arrive months late.

Customer Service. In a downturn, acquisition costs rise (because buyers are cautious) while retention becomes cheaper than ever to justify — keeping an existing customer costs a fraction of winning a new one. So the economy quietly elevates customer service from a nice-to-have to a survival tool. Firms that cut service in recessions to save money often find they've cut the very thing that was holding their revenue steady.

Revenue. Revenue is the meeting point of market demand, product appeal and price — all three of which the economy moves. Revenue is also where the volume vs. value distinction matters: a discount retailer can grow revenue in a recession while a luxury brand shrinks, not because one is better managed but because their revenue streams have different economic betas.

Costs. Perhaps the strongest link in this entire chapter. Costs come in two flavours — fixed and variable — and the economy punishes fixed costs in a downturn and rewards them in an upturn. A business with 80% fixed costs and 20% variable is a machine that swings wildly with the cycle; flip the ratio and you get stability but capped upside. Choosing that ratio is a strategic decision about how much economic risk you want to carry. Most businesses that die in recessions don't die from falling sales; they die from fixed costs that didn't fall with them.

Financials. Everything above funnels into your financial statements, which is why Financials is the last internal factor before Revenue, Costs and the macro trio. Two specifics deserve emphasis. Cash flow, not profit, kills businesses in downturns — your customers pay slower, your suppliers demand faster, your bank tightens covenants, and a profitable firm can still run out of money. And gearing (how much debt you carry) determines whether a rate rise is an inconvenience or an execution. A 2-point rate rise on a $2 million loan is $40,000 a year — trivial for one company, fatal for another with the same trading performance.

Worked Example: One Roastery, One Recession

Let's make this concrete. Bean & Brew is a coffee roastery with $4 million in revenue: 60% wholesale to cafés, 40% direct-to-consumer bags online. It has 18 staff, a $600,000 bank loan for its second roaster, and a five-year lease on a warehouse.

Then the economy turns. Growth goes negative, unemployment rises, interest rates jump from 3% to 7%, and inflation sits at 6%.

Watch the cascade, factor by factor:

  1. Market: office workers stop buying mid-morning coffee. Café footfall drops 15%. Wholesale orders fall 20%.
  2. Raw materials: the currency weakens, so imported green coffee costs 12% more.
  3. Labour and staff: wage expectations lag but staff still want an inflation-matching rise — a 6% ask on a $700,000 wage bill is $42,000.
  4. Costs: rent is fixed and inflation-linked, so it rises. The loan repayment rises by $24,000 a year.
  5. Sales and revenue: DTC volume holds up (coffee is fairly defensive) but wholesale collapses. Revenue falls 11%.
  6. Financials: profit nearly vanishes, and cash tightens because cafés now pay in 60 days instead of 30.
  7. Competitors: two local rivals cut prices to defend volume, inviting a price war in wholesale.

Now the strategic response — and notice it uses every chapter:

  • Product: push the home-brew subscription, which is stronger in downturns, and shrink the premium single-origin line.
  • Distribution: shift marketing budget from wholesale support to the DTC channel, where margins are higher.
  • Suppliers and raw materials: renegotiate into a six-month fixed-price contract before further currency moves, and dual-source a cheaper blend.
  • Distribution/Stores: pause the third roaster and renegotiate the lease at renewal, using the weak commercial property market as leverage.
  • Staff and labour: freeze hiring, retrain two staff on the DTC packing line rather than make redundancies — avoiding redundancy costs and preserving skills for the recovery.
  • Customer service: double down on retention emails to subscribers, because they're the revenue you can most cheaply protect.
  • Financials: extend the loan term to reduce monthly payments, build a six-month cash buffer, and delay all non-essential capital spending.
  • Management: decide now, not in six months. The firms that survive downturns are usually the ones that act early and a bit too aggressively.

Notice what Bean & Brew never did: it never "beat" the economy. It read the conditions, repositioned the pieces, and waited. That's the whole job.

What a Strategist Actually Does With This

Four habits separate strategists who work with the economy from those who get flattened by it.

Read leading indicators, not headlines. GDP figures are history. Watch your own order book, your customers' payment behaviour, unemployment claims, interest rate expectations and input prices. Your business often feels a downturn weeks before the statistics confirm it.

Stress-test your cost structure. Ask the brutal question: if revenue fell 20% tomorrow, what happens? If the answer is "we're insolvent in four months," your problem isn't the economy — it's your cost structure, and that's a Costs and Financials decision you can make today.

Build optionality, not forecasts. You can't predict the cycle. You can hold cash, keep leases short, avoid over-gearing, and stay diversified across products, customers and channels. Optionality is what lets you act when conditions change instead of merely reacting.

Remember that the economy is a multiplier, not a verdict. A well-run business with a resilient product, flexible costs and a strong balance sheet will still lose money in a severe recession — but it will be buying its competitors' assets on the way out. A badly run business will blame the economy, and sometimes be right, but more often it was already fragile. Good times hide bad management; bad times expose it. That's arguably the most important sentence in this entire book.

Summary

  • The economy is the only factor that touches every other factor. It's the board the game is played on, not an opponent across the table.
  • It reaches you through specific channels: demand, input costs, the cost of money, and the tax-and-regulation layer. Name the channel to find the response.
  • It reshapes your market (size and confidence), your competitors (how brutal the fight is), your product (cyclicality and life-cycle speed), and your entire value chain from raw materials to stores.
  • It sets the price of your labour and staff, and it's the backdrop against which management quality is finally measured.
  • It flows through sales, revenue and costs into your financials — where cash flow and gearing decide who survives. Falling sales rarely kill firms; fixed costs and debt do.
  • Tax and regulations move in step with the economy, because the same governments manage both.
  • You cannot control it, so don't try. Read it early, stress-test for it, build optionality against it, and act counter-cyclically when others freeze.
  • Above all: the economy is a multiplier on the other nineteen factors, not a verdict on them. It decides how loudly your strengths and weaknesses are broadcast — and it always finds out which ones you actually had.
