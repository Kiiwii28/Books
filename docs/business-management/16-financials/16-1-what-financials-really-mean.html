16-1 What Financials Really Mean

Part of 16 Financials

Most business owners can tell you roughly how busy they've been and roughly what's in the bank. Very few can tell you, on the spot, whether they actually made money last month. That gap — between feeling like things are going well and knowing whether they are — is exactly what financials exist to close.

The definition worth keeping

Financials are the organised, recorded story of the money moving through a business — what came in, what went out, what's owed to you, what you owe, what you own, and what's left over.

Read that sentence again, because it's doing more work than it looks. Three pieces matter.

Recorded. Not remembered. Not estimated from the feel of the week. Written down, in a consistent way, so that today's numbers can be compared with last month's and next year's. Your memory of a busy Tuesday is useless as evidence; a record of it isn't.

Money. Financials don't measure effort, quality, or how much you care. They measure the one thing that can be counted exactly: money moving, or money promised. That's their strength and their blind spot in the same breath.

The story. Not a single document. "Financials" is plural because it's a set — records kept along the way, statements assembled from them, and the handful of numbers squeezed out of the whole thing. The next subsection takes each of those pieces in turn; here, just note that they're layers of one system, not competing versions of the truth.

Two jobs: a memory and a passport

Why bother with any of it? Two reasons, and they're quite different.

The first is memory. A business generates hundreds of money events a week — a card payment at the counter, a supplier invoice, a wage run, a van repair. Nobody can hold that in their head. Financials are the business's memory, kept outside anybody's skull, where it can't drift or flatter.

The second is trust, and this is where it ties back to the ground you've already covered. Remember the Market chapter's list of gaps between buyers and sellers — space, time, information, trust, quantity, risk? Financials are part of what closes the trust gap at scale. A bank lends you money because it can read your records. A supplier gives you thirty days to pay because your record says you pay. An investor hands over a fortune because the numbers are checkable. Without records, every one of those relationships has to run on personal acquaintance, which caps your business at the size of your friendship group.

There's a legal thread here too. The Company chapter made the point that a company is a legal person, separate from the people who own it. That separation has a price: the entity has to keep its own money straight, apart from its owners'. Financials are how it proves it. Limited liability and clean books are two halves of the same bargain.

The three questions financials answer

Strip away the jargon and every set of financials is trying to answer three questions.

Did we make money? Performance — over a period, usually a month or a year. This is the profit question, and it's the one everybody asks.

What do we own and owe? Position — at a moment in time. The oven, the stock, the cash in the bank on one side; the loan, the unpaid supplier invoices, the tax bill on the other.

Can we pay what's due next week? Liquidity. The most boring question and the one that kills businesses, as you're about to see.

Notice that they're not the same question, and a business can give three different answers. Profitable, but owing more than it's worth. Solid on paper, but with no cash until Friday. You need all three, which is why a single number — however big and shiny — is never enough.

Profit is not cash

Here's the idea that separates people who understand financials from people who own a business and hope.

Profit measures a period. Cash measures a moment. They're related, but they are not the same, and confusing them is one of the most common ways a thriving-looking business dies.

The saying goes that profit is an opinion and cash is a fact. That's a bit unfair to profit — it's a carefully defined opinion, built from real rules — but it captures the practical truth. You can be profitable and run out of money. Plenty of businesses have managed exactly that, often in their fastest-growing year.

Why do they diverge? Five reasons, and you've met most of them already:

  • Credit sales. A café buys £400 of bread and pays in thirty days. That's revenue the moment it's delivered — the Sales chapter's completed exchange — but no money has moved.
  • Credit purchases. The mirror image. You owe the mill for flour, so your costs are recorded and your bank balance hasn't budged yet.
  • Stock. Goods sitting in your storeroom are cash you've already spent and can't use. The Stores chapter called this "cash wearing a different coat" — and it doesn't appear as a cost until the goods are sold.
  • Capital spending. Buy an oven and you've parted with real money, but it doesn't hit profit as one lump. It's spread across the years it will be used — which is the Manufacturing chapter's depreciation, arriving in the accounts.
  • Loan repayments. Paying back the money you borrowed isn't a cost of trading at all, but it absolutely leaves your bank account.

A worked month at Priya's bakery

Watch it happen. One ordinary month:

Amount
Sales made £12,000
— of which, unpaid by cafés (30-day terms) £2,000
Ingredients £3,600
Wages £4,000
Rent, energy, other £2,800
Depreciation on the oven £300
Profit £1,300

By any normal reading, a good month. Now the bank account:

Cash actually moving Amount
Received from customers £10,000
Paid for ingredients, wages, overheads £10,400
Paid for a new mixer (capital) £1,500
Net cash movement −£1,900

Priya was £1,300 in profit and £1,900 worse off in cash. Nothing went wrong. She sold bread, her prices were right, her costs behaved — and she ended the month with £600 in the bank instead of £2,500.

Sit with the strategic implication. At £600 she can absorb a quiet week. If she'd started the month with £1,500 instead of £2,500, one broken fridge would have put her in real trouble — while her accounts showed a healthy profit. That's why "we're profitable" and "we're fine" are different sentences.

And what does she do about it? Not sell more — that would make the gap worse, since every wholesale order adds another thirty days of unpaid revenue. She chases the £2,000, staggers the mixer purchase, or negotiates better terms with her suppliers. Every one of those is a cash decision, and none of them show up as a change in profit.

Financials are a model, not a photograph

One more piece of honesty. These numbers describe reality, but they don't transcribe it.

How long will that oven last — five years or eight? That's an opinion, and it changes the depreciation charge. Is that slow-paying café a customer or a bad debt? That's a judgement. What is the stock in the storeroom actually worth now that the product has started to feel dated? The Product life cycle chapter suggests less than you paid for it.

Two businesses in identical circumstances can produce different numbers because they made different reasonable assumptions. Financials are a model built with conventions — and once you know that, you stop treating them as physics and start reading them the way you'd read a weather forecast: useful, improving, never certain.

The loop they close

Here's where financials sit in the machinery of management, and why the last chapter before this one was Management.


The four jobs from the opening chapter — planning, organizing, leading, controlling — end with controlling: measure what happened, compare it with the plan, correct course. Financials are the instrument that job is performed with. Everything else in that loop runs on opinion; this is the part that runs on evidence.

Which is why late financials are almost worthless as management. A set of accounts that arrives six months after the year ends isn't a control panel, it's a post-mortem. It tells you what happened, not what to do. The value of a number decays fast.

What financials won't tell you

Be clear-eyed about the edges, because the gaps are where strategists earn their keep.

  • They can't see most of what makes a business valuable. The skill of your bakers, your reputation, the fact that regulars trust you, the training you've sunk into your team — these are largely invisible on the page. A business can be worth far more than its numbers, and a business with decent numbers can be hollow.
  • They look backwards. Every figure is a record of something that's already happened. Your Competitors can change the world tomorrow without touching this month's accounts.
  • They don't explain themselves. A margin fell — but was it price, waste, a channel shift, or a supplier? The numbers flag the question and leave you to answer it.

But here's the balance. Almost everything you'd want to measure is soft and arguable. Cash is hard and unambiguous. And it's the hard one that decides whether you're still trading next month.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Learn to read them yourself. You don't need to prepare them, but outsourcing the understanding is like driving with a paper bag over your head.
  • Ask the three questions every month. Did we make money? What do we own and owe? Can we pay what's due? Ten minutes, one page, no accountant required.
  • Treat cash as the survival metric and profit as the performance metric. Both matter. Only one of them kills you on a Tuesday.
  • Bring the numbers to every other decision. Choosing a market sets a revenue ceiling. A product decision locks in costs. A channel decision sets your margin and your cash cycle. A hire adds a fixed cost you'll pay in a quiet month. The Sales chapter's warning about discounts — that 10% off can eat 80% of your profit — is a financial fact about a marketing habit. Strategy without numbers is opinion with a budget.
  • Watch the trend, not the snapshot. One month's profit tells you little. Six months of the same direction tells you a great deal.

Summary

  • Financials are the organised, recorded story of the money moving through a business — what came in, went out, is owed, is owned, and is left.
  • They serve as the business's memory and as its passport: records are what let banks, suppliers, investors and the tax system deal with you at scale.
  • They answer three different questions: performance (profit), position (what you own and owe) and liquidity (can you pay).
  • Profit is not cash. Profit measures a period; cash measures a moment. Credit sales, credit purchases, stock, capital spending and loan repayments all pull them apart — and a profitable business can run out of money.
  • Financials are a model built on conventions and judgements, not an exact photograph of reality.
  • They're the instrument of the controlling job from the management cycle — and their value decays fast, so late numbers manage nothing.
  • They can't see your people, your reputation or your brand, and they only look backwards. Treat them as essential and incomplete.
  • The strategist's habit: read them yourself, check them monthly, and let them sit underneath every decision you make.
