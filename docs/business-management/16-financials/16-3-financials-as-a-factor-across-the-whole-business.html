16-3 Financials as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 16 Financials

Every other chapter in this book has been about a thing your business does: buy from suppliers, transform raw materials, hire labour, make the product, store it, move it, sell it, look after the customer who bought it. Financials is different. It isn't another activity sitting alongside those — it's the room where all of them get added up, and where you find out whether the whole contraption is actually working.

Which is exactly why treating financials as "the accounting bit" is a mistake. It's the wiring that runs through every other decision you make.

The one place your decisions can be compared

A manager faces this problem constantly. Should we spend £10,000 on a second oven, a part-time baker, a refurbished shopfront, or a month of advertising?

Those are wildly different things. You can't compare "a second oven" with "a month of advertising" directly — they have different shapes, different timescales, different risks. But you can compare what each one does to your costs, your revenue and your cash. That's what financials is for.

This is the practical version of the definition from earlier in this chapter: financials is the common language the rest of the business is translated into. Every factor you've met in this book casts a financial shadow, and financials is where those shadows line up side by side:

The factor How it shows up in the numbers
Suppliers Cost of goods sold today; payment terms decide when the cash actually leaves
Raw materials Direct, variable costs — a price shock hits profit pound for pound
Labour Fixed wages you pay regardless of sales, plus variable hours that follow them
Manufacturing Big up-front cash out, depreciated over years, setting your break-even
Stores Cash you've already spent sitting still, costing ~20–25% a year to hold
Distribution Cost to serve per order, per channel — often invisible until you measure it
Sales Revenue, discounts, and the timing of when customers pay you
Customer service Cost to serve — and the retention that decides customer lifetime value
Tax and regulations Cash out, plus compliance costs baked into every process
Economy Interest rates on your borrowing, inflation on your costs, demand for your product

Read that table and a pattern appears. Almost every chapter in this book makes a decision that lands here — and almost every decision lands in a different place than you'd expect.

Three questions, three statements

Here's the thing that trips up new managers. "The numbers" isn't one number. There are three separate questions, and only one of them is about profit:

  • Did we earn? — the profit and loss account, matching revenue against costs for a period.
  • Did the money arrive? — cash flow, which is about timing.
  • What do we own and owe? — the balance sheet, a snapshot on one day.

The same decision can look completely different in each. Sell a £600 bike and profit rises today while cash may not turn up for a month. Buy a £20,000 oven and cash drops today while profit barely notices — you just get a small depreciation charge each year for a decade. Hire someone and you've added a fixed cost, which hurts most in the quiet months — the same fixed-versus-variable machinery from the Costs chapter, viewed from the finance side.


Notice the arrow coming back. Profit isn't the finish line — it's the fuel for the next round of decisions, exactly like the management loop from the opening of this book. A business that earns a profit but can't convert it into cash can't buy the next oven, and the loop stalls.

Same profit, two different businesses

This is the single most useful thing to understand about financials as a factor: two businesses with identical profits can be in completely different health.

Priya's bakery and a rival, the Corner Bakehouse, both made £30,000 last year. On the income statement, they're twins.

Priya's Bakery Corner Bakehouse
Profit £30,000 £30,000
Ovens Owned outright Second one on a five-year loan
Loan repayments £0 £9,000 a year
Stock held 5 days (~£2,000) 4 weeks (~£14,000)
Money owed by customers ~£1,500 ~£25,000 (wholesale on 60-day terms)
Cash in the bank £26,000 £4,000

Look hard at what's going on, because none of it is visible in the profit figure.

The Corner Bakehouse bought stock in bulk for a discount — a Stores decision, sensible in isolation — which tied up an extra £12,000 of cash on the shelves. It sells a big chunk wholesale on 60-day terms — a Sales and Distribution decision that brought in real revenue — which means a quarter of its annual sales are sitting in café bank accounts. It financed its second oven — a Manufacturing decision that raised its capacity — which costs £9,000 a year in cash, of which only the interest portion touches profit.

Priya, meanwhile, has a boring business and a fat bank balance. Same profit. One of these businesses can absorb a bad quarter, a broken oven or a lost customer. The other cannot.

Here's the honest caveat: the Corner Bakehouse might be growing faster, and those wholesale contracts might be worth far more than the cash they tie up. That's a real strategic bet. The point isn't that one is right and one is wrong — it's that the decision was made in Stores, Sales and Manufacturing, and only shows up in Financials. If you're watching only the profit line, you see nothing at all.

The handful of numbers worth sharing

You can measure hundreds of things. You should share about four, so that everyone from the baker to the bookkeeper is pulling on the same rope:

  • Contribution per unit — price minus variable cost. Priya's loaf: £4.00 − £1.20 = £2.80. This is the money each sale leaves towards rent, wages and profit.
  • Break-even volume — fixed costs divided by contribution. At £230 a day of fixed costs, Priya needs 82 loaves just to stand still.
  • Cash conversion cycle — days of stock + days waiting to be paid − days you take to pay suppliers. The number of days your money is out of your pocket. Priya's is short; the Corner Bakehouse's is long.
  • Return on capital — profit divided by the money tied up in the business. The number that answers the only question that ultimately matters: was this worth doing at all?

These are the numbers that make the four management jobs concrete. Planning is a budget built from contribution and break-even. Organizing is deciding who can spend what without asking. Leading is explaining the numbers so people understand why the rota is tight this month. Controlling is comparing actual against plan and correcting — the same loop as ever, pointed at a spreadsheet.

And keep one honest warning in view. Financials is a rear-view mirror: statements tell you what already happened. Used well, it's also a dashboard and an early-warning system — margin creeping down, cost per unit drifting up, customers taking longer to pay. Used badly, it's a museum of last year that nobody visits until it's too late.

Traps worth naming

Managing the profit and losing the cash. Profitable businesses run out of money all the time. Growth eats cash: more stock, more capacity, more customers who owe you money. Fast-growing, profitable and broke is a genuinely common combination.

Judging every decision by this month's profit. Training, maintenance and tooling all look like pure cost in the month you pay for them. They're investments in the loop that comes next.

Treating it as somebody else's job. If the owner doesn't read the numbers, the numbers don't steer anything. Financial literacy isn't the accountant's specialism — it's the manager's basic equipment.

Chasing revenue instead of contribution. A 10% discount costs 10% of revenue and 80% of profit. Sales figures go up, the business gets weaker. That example is in the Sales chapter, and it's a financial decision wearing a marketing costume.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Expect every decision to land here. Whatever chapter you're making a call in, ask what it does to costs, to cash, and to the balance sheet.
  • Watch contribution, not just turnover. Revenue flatters; contribution pays.
  • Time matters as much as amount. When cash comes in and when it goes out can sink a profitable business.
  • Read all three statements, not just the profit line. Two identical profits can mean two completely different futures.
  • Compare your performance to your plan, on a rhythm. That's controlling — the half of the management loop most businesses skip until year-end.
  • Understand that the numbers are the language of strategy. Financials is what lets a baker, a driver, a salesperson and a bank talk to each other.

Summary

  • Financials isn't a separate activity — it's the common language every other factor in the business is translated into, and the place where decisions can finally be compared.
  • Every chapter casts a financial shadow: suppliers and materials in costs, labour in fixed wages, manufacturing in capex and break-even, stores in tied-up cash, distribution in cost to serve, sales in revenue and payment timing.
  • Three questions, not one: did we earn (profit and loss), did the money arrive (cash flow), and what do we own and owe (the balance sheet).
  • Two businesses can post identical profits and be in completely different health. Priya's £26,000 in the bank and the Corner Bakehouse's £4,000 tell a story the profit line hides — and it was written in Stores, Sales and Manufacturing.
  • Share a small set of numbers — contribution per unit, break-even volume, the cash conversion cycle, return on capital — and use them to plan, organise, lead and control.
  • Beware the standard traps: confusing profit with cash, judging long-term investment by this month's earnings, treating finance as someone else's job, and chasing revenue while giving away contribution.
  • Treat the statements as both a rear-view mirror and a dashboard — and remember that profit isn't the finish line. It's the fuel for the next round of decisions.
