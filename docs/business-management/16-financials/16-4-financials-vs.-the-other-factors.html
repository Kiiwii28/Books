16-4 Financials vs. The Other Factors

Part of 16 Financials

Financials vs. the other factors

Every other chapter in this book has been about something a business does. You buy from suppliers, you transform raw materials, you employ labour, you make things in manufacturing, you hold them in stores, you move them through distribution, you sell them through sales, you keep the buyers with customer service, and you do it all inside a company, with staff, under management, in a market, against competitors, in an economy, paying tax and obeying regulations.

Financials does none of those things. It doesn't buy, make, move, sell or serve. It sits alongside all of them and asks one impertinent question: did any of that actually work?

That's why this comparison isn't really a contest. Financials isn't competing with distribution for the title of Most Important Factor. It's the instrument the others are measured with — the scoreboard, the referee and the common language all at once. Which makes the "vs." here genuinely interesting, because it cuts in four different directions at once.

Four ways the other factors meet financials

Look at how the other nineteen factors actually touch financials and a pattern appears. They relate to it in four different ways, and knowing which one you're dealing with saves an enormous amount of confusion.


Factors that write into financials. Suppliers, raw materials, labour, manufacturing, distribution, stores, product, sales, customer service, staff, revenue, costs. You control these, they generate the transactions, and financials is where the transactions land.

Factors that write onto financials. The economy, tax and regulations. You don't control these. They change the number that was already there — inflation flatters it, interest rates tax it, tax rules slice a piece off it, regulation decides what shape it has to be presented in.

The factor that reads it. Management. Financials is a control panel with nobody at it unless a manager shows up. This is the controlling job from the opening chapter, and its instrument is this chapter's subject.

The factors that share the language. Your company must file accounts; your competitors publish theirs. Financials is the one factor in this list that everybody in the game is forced to speak, which is why it's also the best window you'll ever get onto a rival.

Hold those four verbs — write into, write onto, read, share — and the rest of this subsection falls into place.

The factors that write into it: money you spend

These factors flow downward into your costs, and financials records the consequence, not the cause. That gap is exactly where the "vs." lives.

Suppliers. A supplier gives you a price and a promise. Financials turns them into a cost line and a payable. And here's the contrast worth noticing: financials only ever sees what somebody invoices you for. The total cost of ownership idea from the Suppliers chapter — waste, handling, storage, the cash tied up, the batch that goes wrong — mostly never gets invoiced at all, so it never appears in your accounts. Financials is systematically optimistic about cheap suppliers. That's a blind spot only the Suppliers chapter can fill.

Raw materials. Direct, variable, and brutally immediate. A 20% jump in the price of flour hits profit pound for pound, and it does so within a month. Financials records that faithfully — what it can't tell you is whether you should be reformulating the recipe, buying forward, or passing the increase on. It flags the question and leaves you to answer it.

Labour. Financials is very good at wages: payroll records, hourly rates, employer taxes, the true cost of a hire. It is completely blind to the fifty unpaid hours the owner works every week. The Labour chapter called a business that only looks profitable because of donated owner time subsidised, not profitable — and no set of accounts on earth will tell you that. Financials counts what you pay for and ignores what you donate.

Manufacturing. Here financials does something interesting and slightly deceptive. A £40,000 oven leaves your bank account in one brutal afternoon, but appears in your profit as a gentle £5,000-a-year depreciation charge for eight years. The Manufacturing chapter makes capital investment feel dramatic; the accounts make it feel mild. If you read only the profit line, you'll underestimate how much cash your capacity has been drinking.

Distribution. The cost to serve per order, per channel, per mile. Financials will record the van lease and the courier invoices — the difficulty is attributing them, which is why the Distribution chapter insists on per-channel cost to serve. Without that work, the numbers are accurate and useless: an overhead lump instead of a decision.

Stores. Stock is cash already spent and not yet earned. Financials holds it on the balance sheet at what you paid, and it will keep doing that quite happily until somebody admits it's obsolete and writes it down. The product life cycle is the thing that decides when that write-down is due; financials only records it afterwards.

Costs. Costs are the decisions; financials is the receipt. A cost is a choice somebody made — to buy, to hire, to build, to discount, to keep something too long — and financials is where the footprint of that choice shows up, months later, in a column of numbers with no names attached. The Costs chapter tells you why a number is what it is. Financials tells you what it is. You need both.

The factors that write into it: money you make

Market. The market sets the price and puts a ceiling on the revenue. Financials tells you whether your cost base can live with that price. This is the single most useful contrast in the whole book: the market decides what you may charge; financials tells you whether you can afford to. When they disagree — your costs above the market price — no amount of financial cleverness fixes it. You change the product, the cost base, or the market.

Competitors. Mostly they write into your financials indirectly, through price pressure. But the crossover here is beautiful: rivals are required to file accounts (publicly, if listed; in abbreviated form if private), which means the same discipline you run on your own business can be run on theirs. Sizing up rivals, from the Competitors chapter, is largely a financial exercise. Just remember the caveat — their accounts are annual, delayed, minimally detailed and dressed for an audience.

Product. A product decision locks in a cost structure long before a customer ever sees the thing. Financials reveals what you locked in. It cannot tell you whether the design was any good.

Sales. Sales produces revenue; financials decides whether the revenue was worth having. The 10%-discount example runs through this entire book — a 10% cut in price against an 80% cut in profit — and it is a marketing habit with a financial consequence. Sales feels the win. Financials feels the cost.

Customer service. Cost to serve sits in your accounts. Retention, reputation and goodwill largely don't — except that they show up later as revenue, which is both too late and too vague to manage. Customer lifetime value is the bridge the Customer Service chapter builds: half relationship, half arithmetic, and the half that's arithmetic is financials.

Revenue. Revenue is the number everybody quotes and the one that's most misleading alone. It's also, in a sense, financials' little brother: the top line of one statement, wearing a whole chapter's worth of nuance. Revenue is a claim; financials is what happened to it once costs and cash had their turn.

The structure, the people, and the readers

Company. A company is a separate legal person — the Company chapter's central claim — and that separation carries a price. The entity must keep its own books, apart from its owners', and file them. Limited liability and clean records are two halves of one bargain. Financials is the paperwork of a legal fiction, and the fiction is what lets strangers lend you money.

Staff. Payroll appears. Productivity per head appears, if you look for it. Skill, motivation, loyalty, the fact that Dario has been quietly holding the morning shift together for six years — none of it appears anywhere. Financials can tell you your wage bill rose 8% and not tell you whether that was a mistake or the best money you spent all year.

Management. Management is the only factor whose relationship with financials is to consume it. Everything else produces numbers; management is expected to act on them. Which is why the pairing fails in both directions. Financials without management is archaeology — a record of a year nobody read. Management without financials is opinion with a budget, and the opening chapter was blunt about where that ends.

The outside forces that write onto it

Economy. Inflation, interest rates and demand arrive in your accounts dressed as normal numbers. Financials is nominal — it records the pounds, not what the pounds were worth. A 5% revenue rise in a 6% inflation year is a decline, and the accounts will never tell you so. The Economy chapter is the lens that converts a nominal number into a real one, and without it your financials can flatter you into a bad decision.

Tax. The relationship here is unusually tight: your financials are the tax base. Profit is taxed, sales tax is charged on your invoices and collected on the government's behalf, payroll taxes sit inside your wage bill, property taxes are a line of overhead. Tax rules even dictate some of the choices in your accounts — how assets are written down, when revenue is recognised. Financials doesn't just record the tax; in part it's shaped by it.

Regulations. Regulations decide the form. Statutory accounts, filing deadlines, audit thresholds, the legal format of a balance sheet, what has to be disclosed and what may be abbreviated. They also add cost — compliance is a line of overhead that appears in no other chapter. This is the only factor in the whole book that can tell your financials what shape they're allowed to be.

Read that table as a strategist and one conclusion jumps out. Every single factor has something financials cannot see — and every single factor wants something financials can provide. That's not a flaw in financials. It's the description of a tool that's essential and incomplete, which is the most useful kind of tool there is.

Worked example: a second shop, seen through every lens

Priya is thinking about opening a second bakery across town. Watch how the factors line up — and where financials finally takes over.

  • Market: Is there demand over there? Three bakeries already trade on that street.
  • Competitors: Will they cut prices when she arrives? Almost certainly.
  • Product: Does the range travel, or does it depend on her being there?
  • Suppliers: Can her flour wholesaler deliver to a second address at the same price, or does the discount break?
  • Raw materials: Buying twice as much may earn better terms — a real gain.
  • Labour: Can she find a baker in that town, and what are the local wage rates?
  • Manufacturing: Fit-out, a second oven, refrigeration, a counter — heavy capital, heavy fixed cost.
  • Stores: Opening stock of £6,000, plus more shelving, plus rent and business rates.
  • Distribution: Deliveries in two directions, or one combined route?
  • Sales: Somebody has to sell there. Who?
  • Customer service: Service standards hold for six months and then slip, or hold forever?
  • Staff: The staff she hires will not be Dario. They'll need training and managing.
  • Management: She cannot stand in two shops at once. Something is going to be unsupervised.
  • Economy: What does the loan cost over five years, and is demand rising or falling?
  • Tax: Business rates on shop two, VAT on higher turnover, payroll taxes on the new hires.
  • Regulations: Food hygiene registration, planning permission, signage, licensing.
  • Revenue: £180,000 a year, maybe, once it's established.
  • Costs: Variable costs at 66% of revenue; fixed costs around £52,000 including rent, wages and insurance; depreciation £5,000.
  • Financials: Capital needed, cash burn in year one, payback period, return on capital.

Now let financials do its job. Capital required: fit-out and equipment £45,000, deposit and legal £5,000, opening stock £6,000, and a working-capital buffer of £8,000 for the months before the shop finds its feet. £64,000.

Year-one projection: revenue £180,000; variable costs £118,800; contribution £61,200; less fixed costs £52,000; less depreciation £5,000. Profit: £4,200.

That's a 6.5% return on £64,000 — against about 4% for leaving the money in the bank, with none of the risk, none of the work and none of the chance of losing the lot. On year one alone, the numbers say no.

By year three, though, volume might be 25% higher, contribution might reach £76,000 against fixed costs that have barely moved — and profit could be £19,000 a year on the same £64,000. 30%. Suddenly the case is strong.

That's the answer financials gives you, and notice its shape: not "yes" or "no", but the price of the risk, and the time it takes to be paid. You are being asked to accept a year and a half of thin returns in exchange for a much better long-run position, funded out of £64,000 you can't spend on anything else — the opportunity cost from the Costs chapter, made concrete.

And notice what financials could not contribute. It couldn't tell her whether she'd find a good baker, whether the rivals would start a price war, whether her recipes would appeal two miles away, whether she'd have the energy. Those are Market, Competitors, Product and Management questions. Financials tells you whether the bet is adequately paid, not whether you'll win it.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Use financials as the referee, not the player. Every other factor has an opinion. Financials is the only one that can add those opinions up in the same units and tell you what they come to.
  • Ask which direction a factor flows. Does this decision write into the numbers, or is something outside writing onto them? Cost reduction is the first kind. Inflation is the second. They need completely different responses.
  • Trust financials for the total, not for the truth. It will tell you what your suppliers cost you. It won't tell you what they're worth. Every number in your accounts is a partial view of a decision made elsewhere.
  • Watch for the four blind spots. Unpaid owner labour, unbilled supplier costs, invisible asset value, and obsolescence that hasn't been admitted yet. Those are real money, and none of them show up until somebody makes them.
  • Learn to read someone else's accounts. Competitors file. It's the only window you'll get, and it's free.
  • Deflate before you celebrate. Inflation makes growth look better than it is. Interest rates make borrowed capital look cheaper than it is. The Economy chapter is the correction.
  • Remember that tax shapes the number, not just takes from it. Where you buy, how you structure, when you recognise revenue — all of it is a financials decision with a tax consequence.
  • And close the loop. Plan the finances, organise who can spend what, lead the team through the numbers so they understand why the rota is tight, then control it — actual against plan, monthly, without fail. That's the management cycle, and financials is the instrument it's played on.

Summary

  • Financials isn't a competitor to the other factors — it's the instrument they're measured with. It doesn't buy, make, move or sell. It asks whether any of it worked.
  • Every other factor relates to financials in one of four ways: it writes into the numbers, writes onto them, reads them, or shares the same language.
  • The factors you control — suppliers, raw materials, labour, manufacturing, distribution, stores, product, sales, service, staff, revenue, costs — write into the numbers. Financials records the consequence, never the reasoning.
  • The outside forces — the economy, tax and regulations — write onto the numbers. Inflation distorts them, tax shapes and takes from them, regulation dictates their form.
  • Management is the only factor that reads financials. Financials without management is archaeology; management without financials is opinion with a budget.
  • The company and its competitors share the language: filing is the price of limited liability, and published accounts are the best window you'll get onto a rival.
  • Every factor has a blind spot financials can't fill — unpaid owner labour, unbilled supplier costs, invisible goodwill, unadmitted obsolescence, and everything that happens next — and every factor wants something financials can provide.
  • The strategist's habit: let financials referee, but never let it decide alone. It tells you the price of the risk and the time it takes to be paid. It does not tell you whether you'll win.
