16-2 Types of Financial Statements and Records

Part of 16 Financials

Financials gets talked about as if it were one thing — "we need to sort out the financials." In practice it's a small family of documents, and each one answers a different question. Mix them up and you'll draw the wrong conclusion from perfectly accurate numbers. Keep them apart and you'll have something close to X-ray vision over your own business.

Here's the family: three main statements, each looking at the business from a different angle, backed by a layer of records that feed them. That's the distinction the title of this subsection is making, and it's worth holding on to: the statements are the summary, the records are the evidence.

The three statements, and the questions they answer

The profit and loss statement — also called the income statement, or just the P&L — answers: over this period, did we earn anything? It's a period statement: it covers a stretch of time, a month or a quarter or a year. Revenue at the top, costs down the page, and what's left at the bottom is profit.

The balance sheet — sometimes called the statement of financial position — answers: where do we stand right now? It's a moment statement: a snapshot of one single day, usually the last day of the period. It lists what the business owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and the difference (equity).

The cash flow statement answers: did the money actually move? Another period statement, tracking cash in and cash out, split into operating (day-to-day trading), investing (buying and selling long-term assets) and financing (borrowing, repaying, paying owners).


Notice how the arrows run. The records feed the P&L and the cash flow statement; both of those then feed into the balance sheet. That's why the balance sheet is often described as the bridge between two profit and loss statements — last year's closing snapshot, plus this year's profit, adjusted for what the owners took out, equals this year's closing snapshot.

And notice what each one is for. The P&L tells you whether the business is worth running. The cash flow statement tells you whether you'll make payroll on Friday. The balance sheet tells you whether you could survive a bad quarter. Three genuinely different questions — and a business can look wonderful on one and be dead on another.

Profit is not cash, and this is the whole point

This is the single most important thing in this subsection, and it's where most beginners get caught. Profit is a calculation. Cash is a fact.

Watch it happen at Priya's bakery. In one month she brings in £12,000 of sales. Ingredients cost £3,600, wages £4,200, rent £800, energy £400. The P&L looks like this:

Priya's month £
Revenue 12,000
Ingredients (3,600)
Wages (4,200)
Rent (800)
Energy (400)
Profit 3,000

£3,000 of profit. A good month. Now the same month on the cash flow statement. Two things happened that the P&L barely notices: a wholesale customer who owes her £2,000 hasn't paid yet, and she spent £4,000 cash on a new oven.

Cash movement £
Cash collected from customers 10,000
Paid to suppliers and staff (9,000)
Bought an oven (4,000)
Net cash movement (3,000)

Profit of £3,000, and cash down by £3,000. Both numbers are correct. Neither is a mistake.

Now look at what the balance sheet says, because this is where it earns its keep. Her assets moved like this: cash down £3,000, the new oven up £4,000, and money owed by customers (a debtor) up £2,000. Net movement in assets: up £3,000. And equity — the owners' stake — is up £3,000, because profit adds to it. Assets up £3,000, equity up £3,000. It balances.

That's the test that makes the balance sheet such a useful discipline: assets always equal liabilities plus equity. If yours don't, you've either made an arithmetic mistake or somebody is hiding something. It's the cheapest audit in existence, and you can run it yourself.

Read the three statements together and the story is complete. Priya is profitable and temporarily short of cash, because she invested in equipment and her customers owe her money. The Customer Service and Sales chapters would call that a credit-terms problem. The Financials chapter calls it working capital. And this is precisely the trap that kills growing, profitable businesses — the P&L says congratulations while the bank balance says goodbye.

The notes, and the fourth statement

There's usually a fourth statement — the statement of changes in equity — tracking what the owners put in and took out. It's genuinely useful, but it's the quiet one, and you can live without it for years.

More important than the fourth statement are the notes. A set of accounts without notes is a set of numbers with no context, and the notes are where the judgements hide: how stock was valued, over how many years the oven is being written down as depreciation, which debts are considered doubtful, what the lease commitments are.

Two bakeries can produce identical-looking accounts and be in completely different health, because one values its stock optimistically and the other conservatively. When you read anyone's accounts — a rival's, or your own — read the notes. The Competitors chapter's advice about sizing up rivals works far better when you know what their numbers actually mean.

The records underneath

Statements are built, not found. Underneath them sits a layer of records, and they run roughly from smallest to largest.

Source documents are the atom of it all: an invoice you issued, an invoice a supplier sent you, a receipt, a till roll, a bank statement, a signed delivery note. Every number in every statement traces back to one of these. No source documents, no reliable numbers.

Books of account — the ledgers — are where source documents get sorted into categories. A sales ledger tracks what customers owe you, which is the record that tells you whether your credit terms are working. A purchase ledger tracks what you owe suppliers, which is the Supplier chapter's payment terms measured rather than assumed. The nominal ledger collects everything else: wages, rent, energy, repairs.

The trial balance is the checkpoint. It lists every account and its balance, and it should total to zero on both sides. It's the moment you catch errors before they reach the statements.

Subsidiary records back up specific parts of the business, and each one serves another chapter of this book. A fixed asset register lists the machines, vehicles and equipment — the Manufacturing chapter's capital, recorded and depreciated. Inventory records tell you what's in the store, the subject of the Stores chapter. Payroll records hold the wages, tax and contributions, connecting to Labour and Tax. An aged debtors and creditors list shows who owes you and how late, and who you owe and when it's due.

Reconciliations tie your records to reality. A bank reconciliation checks your cash records against the bank statement. A stock count checks your inventory records against the shelves. Both exist to catch the gap between what you think happened and what did.

Finally, there's a second family of records that look forward rather than back: budgets, forecasts and cash flow projections. Same numbers, opposite direction. Budgeting is the planning job from the opening of this book; the actual accounts are the controlling job. Management only works when both exist and meet in the middle.

Statutory accounts versus management accounts

One more distinction, and it's about audience as much as content.

Management accounts are for you. Prepared monthly, internally, in whatever form is useful, often unaudited, and usually finished within a couple of weeks of the month ending. They exist to be acted on.

Statutory accounts are for the outside world. Annual, prepared to a fixed legal format, and — if you're a limited company — filed with a government registry, which is the Company chapter's separate legal existence wearing a suit. Public companies must publish theirs; private ones file abbreviated versions. Larger firms need an audit. Tax authorities build their assessment on them, which is the Tax chapter arriving at the door.

The practical mistake here is treating the statutory accounts as the business's scoreboard. By the time they're filed, the year is long gone and there's nothing left to act on. Management accounts are for steering; statutory accounts are for the record, the shareholders and the tax office.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Match the statement to the question. Is this about earning, surviving or standing? P&L, cash flow, balance sheet. Asking the wrong document gets you a confident wrong answer.
  • Watch profit and cash separately, always. A healthy profit and an unhealthy bank balance is the classic signature of a growing business in trouble. Read the Financials at Work subsection with this pair in mind.
  • Lean on the balance sheet test. Assets equal liabilities plus equity. It's free, instant, and catches errors and evasions alike.
  • Read the notes. Numbers without the assumptions behind them are marketing, not information.
  • Know which records your decisions depend on. Credit terms need an aged debtors list. Stock decisions need inventory records. Equipment plans need a fixed asset register. If a record doesn't exist, you're guessing.
  • Run management accounts monthly and read them before the next month starts. The value of a number is inversely proportional to its age.
  • Remember that records are what makes the numbers trustworthy. Every figure in every statement traces back to a document somebody filed. Sloppy records don't just annoy your accountant — they make every decision downstream a gamble, which is the opposite of what the Management chapter's controlling job is for.

Summary

  • Financials is a family of documents, not one: statements that summarise, and records that provide the evidence.
  • The three main statements each answer a different question. The profit and loss statement (a period) asks did we earn? The balance sheet (a single moment) asks where do we stand? The cash flow statement (a period) asks did cash actually move?
  • Profit is a calculation; cash is a fact. A profitable month can leave you with less cash than you started — Priya's £3,000 profit alongside a £3,000 cash fall is exactly how it works.
  • The balance sheet obeys a rule you can check yourself: assets equal liabilities plus equity. If it doesn't balance, something is wrong.
  • The notes carry the assumptions — stock valuation, depreciation, doubtful debts — and two similar-looking businesses can be in very different health because of them.
  • Underneath sit the records: source documents, ledgers (sales, purchase, nominal), the trial balance, subsidiary registers for assets, inventory, payroll and debts, and reconciliations against the bank and the shelves.
  • Budgets and forecasts are records pointing forward; accounts point backward. Planning and controlling need both.
  • Management accounts are for steering the business monthly; statutory accounts are the annual, legally formatted record for the registry, the owners and the tax office.
  • The strategist's habit: pick the right document for the question, watch cash as closely as profit, and make sure the records underneath are good enough to trust.
