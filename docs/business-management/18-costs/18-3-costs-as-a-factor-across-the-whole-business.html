18-3 Costs as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 18 Costs

Costs have an image problem. Revenue is exciting, profit is the scoreboard, and costs are the boring line in the middle that everyone wants to shrink and nobody wants to talk about. That reputation is undeserved, because costs are where strategy actually gets decided.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: you don't get to choose what you charge. The market does — buyers, rivals, substitutes, whatever the customer's next-best option happens to be. Remember the two sandwich shops from the opening of this book: same street, same footfall, same prices, same wholesaler, and one owner keeps $170 a day while the other keeps $30. Nothing about their revenue explains the gap. Their costs do.

That's why costs deserve to be treated not as a list, but as a factor running through the entire business. Every decision you've met in this book — which supplier, how many staff, whether to buy the second oven, where to put the shop, which channel to sell through, how fast to promise delivery — eventually arrives on your cost sheet. Nothing escapes it. So let's pull back and see how it all connects.

Costs are the other half of every decision

Start with the loop you've seen throughout this book, and notice what feeds it.


Read the two arrows arriving at margin. One is the price, which the Market and Competitors chapters showed you rarely control. The other is cost per unit, which is largely yours. In a competitive market, cost is the lever you can actually pull — which is why the Types of Competition chapter kept pointing out that commodity producers compete almost entirely on efficiency.

That's not a small point. It means cost isn't an accounting chore sitting at the end of the year. It's the half of the profit equation you own.

Two questions about every cost: how much, and how does it behave?

You've already met the fixed-and-variable split in the Types of Costs section. What matters here is what that split does strategically.

Variable costs rise and fall with what you sell. Flour, packaging, hourly labour, delivery fuel. They're forgiving: sell nothing, and they mostly disappear.

Fixed costs don't move. Rent, salaried managers, machine leases, insurance, software subscriptions. They're a bet on volume — and the bet cuts both ways. Run a lot of volume and each unit carries only a sliver of them, which is why scale is such a powerful cost lever. Run too little and every unit carries a crushing share.

This is operating leverage, and it's the single most useful idea in this subsection. A business with high fixed costs and low variable costs makes beautiful money above its break-even point — and bleeds catastrophically below it. A business with mostly variable costs survives a quiet quarter but never gets rich on scale.

You've met this trade disguised in several places already: the second oven in the Manufacturing chapter, the shop lease in Stores, the automated production line in Types of Manufacturing, the permanent-versus-casual ladder in Labour. Every one of them swaps flexibility for a lower cost per unit at volume.

And it's why the product life cycle matters so much to costs. Investing in fixed capacity makes brilliant sense for a product in growth and terrifying sense for one in decline. The kit doesn't care which stage you're in.

What actually drives your costs

If costs were just numbers, you could only stare at them. But they have causes, and every cause is a lever. The main ones:

  • Scale. Spreading fixed costs over more units, plus better terms from suppliers as your volumes grow. Straight from the Suppliers chapter — volume is your strongest negotiating chip.
  • Utilisation. An oven running half-full, a van making one delivery, a shop assistant standing idle at 2pm. Underused capacity costs the same as full capacity and earns nothing.
  • Learning and design. People get faster at repeated work, and — more powerfully — most of a product's cost is locked in by how it was designed. The Product chapter's warning that product decisions lock in costs belongs right here.
  • Technology. Better tools turn variable labour hours into fixed investment. Whether that's good depends entirely on your volume.
  • Input prices. Flour, fuel, energy, wages — set by markets you don't control, as the Raw Materials and Economy chapters make clear.
  • Waste. Spillage, rework, spoilage, downtime, mistakes. Money already spent, generating no revenue. It's the cheapest saving in any business, because it needs no negotiation and no extra customers.

Worked example: one decision, six cost lines

Priya is offered a contract to supply 200 loaves a week to a café chain at £2.60 each. Revenue is easy: £520 a week.

Now watch where the costs actually land.

Cost line Type Per week
Extra ingredients Variable, direct £240
Extra baker hours (earlier shift) Variable, direct labour £90
Extra oven energy Variable-ish £20
Packaging and trays Variable £20
Delivery: van hours and fuel Variable, distribution £55
Invoicing, card fees, credit chasing Indirect £15
Total £440

The contract contributes £80 a week. Notice what happens if Priya only counts what most people count — the flour. She'd have told herself this was a £280-a-week contract. The difference between £280 and £80 lives entirely in cost lines that never appear on the ingredients invoice: labour, energy, packaging, Distribution, and the admin that Sales quietly generates.

That's the whole point of treating costs as a factor across the business. The visible cost is rarely the whole cost. The Suppliers chapter's total cost of ownership idea, and the cost to serve idea from Customer Service, are the same lesson wearing different hats.

There's a cash dimension too. To serve this contract Priya needs a bigger flour buffer — say £600 of stock sitting in the back room — and the café chain will pay her on 30-day terms. So she funds a month of £440 weeks before a penny comes back. That's not a cost on the profit statement; it's a cash squeeze that can still sink her. Costs are a Financials question as much as a profit question.

The cutting trap

When money gets tight, the instinct is to cut costs. It's a good instinct pointed in a dangerous direction, because not all costs are equal.

Waste — the loaf that gets binned, the hour lost to rework, the insurance you're paying on a machine you sold — is pure loss. Cutting it is unambiguously good.

But plenty of costs are actually investments in disguise: staff training, machine maintenance, quality ingredients, marketing, decent wages. Cut these and the savings arrive this month, while the damage arrives over the next year — higher turnover, more breakdowns, worse reviews, fewer customers. You saw that spiral in the management chapter: cuts to training raise turnover, turnover raises mistakes, mistakes lose customers, lost customers force more cuts.

The test to apply to any cost line is simple: does this buy something the customer is paying for? If yes, it's an investment and you should be asking whether it earns its keep, not whether it can be trimmed. If no, cut it today.

Cost as a competitive weapon — and a trap

A durable cost advantage is one of the best assets a business can have, because it gives you choices a rival doesn't have: charge less and win on price, or charge the same and bank a fatter margin. That fatter margin funds the reinvestment arrow in the diagram — better equipment, better people, better terms — and the advantage compounds exactly as the management loop described.

But two warnings.

An advantage you can't defend isn't an advantage. If your only edge is buying more cheaply this month, a rival can match it next month. Real cost advantage comes from hard-to-copy things: scale, a better-designed product, a smarter process, a location, a relationship.

Cost-cutting races hurt everyone. When rivals in an oligopoly all cut to win share, prices fall faster than costs can, and the whole industry's margins get shredded — the price war from the Types of Competition chapter. Being the low-cost producer is a strategy. Being one of five firms all trying the same trick is usually just a way to work harder for less.

The forces outside your four walls

Some of your biggest cost movements aren't decisions at all. They're weather.

  • The economy. Inflation lifts input prices and wage expectations; interest rates decide what that new oven or shop lease really costs you over five years. High-fixed-cost businesses feel this hardest, because their commitments are locked in while prices move.
  • Tax. Payroll taxes, sales taxes, property taxes and duties all sit on your cost base. Two employees on the same salary can cost very different amounts once the full picture is counted — the Tax chapter's point about the true cost of a hire.
  • Regulations. Compliance, safety, labelling, environmental rules — each one adds cost, and each one raises the bar for newcomers, which quietly protects whoever's already in.
  • Suppliers and raw materials. A harvest, a shipping route, a currency swing. The Economy chapter explains the ripples; your job is planning for them rather than being surprised by them.

You can't control these. You can choose how exposed to them you are — which is a cost-structure decision, made in advance.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Know your structure, not just your total. Fixed versus variable, direct versus indirect, cost per unit and break-even volume. Those four numbers tell you how hard a quiet month hits and how much volume you need to be safe.
  • Follow the cost of a decision all the way through. Every choice has cost consequences in several places — labour, energy, packaging, delivery, admin, stock, cash. Count them before you commit, not after.
  • Manage by driver, not by total. Totals tell you what happened. Utilisation, waste, yield, scale and lead times tell you why, and they're the things you can actually change.
  • Separate waste from investment. Cut waste ruthlessly. Test every other cost against whether the customer is paying for it.
  • Remember costs are cash. Money goes out early and comes back late. Growth is the time when a profitable cost structure can still kill you.
  • Treat it as a control loop. Plan the cost base, organise the resources that generate it, lead the people who spend it, then check it on a rhythm — weekly for materials and labour, monthly for overheads. Budgets set in January and unopened until December aren't control; they're archaeology.
  • And keep your eye on the point of it all. Costs aren't the enemy. Unnecessary costs are. The goal was never a cheap business — it was a profitable one, and profit is what's left when the market has set your price and your costs have done their worst.

Summary

  • Profit is revenue minus costs, and the market sets the revenue. Costs are the half of the equation you actually control — which is why they're strategic, not administrative.
  • Costs have two dimensions: how much, and how they behave. Fixed costs are a bet on volume; variable costs are flexibility at the price of a higher cost per unit. That trade-off — operating leverage — is why scale makes money above break-even and bleeds below it.
  • Costs are driven by scale, utilisation, learning, design, technology, input prices and waste. Each driver is a lever; waste is the cheapest one to pull.
  • Every decision carries costs in several places at once. The visible cost — flour, components — is rarely the whole cost. Count labour, energy, packaging, distribution, admin, stock and cash too.
  • Not all costs are equal. Waste should go; training, maintenance and quality are investments that pay back over time. Cutting them saves money this month and costs more next year.
  • A defensible cost advantage is a genuine competitive weapon that compounds through reinvestment — but cost-cutting races between rivals destroy everyone's margins.
  • Outside forces — the economy, tax, regulation, input markets — move your costs whether you plan for them or not. What you control is how exposed your cost structure leaves you.
  • The strategist's habit: know your structure and break-even, follow each decision's full cost, manage by driver, cut waste not capability, watch the cash, and check the numbers on a rhythm.
