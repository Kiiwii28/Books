18-4 Costs vs. The Other Factors

Part of 18 Costs

Costs are strange company among the business factors. Almost every other factor on the list is something you acquire — a market, a product, a store, a member of staff. Cost is what every one of those decisions leaves behind. It is the receipt. Which means you can't really understand cost by looking at cost alone; you understand it by putting it next to everything else and asking the only question that matters: is this worth what it takes?

That's what this subsection does. We'll walk the full list of factors and, for each one, look at the two-way relationship — how that factor drives costs, and how your cost position changes what you can do with it. Hold one idea in your head the whole way through: every factor is a cost decision wearing a costume.

The squeeze you're always standing in

Before the factor-by-factor tour, here's the mental model. Your operating factors push costs up. The market and your competitors set the price you can charge. Profit lives in the gap.


If your floor rises above the ceiling, you don't have a business — you have a hobby with invoices. Everything below is really about managing that gap.

Costs vs. Company

Start with the container. The form a company takes is itself a cost decision before a single product exists. A sole trader has almost no compliance overhead; a limited company adds accounting, filing, audit and insurance costs, but gains limited liability and cheaper access to capital. A group with five subsidiaries carries administration costs that a single trading company never sees.

Company size matters just as much. Bigger companies spread fixed costs — rent, software licences, a finance team, a warehouse — across more units, so cost per unit falls. That's an economy of scale, and it's one of the most reliable sources of competitive advantage there is. But it isn't free: past a certain size, coordination costs, management layers and bureaucracy start pushing unit costs back up. This is why "just get bigger" is not a strategy. Remember the Foundations chapter's point that good management makes or breaks a business — the difference between scale that helps and scale that bloats is management, not ambition.

Costs vs. Labour, Staff and Management

For most businesses selling anything other than pure software, people are the largest controllable cost line.

Labour is where the crudest mistake in business gets made: assuming the cheapest worker is the cheapest labour. Labour cost is not a wage; it's a cost per unit of output. A skilled machinist at £25 an hour who produces 30 good parts costs you 83p a part. An untrained worker at £12 an hour producing 8 parts, half of which get scrapped, costs you £3 a part. Add recruitment, training, sick pay, employer taxes, and the turnover cost of hiring again in four months, and the "cheap" option is often the expensive one. The genuine trade-off is labour versus machinery: replacing people with equipment converts a variable cost into a fixed cost, which lowers cost per unit at high volume and destroys you at low volume.

Staff — the salaried, ongoing layer of the business — behaves differently again. Staff costs are largely fixed and largely invisible to the person making the decision to hire. The tell-tale metric is revenue per employee: how much output each person's cost is buying. If that number drifts down for two years while headcount grows, you have a cost problem hiding inside a growth story.

Management is the meta-factor. Managers don't just incur costs; they decide them. A budget is a management document. A make-or-buy call is a management call. A decision to accept a 2% defect rate is a decision to carry the cost of rework forever. Poor management doesn't show up on the P&L as a line called "bad management" — it shows up as overtime, waste, rework, refunds, and customers who leave. Good management, by contrast, is the only factor that can reduce several cost lines at the same time.

Costs vs. Suppliers, Raw Materials and Manufacturing

This trio is the physical cost base.

Suppliers look like pure cost — their invoice is your cost. But the purchase price is only part of the story. Total cost of ownership includes delivery reliability, defect rates, lead times, and how much stock you have to hold because you can't trust them. A supplier 6% cheaper who misses one delivery a quarter may cost you far more in expedited shipping and lost sales. Payment terms are a separate lever again: 60 days to pay doesn't lower your cost, it lowers your cash requirement — a distinction we'll return to under Financials, and one that trips up a surprising number of otherwise competent owners. Meanwhile, a single-source supplier is cheap and fragile; dual-sourcing costs a little more and buys resilience.

Raw materials are the most volatile factor on the list. Commodity prices move for reasons entirely outside your control, and a 30% rise in your main input can wipe out a thin margin in one quarter. Your weapons here are substitution, buying forward, careful specification, and yield. That last one is underrated: cheap material that generates 12% scrap is more expensive per finished unit than good material with 3% scrap. Buying in bulk lowers unit price and raises storage cost and cash tied up — a cost you've moved, not removed.

Manufacturing is where all of this gets converted into unit economics. Cost per unit falls as volume rises, because setup time, machine depreciation and factory rent get spread across more items. Run a plant at 60% capacity and your unit costs are dreadful; run it at 95% and they're excellent — until a breakdown stops everything and you start paying overtime at a premium. The make-versus-buy question lives here too, and it's never just arithmetic: making it yourself gives you control and absorbs fixed costs; buying it in gives you flexibility and someone else's fixed costs to worry about.

Costs vs. Product, Distribution and Stores

Now the offer and the route to the buyer.

Product is where costs are designed in. Every feature, every material choice, every packaging flourish is a cost committed months or years before the customer ever sees it. Two products can look nearly identical on the shelf and differ by 40% in cost, purely through design choices. This is why value engineering — stripping cost without stripping what the customer actually values — is one of the highest-leverage skills in business. The Product Life Cycle chapter adds a timing dimension: unit costs are highest at introduction (low volumes absorbing fixed costs), fall through growth, and then in maturity the price starts eroding faster than costs fall. Maturity is when a comfortable business quietly becomes an unprofitable one.

Distribution is a cost-structure choice disguised as a logistics question. Sell direct and you take on warehouses, delivery vans, drivers and a website — high fixed costs, but you keep the whole margin and own the customer. Sell through wholesalers and retailers and you swap fixed costs for a big chunk of margin given away on every unit. Neither is right; they suit different volumes. Below a certain volume, direct distribution's fixed costs will crush you; above it, third-party margins become an expensive habit.

Stores are the purest fixed-cost commitment in retail. Rent is due whether anyone walks in or not, which means a store's whole economics come down to footfall per pound of rent — or in the online equivalent, sales per pound of fulfilment cost. Two shops can pay identical rent and one is a goldmine and the other a slow bleed, based entirely on what walks through the door. Fixed costs are wonderful when volumes are high and merciless when they aren't, which is why downturns kill retailers faster than almost anyone else.

Costs vs. Market, Competitors, Sales and Customer Service

Here's the demand side, where your costs meet reality.

Market sets the ceiling. In a price-sensitive market, a high-cost operator simply cannot compete — buyers will take the cheap option every time. In a market that values quality, speed or brand, a higher cost base is survivable because the price ceiling is higher too. The size of the market matters for a subtler reason: a small market may never give you enough volume to absorb your fixed costs, no matter how good you are. Entering a market is a cost decision before it's a revenue decision.

Competitors turn cost from a number into a relative number. You don't need low costs; you need costs that let you survive at the price your rivals set. A price war is a contest of cost positions — the firm with the lowest cost per unit can hold a lower price for longer, and eventually the higher-cost firm runs out of cash. This is why cost advantage is one of the few genuinely durable moats: brand can fade, features can be copied, but a structural cost advantage is very hard to dislodge. Rivals also affect your costs directly, by bidding up the wages and materials you both need.

Sales is where cost has a per-customer flavour. Customer acquisition cost (CAC) — everything you spend to win one customer — must be covered by what that customer is worth over their life. A sales team on salary is a fixed cost; a commission-only team is variable. Both are legitimate, but a business that grows its headcount of salaried sellers before proving the CAC-to-value maths is buying revenue at a loss and calling it growth.

Customer Service is the classic "cost centre" that isn't. On the surface it's pure expense: staff, systems, refunds. Underneath, it's the thing that stops the customer leaving, and acquiring a replacement customer usually costs several times more than keeping the one you have. The strategic move is not to cut service costs but to find their root cause: a great deal of service cost is really the cost of a product defect, a confusing instruction, or a broken process. Fix those and service costs fall while revenue rises.

Costs vs. Financials and Revenue

Financials are the scoreboard that reveals cost at all. Without accounting you don't know your costs — you feel them. Financial statements turn the physical reality of wages, rent and materials into numbers you can compare, trend and act on. Two cautions. First, depreciation and accruals spread a cost across time, so profit and cash tell different stories. Second, allocated overhead can mislead: a product shown as "profitable" after overhead allocation may still be consuming cash. Also note that a cost incurred is not a cost paid: the timing of payments is a cash question, not a profit question, and confusing the two is one of the most common ways profitable businesses go bust.

Revenue is the other half of every cost decision. Revenue without reference to cost is a vanity number. The joining concept is contribution margin: price minus the variable cost of delivering one more unit. That single figure tells you whether extra volume helps or hurts, and how much you must sell before fixed costs are covered. A business can grow revenue, celebrate, and go bankrupt — because it bought each sale for more than it earned. Costs are finite; there's a floor to how much you can cut. Revenue has no such ceiling. That asymmetry is the reason a good strategist treats cost cutting as damage control and growth as the mission.

Costs vs. Economy, Tax and Regulations

Three external forces that reshape your cost base without asking permission.

Economy. Inflation raises input costs, often faster than you can raise prices. Interest rates change the cost of borrowing and therefore whether that new machine or store is worth financing. Exchange rates change the cost of anything you import. Recessions hit the revenue side and the cost side simultaneously — volumes fall, so your fixed costs get spread over fewer units and unit costs rise just as buyers become more price-sensitive. That double squeeze is why timing matters so much when you commit to fixed costs.

Tax. Tax is a cost, but a strange one: it's a share of your success, not a hurdle you must clear before you can trade. That matters strategically. Tax-deductible business costs are effectively cheaper than they appear, because part of the expense is shared with the government — which is one reason decisions made on headline price alone get it wrong. Watch the distinction between taxes on profit, which only bite when you're winning, and taxes on employment or consumption, which are real operating costs you build into your price from day one.

Regulations. Compliance is a cost with no revenue attached — and its cost scales with size, which means it hurts small firms more per pound of revenue. Oddly, that's a competitive advantage for established players: regulation is a wall that keeps newcomers out. But regulation also raises genuine risks, since the cost of getting compliance wrong (fines, shutdowns, lawsuits) dwarfs the cost of getting it right. Treat compliance spend as insurance with a legally mandated premium.

A worked example: Ember & Oak Coffee

Picture a small roastery selling bags at £22 each, mostly through its own shop and website. Incremental cost per retail bag — beans, bag, label, power, packing time — is about £6. Nice contribution margin.

A local café chain offers to take 400 bags a month on a wholesale basis at £9 a bag. New incremental cost per bag for the wholesale format: plainer packaging, larger bags, bigger roast batches, a bit less labour per kilo — call it £6.50. Contribution: £2.50 a bag, or £1,000 a month. Not exciting.

Now put it next to the other factors:

  • Manufacturing and Stores: the roaster already sits half-idle on weekday mornings, and the rent is fixed. Those 400 bags use capacity that currently earns nothing. The relevant comparison isn't £2.50 versus £16 — it's £2.50 versus £0.
  • Labour: the wholesale run adds packing hours, pushing one part-timer from 16 to 22 hours a week — cheap, because no new hire or training is needed yet.
  • Competitors: the café is also quoting a roaster across town at £8.50. Undercutting to win would shave contribution to £2, and if the rival pushes to £8, Ember & Oak still survives where the rival — newer, higher rent, less efficient — does not.
  • Raw materials and Economy: green bean prices jump 30%. Retail price can rise £1 with a shrug. The wholesale contract is fixed at £9 for twelve months — contribution drops to well under £1 a bag. Suddenly 400 bags of low-margin work is crowding out the higher-margin retail roast.
  • Customer Service and Sales: wholesale accounts complain less and reorder without marketing spend, so CAC is near zero. But losing the account later costs a fifth of revenue in one email.
  • Financials: the £1,000 a month is profitable in the accounts but arrives 45 days after invoicing, while the beans are paid for on delivery. Profitable and cash-hungry at the same time.

That's the whole discipline in one decision. Not "is £2.50 enough?", but "enough compared to what, in light of everything else, and what happens if the world moves?"

Summary

  • Cost is the receipt for every other factor. To judge a cost properly, you have to judge it against what it buys.
  • Company form and size decide your fixed-cost base and how thinly you can spread it.
  • Labour, Staff and Management are usually the biggest controllable cost — and cheap labour is often the expensive option. Management doesn't just incur costs, it decides them.
  • Suppliers, Raw Materials and Manufacturing are the physical cost base: purchase price is only part of total cost, and yield, reliability and utilisation matter as much as price.
  • Product, Distribution and Stores are where cost is designed in, channels are chosen, and fixed costs are committed. Design and channel decisions are cost decisions in disguise.
  • Market, Competitors, Sales and Customer Service set the ceiling your costs must fit under. Cost matters relative to rivals, and service is revenue protection dressed as a cost centre.
  • Financials and Revenue are the measurement layer. Contribution margin is the bridge; remember that a cost incurred is not a cost paid, and that revenue has no ceiling while cost cutting does.
  • Economy, Tax and Regulations reshape your cost base from outside — through inflation, interest rates, deductibility, and compliance walls that hurt small firms most.

The strategic takeaway: cost is the factor you control most directly and the one most easily mismanaged, because the cuts that look best on this month's P&L are often the ones that quietly drain next year's revenue. Cut what doesn't create value. Protect what does. And never lose sight of the gap between the floor and the ceiling — that gap is the business.
