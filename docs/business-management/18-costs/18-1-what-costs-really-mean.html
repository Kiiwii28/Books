18-1 What Costs Really Mean

Part of 18 Costs

Ask most business owners what something costs them and you'll get a number off the top of their head. "Flour's about a pound twenty a loaf." That number came off an invoice, and it's not wrong — it's just roughly a third of the answer.

Costs are the least glamorous subject in this book and the one that decides whether you have a business at all. Every chapter so far has quietly been spending money: suppliers, raw materials, labour, manufacturing, stores, distribution, sales, service. This chapter is where all that spending gets named, added up, and — if you're any good at this — reduced. But before we can sort costs into types, we need to be precise about what a cost actually is, because the everyday meaning is narrower than the real one.

The definition worth keeping

A cost is the value of the resources a business gives up in order to get something.

Three phrases in that sentence are carrying the weight.

Value of the resources. Costs are measured in money, but they aren't always paid in money. The fifty unpaid hours Priya works every week have a cost — the Labour chapter was blunt about this. So does the stock that spoiled in the back room, and so does the wear on an oven that nobody invoices you for.

Gives up. Cost is the sacrifice side of every decision. You hand over something — cash, stock, time, a machine's working life — and you get something back. Cost is what it took.

In order to get something. This is the phrase people skip. A cost doesn't exist on its own; it exists against a purpose. Spending £40 on a second-hand display case is a cost if it sells more buns and pure waste if it doesn't. The money looks identical in both cases.

Which leads to the reframe that runs through this whole chapter: every cost is a decision made visible. A cost line isn't an abstract number that happens to you. It's the footprint of a choice somebody made — to buy, to hire, to build, to discount, to keep something too long. When you look at your costs, you're not auditing arithmetic. You're auditing decisions.

Price is where cost starts, not where it ends

Here's the muddle that catches nearly everyone. The price you pay a supplier is not the cost of what you bought. It's the first instalment of it.

The Suppliers chapter made this point with total cost of ownership, and it's worth one line here because it's the same idea wearing different clothes. A cheap flour has a price of £17 a sack. Its cost includes what you pay for delivery, what you lose to damp in the back room, the cash tied up in having bought a month's worth at once, and the batch that turns out wrong and has to be binned.

So when someone says "our costs went up", always ask which one they mean:

  • the price you were quoted,
  • the spend you actually handed over,
  • or the cost you truly incurred to get the thing and have it ready to use.

They're three different numbers, and only the middle one shows up on a receipt.

A cost is not the same as cash leaving the building

This is where cost and cash part company, and it trips up beginners constantly.

Timing. You pay for a machine today and use it for ten years. The cash left in one go, but the cost arrives slowly, in small slices, as the machine wears out. Accountants call those slices depreciation, and the Financials chapter will show you why it matters. The same is true in reverse: buy flour now on 30-day terms and you've incurred the cost today but parted with no cash at all.

Non-cash costs. The owner's unpaid labour. A spoiled pallet nobody invoiced you for. The value of a van quietly losing worth every month. Real costs, no bank transfer.

That's why a business can be profitable on paper and out of cash, or flush with cash and losing money. Both happen, both are dangerous, and both are invisible if you only ever look at one column. Costs and cash are two views of the same business, and you need both.

Cost per unit: the number decisions are actually made with

Nobody prices "total costs". You price a loaf, a haircut, an hour of advice. So the cost that matters is almost always cost per unit.

And here's the twist that makes costs so interesting: cost per unit depends on how many units you make. Some costs rise every time you bake another loaf — flour, bags, gas. Others sit there regardless: the rent, the insurance, Dario's wage whether the ovens run or not. Spread those fixed costs over 100 loaves and they're small; spread them over 30 and they're crushing. Same recipe, same suppliers, wildly different cost per loaf — purely because of volume.

Each arrow widens the number and narrows what you can do with it — until, at the end, you have the only figure that tells you whether a sale is worth having.

Two costs that point in opposite directions in time

Sunk costs are behind you. Money already spent, already gone, unrecoverable. The oven you bought last year. The stock that didn't sell. Here's the discipline: sunk costs should have no vote in what you do next. The only question that matters is whether what's ahead of you earns more than what's ahead of you costs.

The trap is emotional, and it's everywhere: "we've put £8,000 into this, we can't stop now." You can. The £8,000 is gone either way. Continuing because of money you've already lost just adds a second loss on top of the first — which is precisely the spiral from the Why Good Management Makes or Breaks a Business section, where each bad turn makes the next one worse.

Opportunity costs are beside you. This is the value of the best thing you gave up. When Priya spends Saturday morning running the market stall, the cost isn't just the stall fee — it's the wedding-cake consultations she didn't hold, and the rest she didn't get. Opportunity cost never appears in any account, and it's often the largest cost in the building.

That's why strategy is mostly a series of noes. Every yes commits resources that can't be somewhere else: the oven's hours, the owner's attention, the £4,000 of spare cash. The Competitors chapter called choosing which fights to enter the biggest decision management makes. Opportunity cost is the price tag on that decision.

The other half of profit

Revenue minus costs is profit. That's the whole income statement in five words, and the Revenue chapter took one half of it. This chapter takes the other.

Here's why this half deserves the attention. Revenue is largely what the market allows you — the going price, the elasticity of demand, what the shop down the road is charging. Costs are largely what you allow yourself. One is negotiated in a market you don't control. The other is a set of decisions you make in your own building.

That asymmetry is the whole reason costs reward study. When the Market chapter said "you don't argue with the market price — you lower your costs," this is what it meant.

And one caution, because cost-cutting has a bad reputation for good reason: costs are not the enemy. A business with no costs is a business with no product, no staff and no customers. A cost that buys more than it costs is the entire mechanism of profit. The goal was never the lowest cost — it's the best ratio of cost to value delivered. A £45 tasting session that wins an £180 wedding cake is expensive and worth every penny. A £2 saving on packaging that loses you a café contract is cheap and ruinous.

Worked example: what does a loaf really cost Priya?

Let's put it together. Priya sells a counter loaf for £4.00 and a wholesale loaf to a café for £2.60.

Per loaf The invoice view What it actually costs
Flour, butter, yeast, salt, bag £1.20 £1.20
Waste (about 5% of what she bakes, binned) — £0.06
Gas for the oven, per loaf — £0.05
Dario's three minutes — £0.34
Delivery to the café — £0.12
Cost of putting one loaf in front of a buyer £1.20 £1.65 counter / £1.77 wholesale

Look at the two ends of that table. The invoice says £1.20. The real cost of getting a loaf into a customer's hands is £1.65 over the counter and £1.77 to the café — between a third and a half higher.

Now the consequences. The counter loaf leaves about £2.35 after its direct costs; the wholesale loaf leaves about £0.83. That's the Sales chapter's arithmetic, and now Priya can see exactly where the gap comes from — not from the recipe, which is identical, but from the delivery and the lower price. It also tells her something the invoice never would: the wholesale loaf is worth keeping anyway, because it's 400 units a month on a repeat contract with one conversation, and it uses capacity she'd otherwise waste.

And notice what isn't in that table: the rent, the insurance, the till system, Dario's wage for the hours the shop is empty. Those don't rise with a loaf. Tuck them into the per-loaf figure and it changes depending on whether she bakes 30 that day or 100 — which is exactly the fixed-versus-variable question the next subsection takes on.

One more thing. Last spring Priya bought £600 of proving baskets, because a supplier talked her into them. They're in the loft. That £600 is sunk — it should have no say in anything she decides now. What should have a say is the opportunity cost of her own Saturday, which she currently spends on the market stall. It appears nowhere in her accounts, and it's a real cost every single week.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Count the whole cost. The invoice is the beginning, not the total. Add waste, storage, handling, delivery, the cash tied up and the cost of things going wrong.
  • Keep cost and cash separate in your head. Both matter, they move on different timetables, and confusing them is how profitable businesses run out of money.
  • Work in cost per unit, at your real volume. A number that assumes a busy month will mislead you in a quiet one.
  • Ignore sunk costs; watch opportunity costs. The money behind you is gone. The thing you gave up to be here is the one nobody puts on a spreadsheet — which is exactly why it's so often missed.
  • Remember that yes has a price. Every decision commits oven hours, staff attention or cash that can't be used elsewhere. Strategy is the art of spending those on purpose.
  • Ask what each cost bought. A cost is a decision made visible, and decisions can be checked. Run the management loop on it — plan what you expect to spend, organise so it happens, and control it by comparing the bill against what it delivered.
  • Then check the cheapest costs most carefully. A cost that buys less than it costs is the easiest money in the business to find, and the easiest to miss.

Summary

  • A cost is the value of the resources a business gives up in order to get something — the sacrifice side of every decision, and a decision made visible.
  • Price is not cost. The price you pay is the first instalment; the real cost adds waste, storage, handling, risk and tied-up cash.
  • Cost is not the same as cash leaving the building. Depreciation, unpaid owner labour and spoiled stock are all real costs with no bank transfer — which is why profit and cash can point in opposite directions.
  • Cost per unit is the number decisions are made with, and it depends on volume, because some costs rise with each extra unit and some don't.
  • Sunk costs are behind you and should have no vote in what happens next. Opportunity costs sit silently beside you and are often the biggest costs of all.
  • Profit is revenue minus costs. Revenue is what the market allows; costs are what you allow yourself — which is why this half of the equation rewards attention.
  • Costs aren't the enemy. A cost that buys more than it costs is the mechanism of profit; the aim is the best ratio of cost to value, not the lowest number.

Next, we'll take the cost base apart and sort it into types — direct and indirect, fixed and variable, and the ones that catch businesses out precisely when they can least afford it.
