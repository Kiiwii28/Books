Types of Costs

Look at a business's costs and they seem like one big pile — money out, listed in a column. But that pile is doing at least four quite different things at once, and the differences decide almost everything: what happens to your profit when sales dip, whether a new order is worth taking, where your break-even sits, and how badly a price war hurts you.

Sorting costs into types is the most useful half-hour of arithmetic a manager can do. Get it right, and your accounts stop being a record of the past and start being a tool for decisions.

Start with one question

Every cost in your business can be interrogated with a single question:

If I sold one more unit tomorrow, would this number change?

Answer that honestly for each line and you've already done most of the work. The rest of this subsection is about naming what you find — and knowing which type of cost you're looking at is what tells you whether a decision will help or hurt.

Direct and indirect: where the cost lands

Direct costs can be traced to a specific thing you sell. The flour in a loaf, the steel in a bike frame, the card-processing fee on one transaction. If you stopped selling that item tomorrow, the direct cost would vanish with it.

Indirect costs — usually called overhead — keep the place running but belong to no single unit. The rent, the insurance, the accountant's fee, the cleaner, the wifi. Sell a thousand loaves or none, the bill arrives the same.

You've met this split twice already in different clothes. The Raw Materials chapter separated direct materials (which become the product) from indirect materials like machine oil and cleaning fluid. The Labour chapter did the same for people: direct labour touches the product, indirect labour supervises, cleans and files. Same distinction, three costumes.

It matters because it decides where a cost sits — in the cost of goods sold, right next to the product, or up in overhead. That affects how you price, how you compare products, and which products turn out to be quietly losing you money.

Fixed and variable: how the cost behaves

Now the big one. This is the split that decides your break-even, your risk and, frankly, whether your business survives a bad quarter.

Fixed costs don't move with how much you sell in the short run. Rent, business rates, insurance, a salaried manager, the machine lease, the website subscription. You pay them whether you sell a hundred units or a thousand.

Variable costs rise and fall with each unit. Flour, packaging, hourly wages, delivery charges, a platform's commission. Sell nothing and they're near zero; double your output and they roughly double.

Stepped costs are the awkward middle. Fixed up to a point, then they jump. Your oven bakes 200 loaves a day; want 400 and you need a second oven, which is a whole new lump of fixed cost. A second delivery van, a second supervisor, an extra warehouse — these are steps, not slopes.


Here's the strategic punchline. Fixed costs are a bet; variable costs are a tax. Every pound you convert from variable to fixed — automating a task, hiring a permanent employee, signing a warehouse lease — is a bet that volume will come. Win the bet and your cost per unit falls. Lose it and you're paying for capacity that isn't working. Remember the Manufacturing chapter's furniture workshop: the automated line only wins above about 1,000 units a month, and bleeds badly below it.

It's also why the Labour chapter's ladder of commitment matters so much. Every rung you move down — permanent, part-time, fixed-term, casual, agency — shifts labour from fixed to variable. You buy the ability to shrink your wage bill when demand does, and pay for it in consistency, skill and loyalty.

Total, average and marginal: three ways to look at the same money

Once you know what's fixed and what's variable, three numbers fall out of them:

  • Total cost = fixed costs + variable costs. The whole bill.
  • Average cost per unit = total cost ÷ units produced. This falls as volume rises, because the fixed costs get spread over more units. It's why factories chase scale.
  • Marginal cost = the cost of producing one more unit. Usually it's just the variable cost — the oven's already hot, the rent's already paid.

That third one sounds technical and is actually the most useful. Decisions are made at the margin. If Priya's marginal cost of a loaf is £1.20, then any sale above £1.20 adds something, even one that looks "too cheap" against her average cost.

Which is exactly where a lot of businesses get themselves into trouble — because they price against average cost and turn down work that would have made them money.

Contribution: the bridge between the two

The gap between the price of a unit and its variable cost has a name.

Contribution = selling price − variable cost per unit.

It's called contribution because that's what it does: it contributes towards your fixed costs, and once the fixed costs are covered, it becomes profit. It's the number you met in the Suppliers, Manufacturing and Sales chapters, and here's where it comes from.

Priya's bakery makes it concrete. Her loaf sells for £4.00. Ingredients cost £1.20. Her daily fixed costs — rent, salaries, energy — are £230.

Per day
Revenue (100 loaves × £4.00) £400
Variable cost (100 × £1.20) £120
Contribution £280
Fixed costs £230
Profit £50

Contribution per loaf is £2.80. So her break-even is £230 ÷ £2.80 ≈ 82 loaves a day. Below 82 she loses money; above it, every extra loaf drops £2.80 straight onto the bottom line. That's the whole logic of scale in one sum.

Now watch it earn its keep. A café offers to buy 50 loaves a day at wholesale — £2.60 each, well below her counter price. Bad deal?

Not necessarily. Contribution is £1.40 a loaf, so 50 loaves adds £70 a day. Her fixed costs are already covered by her counter trade. If the run can be done with the van already going that way, that £70 is nearly pure profit — a 40% jump on her £50 a day.

But if the order needs a dedicated morning delivery run at £80 a day, the answer flips: £70 of contribution against £80 of new cost is a loss. That's the difference between a relevant cost (the new delivery) and an irrelevant one (the rent she's paying anyway). Which brings us neatly to the trickiest type of all.

Sunk costs: money that should stop talking

A sunk cost is money already spent and impossible to recover. The market survey for a product you've now shelved. The £8,000 spent developing a feature nobody wants. The deposit on a venue you've outgrown.

Here's the rule, and it's a hard one to follow: sunk costs are irrelevant to the next decision. The only question that matters is what happens from here — what you'll spend next and what you'll get back.

That sounds obvious and almost nobody does it. You'll hear it in every boardroom: "we've put so much into this already, we can't stop now." That's the sunk-cost fallacy, and it's how businesses throw good money after bad for years. The £8,000 is gone either way. Spending £20,000 more to "justify" it doesn't recover anything — it just adds £20,000 to the loss.

The discipline is simple: cross out every cost that's already been paid, and decide on what's left.

Opportunity cost: the cost that never appears on an invoice

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you gave up when you made a choice.

Priya's back room holds the flour. It could hold something else — a bigger counter, a small seating area, a second fridge of cakes. Whatever she gives up by using that space for flour is a real cost of keeping flour, and it appears on no statement anywhere.

This idea runs through the whole book once you start looking:

  • Shelves. Every square metre given to slow-moving stock is a square metre not given to something that sells — the Stores chapter's core problem, measured in stockturn rather than rent.
  • The owner's time. The Labour chapter's blunt lesson: a business that only looks profitable because the owner is quietly donating fifty hours a week isn't profitable, it's subsidised. Those hours had an alternative use.
  • Channels. Selling through a platform that takes 25% isn't just a fee — the Distribution chapter's question is what the next best channel would have earned.
  • Cash. Money sitting in stock is money that can't buy an oven, pay down debt, or sit in the bank — the Financials chapter's working-capital squeeze, arriving from a different direction.

Opportunity cost is invisible to accounting and decisive for strategy. If you only ever count the money that left, you'll systematically overvalue the status quo and undervalue change.

Controllable and uncontrollable: where to spend your energy

One last cut, and it's about attention as much as money.

Controllable costs are the ones your decisions move: what you buy, who you buy it from, how much you waste, how you schedule staff, what you pay for a delivery.

Uncontrollable costs arrive from outside: business rates, interest rates, the world price of flour, wage inflation, a change in tax or regulation. The Economy, Tax and Regulations chapters all deliver these straight to your door.

The point of the split isn't to shrug at the uncontrollable ones — it's to stop wasting management energy arguing with the weather and point it at what you can actually move. Remember the Suppliers chapter's test: how easily could I walk away? A cost you can switch supplier on is controllable. A cost set by a world market is not, and your only real responses there are to reduce how much you need of it, substitute something else, or redesign the product so the cost matters less.

A note on the long run

One quiet caveat hangs over all of this. In the short run, many costs are fixed. In the long run, almost none of them are.

This month, Priya's lease is fixed — she signed it. Over three years, it isn't: she can renegotiate, move, or go smaller. The oven is fixed today; over five years it's a decision.

That's why a choice that makes no sense right now can make perfect sense on a longer view, and vice versa. It's also why fixed costs feel so heavy in a tough quarter: you're stuck with a decision you made when things looked different. The product life cycle chapter makes the same point from the other direction — dedicated equipment is comfortable in growth and terrifying in decline.

Summary

  • Ask one question of every cost: would this change if I sold one more unit? The answer sorts the pile.
  • Direct costs trace to a specific product; indirect costs (overhead) keep the business running. The same split appears in materials and labour.
  • Fixed costs don't move with volume; variable costs do; stepped costs are fixed until they jump. Converting variable to fixed is a bet on volume.
  • Total, average and marginal are three views of the same money. Average cost falls with scale; decisions are made at the margin.
  • Contribution (price − variable cost) is what each sale hands over to cover fixed costs. Break-even = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit — the single most useful sum in this chapter.
  • Sunk costs are already spent and must be ignored in forward decisions, however much they hurt.
  • Opportunity cost — the value of the best alternative forgone — never appears on an invoice and shapes every real strategy choice.
  • Separate controllable from uncontrollable costs, and spend your energy on the first.
  • In the short run many costs are fixed; in the long run almost none are. Match the decision to the horizon.

Sort your own costs into these types this week. The exercise is dull, takes an afternoon, and will tell you more about your business than any report you could buy — because it tells you exactly which levers make you money, and which ones you're only arguing with.
