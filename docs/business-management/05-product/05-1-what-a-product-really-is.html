05-1 What a Product Really Is

Part of 05 Product

Most people, asked what a product is, point at a thing: a loaf of bread, a phone, a can of paint. That answer isn't wrong, it's just the visible tenth of the idea. Get the full definition straight now and a dozen later decisions — what you charge, what it costs to make, who you sell it to, why customers come back — suddenly have a logical place to sit.

The definition worth keeping

A product is anything offered to a market that can satisfy a want or need — and what the customer buys is the benefit, not the object.

Read that twice. The object is real, and somebody has to make it well. But the customer isn't buying flour, water and yeast bound into a crust. They're buying "something nicer than the supermarket loaf to go with Sunday breakfast", or "a treat that makes the walk to the office worth it". The loaf is the vehicle. The benefit is the purchase.

This is why the Market chapter and this one lean on each other. A product only becomes a product when there's a market willing to trade for it. Invent the world's finest left-handed corkscrew in a town where nobody drinks wine and you haven't made a product — you've made an object with costs attached.

It doesn't have to be a physical thing

The word "product" covers a lot more ground than the shelf in a shop:

  • Goods — a bicycle, a jar of honey, a printed book.
  • Services — a haircut, an MOT, a fortnight of accounting.
  • Combinations, which is nearly everything. A delivered pizza is a good and a service. A software subscription is a licence plus support plus updates.

Treat goods and services as two ends of one line rather than two categories. A hand-made chair is mostly good, with a little service — the delivery, the assembly instructions. A taxi ride is mostly service, with a little good — the car. Once you see the line, you stop asking "is this a product?" and start asking "which parts of this do I actually control?"

Three levels, and the customer only cares about the first

Here's the most useful mental model in this whole chapter. Every product exists at three levels at once.


The core benefit is the need being satisfied. Not "bread" but "a reliable, pleasant lunch". Not "drill" but "a hole in the wall".

The actual product is the tangible version: the recipe, the size, the packaging, the label, the flavour, the way it's presented. This is what most businesses spend their time on, and rightly — it's the part you can change.

The augmented product is everything wrapped around it: how easy it is to buy, how fast it arrives, whether returns are painless, the guarantee, the support, the reputation of the name on the box. In competitive markets, this is where the fight is actually won, because the actual products have converged. Two loaves of sourdough can be near-identical. One is bought from a shop that opens at 7am with a smile and a paper bag; the other from a shop with no sign and a queue out of the door.

Notice how this reaches into other chapters. The augmented product is the Customer Service, Distribution and Stores chapters in disguise. When those sections talk about delivery speed, returns policy or how a shop feels, they're describing product decisions, not afterthoughts.

Features vs benefits: the translation you must do

A feature is what your product has. A benefit is what it does for the customer. Businesses fall in love with features because they're the fun part to build. Customers buy benefits and ignore everything else.

Feature (what you made) Benefit (what they hear)
Stoneground heritage flour Bread that stays fresh until Thursday
24/7 online ordering You can sort tomorrow's lunch at 11pm
Reinforced waterproof shell You stay dry on the school run

The translation isn't spin. It's the difference between describing your product and describing your customer's day. If you can't put your product into a single sentence that names a benefit, you don't yet know what you're selling — and neither will anyone else.

Worked example: Priya's sourdough loaf

Priya bakes a sourdough loaf. Follow it through the three levels.

Core benefit. Her customer, Sam, isn't solving a flour shortage. She's solving "I want fifteen good minutes and something better than the office vending machine."

Actual product. The loaf itself: a 800g sourdough with a dark crust, baked at 5am, sold for £4, wrapped in paper with a hand-stamped label. Recipe, size, price, packaging, name. Every one of those is a decision somebody made.

Augmented product. The shop opens at 7am so Sam can buy before work. There's a loyalty card — every tenth loaf free. Staff will slice it if she asks. If a loaf is stale when she gets it home, Priya replaces it without an argument. None of that is bread. All of it is why Sam chooses Priya's over the supermarket meal deal and the café two doors down.

Now the strategic part. When Priya raised her price from £4 to £4.40 (see the Prices subsection), she didn't change the core benefit or the recipe. She was testing whether her augmented product — the reputation, the convenience, the relationship — had earned her some pricing power. That's the Competitors chapter and the Costs chapter meeting head-on: what customers will pay depends on how substitutable you are, and how substitutable you are depends on the whole bundle, not the loaf alone.


Read that loop and notice something important: the product is the hinge of the whole business. Every cost you incur — raw materials, labour, manufacturing, storage, distribution — arrives at the product. Every pound of revenue leaves from it. A business is, in a very real sense, a machine for converting inputs into a product and a product into money. Get the product wrong and nothing downstream can save you.

The product decides most of your costs

Beginners think costs are a separate topic, managed later by someone else. In truth, most of your costs are locked in the moment you define the product.

Design a loaf that must be eaten within six hours and you've just signed up for fresh-baked batches, small-scale production, and waste at the end of the day. Design one that keeps for a week and your costs shift to packaging and cold chain. Choose a thick crust and you may need a different oven. Choose a delicate one and you may need different Distribution — a van that doesn't crush it.

This is why the Raw Materials and Manufacturing chapters come after this one in the book. They're consequences of product decisions, not independent choices. A good strategist thinks about a product change and immediately asks: what does this do to my cost per unit, my lead times, my supplier list, my storage, and the skills my staff need?

A product is not a brand

Worth separating, because the two get muddled constantly.

The product is what actually gets delivered — the loaf, the app, the haircut. The brand is the set of expectations in the customer's head about what the product will be like. The brand is a promise; the product is the keeping of it.

A brand can be built in years and destroyed in a week by a product that doesn't deliver. That's why consistency beats occasional brilliance. A bakery whose loaves are superb on Tuesday and mediocre on Friday has a product problem and a brand problem, and the second one is harder to fix.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Start from the need, not the idea. Ask what job the customer is hiring your product to do for them. Everything else is downstream.
  • Define the benefit in one sentence. If you can't, you don't yet know what you're selling, and neither will your staff, your suppliers, or your customers.
  • Manage all three levels. The core benefit is settled by the customer's need, but the actual and augmented layers are where you can win. Service, delivery and returns are product decisions — the Customer Service and Distribution chapters are really about this.
  • Remember that a product is never finished. Tastes shift, competitors move, markets shift under you. That's why the next two subsections cover the types of products and the life cycle they pass through — both are tools for deciding what to do next, not just ways of labelling what you already have.
  • Never fall in love with the object. The world is full of beautifully made products that nobody wanted, beaten by average products sold by well-managed businesses. The market rewards the benefit delivered, not the effort spent.

Summary

  • A product is anything offered to a market that satisfies a want or need — the customer buys the benefit, not the object.
  • Products include goods, services and combinations; treat them as two ends of one line, not two categories.
  • Every product has three levels: the core benefit (the need), the actual product (the tangible thing), and the augmented product (delivery, service, guarantee, brand). Competition usually happens at the third level.
  • Features describe what you made; benefits describe what the customer gets. Always translate.
  • The product is the hinge of the business — all costs arrive at it, all revenue leaves from it — and most costs are locked in by product decisions made early.
  • A brand is the promise; the product is the keeping of it. Consistency is what keeps the two aligned.
  • The test for any product decision: does this deliver more of what the customer actually needs, at a cost the business can live with?
