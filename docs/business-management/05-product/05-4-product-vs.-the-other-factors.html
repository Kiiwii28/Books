Product vs. The Other Factors

Every chapter in this book so far has described a piece of the machine. Now we come to the piece that all the others are quietly serving. The product is where the business actually meets the world — the only one of the twenty factors a customer ever knowingly pays for. Everything else is either how you build the product, how you move the product, how you tell people about the product, or how you count the money the product brought in.

That makes the product unusual, and it makes this comparison unusually important. Think of the other factors as spokes and the product as the hub. Spin the hub and every spoke turns with it.


There is a second reason the product deserves special treatment: it is the hardest decision to reverse. You can change a price on Monday and a promotion on Tuesday. You cannot un-design a product. Once you have chosen aluminium cans, a co-packer, a cold chain and a shelf-ready case size, you have committed suppliers, tooling, trucks and cash for years. Strategists call this path dependence. Practically it means the product decision is where the most leverage sits — and where the most expensive mistakes live. Get the product right and the other nineteen factors have a chance to work. Get it wrong and no amount of clever pricing, energetic sales or lean manufacturing will save you.

So let's walk around the hub, one group of factors at a time.

Product and the demand side: Market, Competitors, Distribution, Stores, Sales, Customer Service

These are the factors that face outward, and the product is their common language.

Market. In the Market chapter you learned that a market is buyers with a want and the ability to pay. The product is your answer to that want — and the market decides whether the answer was right. This is what people mean by product–market fit: not "do people like it?" but "does the market's definition of value match what I built?" A market also tells you how many variants are worth making. One national market with three clear buyer segments might justify three product versions; a fragmented market where every region wants something different may justify none, because complexity will eat the margin.

Competitors. Competitors are the reason your product cannot be judged in isolation. A product has no inherent price — its price is set relative to what else the buyer could pick. The Competitors chapter gave you the tools for sizing up rivals; apply them directly to the product: which features are table stakes, which are differentiators, and which are expensive flourishes nobody pays for? Every unnecessary "nice to have" you add to match a rival is cost you carry forever. The competitor's product also sets your price ceiling, which is why a competitor's launch can wreck your unit economics overnight even if nothing about your own factory changed.

Distribution. A product's physical nature dictates its route. Bulky, fragile, perishable, hazardous, digital — each one draws a different map. Water is heavy, so you don't ship it far. Ice cream needs a cold chain, which means distributors with freezer trucks. Software has near-zero distribution cost, which is why a digital product can reach a global market on a tiny budget. Read the Distribution chapter again with this in mind: channel choice is really a product design consequence, and changing the channel often forces a redesign (smaller pack, longer shelf life, tougher box).

Stores. Shelf space is scarce, and it is rented by results. In a physical store your product must earn its slot against every other product competing for the same 30 centimetres, and it must sell itself off the shelf, because most shoppers decide in seconds. That is why packaging is sometimes called the silent salesperson. Store format also shapes the product: convenience stores push single-serve sizes, warehouse clubs push bulk multipacks, and a product designed for one often fails in the other. Add the private-label problem — the store's own version of your product competes with you on the same shelf, usually cheaper.

Sales. Salespeople sell products, and the product's complexity sets the length and cost of the sales cycle. A product that's easy to demonstrate and easy to explain mostly sells itself; a complex one needs trained reps, long meetings and possibly a technical specialist riding along. Remember that sales can amplify a good product but cannot rescue a bad one indefinitely — word of mouth travels faster in reverse.

Customer service. Service load is largely a product decision made months earlier. A product that breaks generates warranty claims. A product that confuses generates support calls. A product with a baffling manual generates returns. In the Customer Service chapter you saw that service can also be part of the product — a warranty, an installation visit, a subscription upgrade. Either way, the cost of service sits downstream of the design decision, which is why experienced managers budget service costs at the design stage, not the complaints stage.

Product and the supply side: Suppliers, Raw Materials, Manufacturing, Labour, Staff

These factors face inward. The product is the specification they all have to hit.

Suppliers. Your product's bill of materials picks your suppliers. Design a product around a rare component and you have just married yourself to whoever makes it. This is why smart firms involve suppliers early in design — not as vendors but as co-designers who can say "we can't hold that tolerance" or "there's a cheaper standard part that does the same job." Switching suppliers later often means redesigning the product, which is exactly the kind of hidden cost that kills a launch.

Raw materials. Materials set a ceiling on quality and a floor on cost. Choose steel and you get durability and weight; choose plastic and you get cheapness and a different set of customer expectations. Commodity materials also import volatility: a cocoa or copper price spike arrives as an uninvited change to your product margin. The strategic response is usually a design response — lightweighting, substituting, or reducing waste per unit — which is why the Raw Materials chapter and this one are effectively the same conversation held twice.

Manufacturing. Manufacturing constraints shape what the product can be. Tooling costs mean you need volume before a design makes sense. Minimum order quantities mean your "small trial batch" might not exist. The variety tax is real: every extra variant adds changeovers, setup time and quality risk, so a portfolio of forty SKUs can be measurably less profitable than a portfolio of eight. Design for manufacture is the discipline of shaping the product so the factory can make it cheaply and consistently — and it is usually worth far more than squeezing the factory to make a badly designed product.

Labour. Product complexity determines the skill mix, and the skill mix determines your wage bill and your exposure to labour shortages. A product with heavy hand-work content is vulnerable to wage inflation and to losing key people; a highly automated product line is vulnerable to capital cost instead. There is no free option — only a choice about which risk you'd rather carry.

Staff. Your staff are the product's first internal customers, and they need to understand and believe in it. A product nobody internally can explain will be explained badly to customers. Product changes also create retraining costs that rarely appear in the launch budget. And there's a quieter effect: the products a company makes determine who wants to work there. Interesting products attract good people, which loops straight back into Labour and Management.

Product and the money: Costs, Revenue, Financials

Here the relationships become arithmetic. Recall the shape of profit:

Profit = (price − variable cost) × units sold − fixed costs

Look at how many of those terms the product touches. It sets the price ceiling (via Competitors and Market), the variable cost (via Raw Materials and Manufacturing), the volume (via Stores, Sales, Distribution and Customer Service), and the fixed cost (via tooling, plant and development). Very few decisions move all four terms at once. This one does.

Costs. Most of a product's cost is committed at design. Analysts sometimes say 70–80% of unit cost is locked in before the first unit ships, because after that you are only optimising within the design you chose. Value engineering — systematically stripping cost out of a product without stripping value out — is one of the highest-return activities in business.

Revenue. Revenue is product × price × volume, but the quality of revenue matters as much as the size. One blockbuster product is efficient but terrifying; twenty small products are diversified but expensive to run. Subscription and consumable products shift revenue from one-off to recurring, which is worth far more than the same money earned once.

Financials. This is where the truth about your product portfolio shows up. A product-level profit-and-loss statement routinely reveals that a third of your SKUs make no money at all — they exist because nobody ever checked. Inventory on the balance sheet is simply products you've paid for and not yet sold. Development spending may be capitalised as an asset that future sales must justify. Warranty and returns provisions are product promises recorded as financial liabilities. Read your financials with a product lens and you will usually find a decision waiting to be made.

Product and the frame around the business: Company, Management, Economy, Tax, Regulations

Company. The company is the container; the product is the offering. A product must fit the company's identity, capabilities and brand promise — a premium brand selling a cut-price product damages both. The company also carries the legal liability for what the product does, and it owns the product portfolio as a whole. Recall the Company chapter's point about parts working together: the product is the part every other part must line up behind.

Management. Management's core job with respect to products is portfolio management: deciding what to fund, what to grow, what to harvest and — hardest of all — what to kill. Roadmaps, stage gates, and the discipline to stop a failing project before it eats the next one are management functions, not product functions. In the Management chapter we saw that managers allocate scarce resources; the product portfolio is where those allocations become visible.

Economy. Products have elasticity. Discretionary, big-ticket, borrowed-money products suffer first in a downturn; staples barely notice. Input inflation, interest rates and currency movements all arrive through the product's cost and price. And the economic cycle interacts with the product life cycle: launching a genuinely new category into a recession is brutal, while launching a cheaper alternative into one can be a gift. Great strategists time products, not just design them.

Tax. Tax treatment is not uniform across products, and design can follow tax. Excise duties hit alcohol, tobacco and fuel. Sugar taxes have prompted whole reformulation programmes. Sales tax and VAT rates differ by category — basic food versus luxury goods, physical book versus e-book. Tariffs apply to imported components but not to locally made ones, which can make a design decision a tax decision. Research and development tax credits reward the development itself. The Tax chapter's lesson holds: a product that is cleverly structured can be worth more after tax than a better product that is structured badly.

Regulations. Compliance is a design input, not an afterthought. Safety standards, labelling, packaging rules, environmental requirements (recyclability, restricted substances, take-back schemes), advertising restrictions and product liability all shape what you are allowed to make and sell. Regulation can also be a moat: if the compliance bar is high, fewer competitors can clear it. Sometimes regulation creates a product category outright — think energy-efficient appliances or low-emission vehicles. And it can remove one just as fast, which is why regulatory horizon-scanning belongs in product planning, not in the legal department's inbox.

Worked example: Northwind Coffee launches a canned cold brew

Northwind is a small roastery with a shop and an online subscription. It wants to sell a canned cold brew in grocery stores. Watch how every factor pushes on one product decision.

The company is a craft roaster with a promise of quality — a cheap, syrupy canned drink would contradict it, so the recipe must taste like Northwind. The market is cold coffee drinkers, a young and growing segment, but the competitors are two global brands with shelf power and a lower cost base, which caps the price around $3.50 a can. Raw materials — green beans, water, and aluminium cans — plus suppliers who can guarantee can supply at volume, set the bill of materials. Manufacturing is outsourced to a co-packer with a 20,000-can minimum run, which means Northwind must commit cash before it knows whether the product sells. Labour needs a new production coordinator. Distribution requires refrigerated trucks, and stores will only take the product if it fits a standard shelf-ready case. Sales requires a broker to win the listing. Customer service will field complaints if a can is flat or the shelf life fails. Costs land at $2.10 a can all-in, giving a $1.40 gross margin. Revenue depends on moving 20,000 cans before they expire. Financials show a working-capital hit of tens of thousands before a single dollar comes back. Tax adds deposit-return scheme obligations on the cans and sales tax on the category. Regulations demand nutrition labelling, allergen declarations and food-safety compliance at the co-packer. And the economy decides whether consumers keep paying a premium for a nice-to-have drink when budgets tighten.

Change one thing — say, switch to a 12,000-can minimum with a smaller co-packer — and labour, distribution, stores, financials and risk all change with it. That is the whole point of this subsection.

Summary

The product is the hub the other nineteen factors turn around. On the demand side, Market and Competitors define what the product must be worth, while Distribution, Stores, Sales and Customer Service decide whether it ever reaches a buyer and at what cost. On the supply side, Suppliers, Raw Materials, Manufacturing and Labour are all specified by the product, and Staff give it a voice. Costs, Revenue and Financials turn the product into a profit-and-loss reality — profit = (price − variable cost) × units − fixed costs, every term of which the product touches. Company, Management, Economy, Tax and Regulations form the frame the product must fit inside, and each can be a constraint or an advantage depending on how early you plan for it.

The strategic conclusion is simple to state and hard to live by: because the product commits so many other factors, and because it is the hardest decision to reverse, it deserves more of your thinking time than any other single factor. Do the design work carefully, test it against every spoke of the hub, and the rest of the business gets a fair chance to be profitable. Skip that work and you will spend years optimising everything except the thing that was wrong.
