05-3 The Life Cycle of a Product

Part of 05 Product

Your best-selling product will not sell forever. It gets launched, it catches on (or doesn't), it settles into a comfortable groove, and eventually something — a rival, a trend, a new technology, a change in what customers want — starts to pull it downwards. That shape, from launch to fade, is the product life cycle, and understanding it is what stops you from being surprised by your own business.

The shape of a product's life

The product life cycle is the pattern a product's sales and profits tend to follow over its life: introduction, growth, maturity, decline. There's also a stage before the beginning — development — where you spend money and sell nothing at all.

Here's the whole thing at a glance:


And here's what's actually going on in each stage:

Stage Sales Rivals Customers What the stage demands
Development None yet None yet Nobody Decide whether it's worth launching
Introduction Low, rising slowly Few or none Curious early buyers Get noticed, fix the flaws, keep costs survivable
Growth Rising fast Many piling in The mainstream arrives Build capacity, distribution and brand fast
Maturity Flat, at its peak Shake-out — a few big players Nearly everyone who'll ever buy Defend share, cut costs, differentiate
Decline Falling Rivals exiting The last loyal buyers Harvest, sell, reinvent — or retire it

Read that table twice, because the middle columns matter more than the sales curve. A product's life cycle is really a story about competition. As a product succeeds, rivals arrive. As rivals arrive, the fight shifts from "can we make enough?" to "why should anyone pick ours?"

Why profit peaks before sales do

This is the part people miss. Sales usually peak in maturity — but profit tends to peak earlier, somewhere late in growth.

Three things cause the squeeze:

  • Costs per unit fall first, then stop falling. Early on, small volumes mean every bun carries a share of the fixed costs — the oven, the rent, the staff on shift. That links straight back to the Costs chapter: fixed costs spread over few units are painful, and spreading them over many is a gift. But once you're running at capacity, the gift runs out.
  • Prices come under pressure. As rivals multiply, your product stops being the only answer to the customer's need. In the language of the Market chapter, your demand becomes more elastic — a small price rise now costs you more customers than it did two years ago. In the language of the Competitors chapter, your market structure hardens from a crowd of small differentiated sellers into a handful of big ones fighting on everything but price.
  • Selling costs rise. In growth, word of mouth does the work. In maturity, you're paying for it: promotions, loyalty schemes, better packaging, a nicer display.

Put those together and you get the classic squeeze: more units sold, less money kept per unit.

Worked example: Priya's cinnamon buns

Priya adds a new product to her bakery — cinnamon buns. Watch it live through a full cycle. (Contribution here means what's left of the price after the ingredients: the money that goes toward rent, wages and profit.)

Stage Price Cost per bun Contribution each Sold per day Daily contribution
Introduction £3.00 £1.30 £1.70 30 £51
Growth £3.00 £0.95 £2.05 90 £184
Maturity £2.60 £0.90 £1.70 100 £170
Decline £2.60 £0.90 £1.70 40 £68

Read the growth and maturity rows together, because that's the lesson in one glance. In maturity she sells more buns than in growth and makes less money doing it.

What happened in between? In growth, her bigger volumes let her buy flour and butter in bulk from her supplier at a better price, and 90 buns come out of a single baking session. By maturity, two nearby cafés have copied her, so £3.00 stops working and she drops to £2.60 to hold her spot. She also pays for a new display case and a month of local advertising. Sales are at their highest; profit is quietly going backwards.

And it all started with six weeks of recipe testing in development, when the buns sold nothing at all.

Not every product follows that curve

The four-stage curve is a pattern, not a law. Real products take several shapes, and mistaking one for another causes expensive mistakes:

  • The fad — a sharp spike and a fast collapse. It never reaches maturity because it was never really about the product; it was about being in on something. If you build a factory for a fad, you own a factory.
  • The fashion — a gentler rise, a real plateau, a gradual fade. Clothing styles, restaurant trends, most "healthy eating" waves.
  • The classic — a long, long maturity, sometimes decades. Bread. Bicycles. A well-run corner shop. These are the ones that pay the bills.
  • The stillborn — a product that never gets past introduction, usually because it solved a problem nobody had, or was priced wrong, or nobody ever heard of it.

Two practical points fall out of this. First, the type of product shapes the cycle — a fashion item lives its whole life in months, while a durable tool can sit in maturity for a generation. Second, some products get a second life. Vinyl records were written off as dead and came back as a premium product for a different buyer. A product in decline is not always a product at the end.

Extending the cycle

Nothing in strategy says you have to accept the curve as it arrives. Before you let a product slide into decline, you have four levers — and they're not mutually exclusive:


A bakery example of each: selling buns to a café wholesale (new market), promoting them as an after-school snack as well as a breakfast (new use), launching a mini four-pack (product change), or going premium with a hand-rolled, slow-proved version at twice the price (repositioning).

Two honest cautions. Extension costs money and, more importantly, it costs management attention — those are the scarce resources from the opening chapter, and every one spent reviving an old product is one not spent on a new one. And a product you keep alive out of sentiment is not a strategy; it's a bill.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Read the signals, not the sales figures. Watch competitor entries, repeat-purchase rates, your own price resistance and the trade press. These are the controlling half of the management loop — the check that tells you a stage is turning before the accounts do.
  • Invest ahead of the stage, not after it. Order the second oven while you're still in growth, because you'll wait six weeks for it. Add the extra delivery van, hire the driver, and expand into the wholesale channel before the queue forms. That's planning and organizing, and it's where most product launches are won or lost.
  • Match your other factors to the stage. Distribution widens as you move from introduction to growth; customer service costs climb in maturity as everyone expects support; staffing needs shift from flexible all-rounders to specialists; suppliers get squeezed for better terms once your volumes justify it. The product cycle pulls every other chapter along with it.
  • Mind the cash. Growth eats cash — stock, capacity, credit given to wholesale customers. Maturity throws it off. That's why a healthy company runs a portfolio of products at different stages, with the mature ones funding the launches.

  • Don't kill a cash cow because it looks boring. A mature product with thin margins may still be paying your rent and bringing customers through the door who then buy other things. Equally, don't cling to a product that's genuinely finished. The question is never "is this old?" — it's "does the money and attention this product consumes earn more here than somewhere else?"

Summary

  • The product life cycle runs through development, introduction, growth, maturity and decline — and it's really a story about rivals arriving as your product succeeds.
  • Profit usually peaks before sales do, because unit costs stop falling, rivals force prices down, and selling gets more expensive.
  • The curve is a pattern, not a law: fads spike and collapse, fashions plateau and fade, classics stay mature for decades, and some products die at introduction or come back years later.
  • You can extend a cycle with new markets, new uses, product changes and repositioning — but every extension costs money and management attention.
  • Use the cycle to plan ahead: build capacity and channels before the growth surge, not during it, and check the signals like any other control loop.
  • Run a portfolio — mature products generate the cash that funds the growth products of tomorrow.
  • A product's stage should never be a surprise. If it is, the failure isn't in the product; it's in the managing.
