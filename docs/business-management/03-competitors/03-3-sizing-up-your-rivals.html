03-3 Sizing Up Your Rivals

Part of 03 Competitors

You already know who your competitors are. The next job is harder: figuring out what each of them is actually like — how strong, how fast, how cheap, how clever — so you can stop guessing and start planning. That's what "sizing up your rivals" means. It's the everyday practice of gathering what's publicly knowable about the businesses you're competing with, comparing it honestly against your own, and turning the difference into decisions.

Think of it as building a mental scorecard for each rival. Not a dossier you lock in a safe, just a clear, current picture you can act on.

Start With What's Hiding in Plain Sight

Beginners assume competitor research means secret information. In reality, most of what you need is already public — you just have to look systematically instead of casually.

  • Their website and pricing pages. What do they sell, at what price, with what promises? Screenshot it. Prices change, and the changes tell a story.
  • Their financial reports. Public companies must publish annual reports and accounts. Smaller ones often file abbreviated accounts with a company registry. These reveal revenue, profit, borrowing, and how fast they're growing.
  • Their job adverts. A company hiring ten warehouse staff is expanding its logistics. One hiring for its first-ever "Head of Data" is investing in analytics. Job ads are a public announcement of strategy.
  • Their customers. Read reviews, complaints, and social media comments. Every recurring gripe is a gap you might fill.
  • Their suppliers, distributors, and partners. Trade press, press releases, and industry events often reveal who they're working with.
  • Wider context. Industry reports, court records, patents, and regulatory filings fill in the picture you can't see from the shop window.

Build a Competitor Profile

A competitor profile is a short, structured summary of one rival. The point isn't length — it's comparability. If you use the same headings for every rival, you can line them up side by side and spot the gaps.

Dimension What to look at Why it matters
Offer Product range, quality, features Where you overlap and where you don't
Price List prices, discounts, bundles Their price position sets your ceiling
Reach Locations, online presence, delivery How easily customers can find them
Promotion Adverts, social activity, sponsorships How loud they are, and where
Service Reviews, response times, guarantees Often the cheapest thing to beat
Cost base Scale, automation, own manufacturing Who can survive a price war
Firepower Profit, cash, investors, borrowing Who can outspend you
Talent Who they hire, staff reviews Signals of ambition and culture
Momentum New launches, new sites, growth Who's moving and who's stuck

Keep each profile to a page. If it doesn't fit on a page, you're collecting trivia, not intelligence.

Turn Observations Into Benchmarks

Benchmarking means measuring yourself against a rival on the same yardstick. Raw observations — "they're cheaper" — are vague. Numbers force honesty.

Score each rival from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) on the dimensions above, and score yourself the same way. Then do the arithmetic that matters:

  • Market share. Your sales divided by total sales in the market. If you and your rivals sell 10,000 units combined and you sell 2,000, you hold 20%. Falling share in a growing market is a warning sign even when your own sales rise.
  • Relative price. Compare like-for-like products. Are they 10% cheaper, or 10% dearer? A rival who is dearer and growing is telling you something about branding or convenience.
  • Relative reach. Count the customer touchpoints: stores, delivery options, channels, languages. Reach explains a lot of sales gaps that quality alone can't.
  • Growth rate. Compare year-on-year changes, not just totals. A small rival doubling every year will be a big rival soon.

Map the Field at a Glance

Once you have scores, a positioning map turns the spreadsheet into a picture. Pick the two dimensions customers care about most — usually price and quality, or convenience and range — and plot everyone, including yourself.


The loop matters more than any single pass. Rivals move; a profile that was accurate last year may be misleading today. Review the big ones quarterly, and keep a lighter watch on the rest.

Sources and the Lines You Don't Cross

Competitive intelligence is legal, ethical, and ordinary. Espionage is none of those things — and it destroys trust, invites lawsuits, and can end a career.

Safe and smart:

  • Reading everything a rival publishes.
  • Visiting their stores and using their service as an ordinary customer.
  • Talking to shared suppliers, industry bodies, and trade journalists.
  • Attending the same trade shows and reading the same reports.

Off limits:

  • Pretending to be someone you're not to obtain confidential information.
  • Encouraging a rival's employee to hand over trade secrets or breach a confidentiality agreement.
  • Hiring someone purely to extract the secrets of their old employer.
  • Trespassing, hacking, or misusing private data.

A simple test: if your rival read about your method on the front page of a trade magazine, would you be comfortable? If not, don't do it.

From Notes to Action

Research that changes nothing is a hobby. Turn each profile into a one-page battlecard — the offer, the price, their strengths, their weaknesses, and the single sentence telling your team how to win against them. A salesperson about to meet a customer who is also talking to a rival should be able to read it in two minutes.

Then ask three questions of every rival:

  1. Where are they strong? Don't attack a fortress. Compete somewhere else.
  2. Where are they weak? Slow delivery, rude service, narrow range, ageing product. Weaknesses in reviews are the cheapest opportunities you'll ever find.
  3. What will they do next? A new store opening, a funding round, a price cut. Anticipate, and prepare your response before it lands.

Finally, watch for trigger events: a sudden price drop, a new product launch, an acquisition, a key executive leaving. These are the moments when a static profile becomes urgent, actionable intelligence.

Summary

Sizing up your rivals is a repeatable habit, not a one-off project. Start with public signals — websites, filings, job ads, reviews. Turn them into short competitor profiles using identical headings so rivals can be compared. Convert those profiles into benchmarks: market share, relative price, relative reach, and growth rate. Picture the result on a positioning map so you can see where the crowd is and where the space is. Stay firmly on the right side of the legal and ethical line. And finish the job by writing battlecards and asking what each rival will do next. The goal isn't to copy your competitors — it's to know exactly where you can beat them.
