03-4 Competitors vs. The Other Factors

Part of 03 Competitors

You've spent three subsections on this chapter. You know who your competitors are — including the deli, the vending machine and the customer's own kitchen. You know what kind of fight you're in, from perfect competition to a local monopoly. And you know how to size a rival up honestly, using public information and a scorecard.

So we arrive at the closing question of the chapter, and it's the same one the Company section ended on: what kind of thing is a competitor, compared with everything else on the list?

Because look at the neighbours. Nineteen other factors crowd around it — suppliers, labour, product, tax, the economy. And nearly all of them are, in some sense, things. Inputs, conditions, machinery, rules. Competitors are different. And the difference changes how you should hold the whole rest of this book in your head.

The only factor that has an opinion about you

Every other factor on the list is either indifferent, on your side, or simply inert.

  • The economy doesn't know your name.
  • Regulations and tax would apply exactly the same way if you vanished tomorrow.
  • Your suppliers want you to thrive, because your success is their order book.
  • Your staff want the business to work, because it pays them.
  • Your customers want your product to be good, because they want the benefit.
  • Raw materials, manufacturing, stores and distribution hold no view whatsoever. They're apparatus.

A competitor is the only factor on the list actively working to take something from you. Not out of spite — just by doing its job. Every sale it wins is a sale you didn't get. Every good baker it hires is one you can't. Every efficiency it finds is one that lets it undercut you next quarter.

That single difference — an opposing goal — is why strategy exists as a discipline. A monopolist doesn't need strategy; it needs a regulator-watcher and a queue system. Competition is what turns administration into strategy. Which means: the moment you stop having meaningful competitors is the moment your job changes shape.

The second difference: competitors are the yardstick

Here's the consequence, and it's the most immediately useful idea in this subsection.

Take any factor in this book and ask "are we good at this?" The question is meaningless on its own. Good compared with what?

A 30% food cost is excellent — or catastrophic — depending on what the café down the road pays. Two weeks of stock cover is prudent or reckless depending on what your rivals hold. A 4% net margin is a triumph in a supermarket and a scandal in a software business.

Competitors are what make every other factor's number mean anything.

That's precisely why the earlier Sizing Up Your Rivals subsection pushed you into benchmarking rather than observing. "They're cheaper" is a feeling. "They're 8% cheaper on a like-for-like loaf and still growing" is a strategy input. The competitor is the instrument that converts your internal figures into a competitive position.

And it runs the other way too — competitors set the standard you have to beat. Their cost base sets your price ceiling. Their service level sets what your customers think is normal. Their delivery speed this year becomes the minimum speed next year. Nothing about your business is judged in a vacuum; it's judged against the version of it your rival is offering on the same street.

The map: four ways every other factor connects

Every item on that list of nineteen relates to competitors in one of four ways. Here's the whole picture in one page.


Let's take them in turn.

Fought through: product, price, sales, service, stores, distribution

These are your weapons. They're the factors you deploy against rivals: a sharper product, a keener price, friendlier service, a better-placed shop, a faster delivery.

Two lessons live here. First, these are the factors customers actually experience — so they're the ones competition is fought over most loudly. Remember the augmented product from the Product chapter: when actual products converge, the fight moves into delivery, returns, service and convenience. That's not a marketing nicety; it's the front line.

Second, and more sobering: weapons get copied. Anything you can buy, a rival can buy. File a new menu and it's on their board in a fortnight. Cut your delivery time and theirs matches it. This is why the price competition warned about in Types of Competition is a trap — you cut your price, they match it within a week, and now you've both given away margin for nothing.

Fought for: labour, staff, suppliers, raw materials, locations, capital

These are the shared pond. You and your rivals are fishing in it at the same time — for the same qualified bakers, the same reliable mill, the same corner site, the same bank loan.

Notice what this does to the idea of a "good" factor. A great supplier relationship isn't an advantage because it's good. It's an advantage because your rival doesn't have one. A trained, loyal team isn't a strength in the abstract — it's a strength because the café down the road is retraining its third barista this year.

So: advantage lives in the gap, not in the absolute number. Stop asking whether your operations are good and start asking whether they're better than the alternative your customers could choose.

And be honest about the mechanism underneath. When two businesses bid for the same scarce thing, they push its price up. Two bakeries chasing one qualified baker raises wages in that town. Everyone's cost base rises and the advantage disappears — the same way profits get competed away, one input at a time.

Faced together: the economy, tax, regulations

This is the group people most often get wrong. A minimum wage rise hits you and your rival equally. A recession hits you both. A new food-labelling rule applies to every bakery in the country.

So is it competitive? Yes — but not as a difference in exposure. As a difference in response.

Shared factors are a test both of you sit at the same time, and the scores differ. The rival with cash in the bank absorbs an input-cost spike; the one with a thin margin passes it on and loses customers. A £50,000-a-year compliance burden is a mild annoyance for a chain and an unscalable wall for a startup — which is exactly why regulation so often protects incumbents. The Economy, Tax and Regulations chapters aren't background weather. They're a shared exam, and your relative resilience is the mark.

Fought in: the market, and the frame you fight with

Two more relationships to name, because they aren't quite the same as the three groups above.

The market is the arena. And here's the circular part: your market is defined by which rivals customers treat as real substitutes, while market structure — the spectrum from perfect competition to monopoly — defines how those rivals behave. The Market and Competitors chapters aren't two topics. They're two views of one thing. Change your market boundary and you've changed your competitor list; change your channel and you've done it again, as the bakery's wholesale business discovered.

The company is the vehicle. The legal form you chose in the Company chapter sets your risk ceiling — how big a bet you can take in every other factor. Limited liability and a healthy balance sheet are what let you sign the bigger contract, open the second site, or survive a price war that would finish off a sole trader across the road. And a company can itself be bought and sold, which is the ultimate competitive move: your rival doesn't have to out-compete you, it can simply acquire you.

Worked example: Priya's scorecard against the café

Here's the whole argument in one table. Priya's bakery against the café two doors down, factor by factor, with an honest verdict on each.

Factor Priya The café two doors down Verdict
Product Hand-made sourdough, narrow range Wider range, less distinctive Advantage — but only if customers care
Market Same street, same lunchtime buyers Same Shared
Raw materials Same mill, similar price Same mill, bigger volume Weakness on unit cost
Suppliers No contract, buys as needed National contract, better terms Weakness
Labour and staff Three people, low turnover, trained in-house Twelve people, high churn Advantage in quality and cost per unit
Manufacturing One oven, 100 loaves Two ovens, 400 units Weakness on scale
Stores Small, cramped, no seating Bigger, seating, better display Weakness
Distribution Own van, three café deliveries Own van, more routes Roughly parity
Sales Counter, wholesale, market stall, app Counter only Advantage in reach
Customer service Knows regulars by name Transactional, scripted Advantage, and hard to copy
Management Owner-operator, decides today Regional manager, decides in head office Advantage in agility
Financials Thin margins, no cash buffer Backed by a parent company Weakness — she can't outlast them
Revenue £4 a loaf, 100 a day £3.50 a unit, 400 a day Weakness on total
Costs £1.20 ingredients, small volume Lower unit costs from scale Weakness
Economy, tax, regulation Same rules, same town Same rules, same town Shared

Count the columns. Roughly as many weaknesses as advantages — and yet Priya's bakery can be perfectly viable, even thriving.

That's the lesson of the table, and it's the opposite of what most beginners take from a competitor analysis. You are not trying to win every row. You can't be cheaper and better and broader and faster. What you need is a coherent position: a small set of rows where you're genuinely, visibly better (product, service, agility, reach), enough parity elsewhere to stay credible, and a deliberate acceptance of being worse on the rows customers don't care about.

Look at where her advantages sit: the ones that are hard to buy. Her rival can lease a bigger shop tomorrow. Building a team that stays, a reputation that travels by word of mouth, and the freedom to decide something on a Tuesday morning — those take years and can't be copied with a purchase order. That's not sentimentality; it's the most durable kind of advantage there is.

And notice the row that decides the whole fight: financials. Every advantage Priya has is worth something only if she survives long enough to use it. A rival with a parent company's balance sheet can run at a loss for eighteen months waiting for her to blink. That's the Competitors chapter and the Financials chapter shaking hands.

Competition is a tax on profit — and a receipt for it

Here's the paradox worth holding on to as you move through the rest of this book.

Competition is what stops you earning fat profits. It's also the proof that your market is worth being in. A market with no competitors is usually a market with no money in it — or one protected by something: a patent, a licence, a geography, a brand so strong nobody bothers trying.

Economists put it neatly, and it's worth saying plainly: profit attracts entry, and entry competes profit away. In a market with no barriers, rivals keep arriving until returns fall back to the bare cost of capital. Every above-average profit is both an invitation and a countdown.

Which means the strategic question is never "how do I get rid of competition?" It's:

What is stopping this market from competing my profit away — and can I build more of it?

That "what is stopping it" has a name: a barrier, or if you prefer the metaphor, a moat. Scale. Brand. A locked-up supply of the best raw material. A prime location. A switching cost. A patent. A culture that keeps excellent people. A distribution network nobody else can replicate.

Read that list again and notice something. Every moat is built out of the other factors. Competitors are the jury that decides whether your version of each factor actually counts for anything. You do the work in the other nineteen chapters; the twentieth tells you whether it mattered.

Competitors as a factor in their own right

Before the takeaways, four ways the competitor factor behaves like something you actively manage rather than a background condition.

Competitors can be bought and sold. You can't purchase the economy. You can buy a rival. Acquisitions, mergers, a bigger group absorbing the shop down the road — when a competitor changes hands, your landscape can change in a week, and the new owner often has deeper pockets and different ambitions. Watch your rivals' ownership as closely as their products.

Competitors can be customers, suppliers and partners. Rivals buy from each other when capacity is short. They supply each other. They share industry standards, trade bodies and training schemes. This is coopetition, and it's entirely normal. It's also where the nastiest competitive shocks come from — a supplier who starts making your product, a big customer who starts making it themselves. The threat rarely arrives from the businesses that look like you; it arrives from the ones that used to just serve you.

Competitors set your price ceiling. You don't decide your price. You discover the top of the range, and the range is set by what the buyer could choose instead. That's the supply-and-demand machinery from the Market chapter, with a face on it.

Competitors are the reason profit stays scarce. Which is why "business is going well" should always be followed immediately by "so who's about to notice, and what will they do about it?"

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Read your business twice. Once as a list of factors — costs, staff, suppliers, product. Once as a comparison against rivals, factor by factor. The first view tells you what you have. The second tells you whether it's worth anything.
  • Don't try to win every row. You cannot be cheaper and better and faster and broader. Choose where to be genuinely distinctive, where "good enough" will do, and be honest about the rest.
  • Advantage lives in the gap, not the absolute number. Stop asking whether a factor is good. Ask whether it's better than the alternative your customer could pick.
  • Compete on cost, not price, where products are interchangeable. A price cut gets matched in a week. A permanently lower cost base is something a rival has to earn.
  • Watch for threats arriving from the other factors. Suppliers, customers and distributors are all one decision away from becoming competitors.
  • Treat the shared factors as an exam, not weather. The economy, tax and regulation hit everyone. Who absorbs them best is the competitive question — and regulation can be a moat if you're the one who can afford it.
  • Build what can't be bought. Anything purchasable will be purchased by someone with more money. Culture, reputation, accumulated skill, relationships, data and location are the assets that survive a well-funded rival.
  • Remember what you're competing for. Not to beat a rival — to earn a profit. Crushing the shop down the road in a market with no margin is a victory nobody wants.
  • And use the one factor that can see all nineteen at once. Management is the only factor that can survey the whole list, decide where to fight, and change course when the check says you were wrong. Competitors are the reason you need it.

Summary

  • Competitors are the only factor with an opposing goal. Every other factor is indifferent, supportive, or inert. That alone is why strategy exists — a business with no rivals needs administration, not strategy.
  • They are also the yardstick for everything else. No other factor's numbers mean anything in isolation; they mean something only compared with a rival's version.
  • The other factors connect to competitors in four ways: you fight through them (product, price, sales, service, stores, distribution), you fight for them (labour, staff, suppliers, raw materials, locations, capital), you face them together (economy, tax, regulations), and you fight in the market that competitors themselves help define.
  • Advantage lives in the gap, not in the absolute number. And what you fight for gets more expensive as rivals bid for the same scarce things.
  • Shared factors — economy, tax, regulation — aren't neutral. They're a test everyone sits, and relative resilience is the score.
  • The company is the vehicle you compete with, and its legal form and balance sheet set how big a bet you can take in every other factor.
  • Competitors behave like a factor in their own right: they can be bought and sold, they can be customers, suppliers and partners, they set your price ceiling, and they are the mechanism that keeps profit scarce.
  • Competition is both a tax on profit and a receipt for it. The strategic question is never how to eliminate it, but what stops it competing your margin away — and every moat is built out of the other nineteen factors.
  • You don't have to win every row. You have to be coherent, distinctive where it matters, and solvent long enough for the advantages to compound.
