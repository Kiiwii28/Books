14-3 Staff as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 14 Staff

Staff, so far, has been the people who supply the effort your business runs on. That framing is useful up close — it tells you what you're actually buying when you hire someone. But pull the camera back and something bigger appears.

Every other factor in this book is inert until a person picks it up. Raw materials sit in a warehouse. Machines wait for somebody to switch them on. Money does nothing in an account. Even your systems and processes are just documents until a human being follows them. Staff aren't one factor sitting alongside the rest — they're the factor that operates all the rest. Which means a decision about your people is really a decision about your costs, your quality, your service, your cash and your competitive position, all at once.

One thread, many functions

Here's the picture worth holding.


Notice that staff points at both sides of the income statement at the same time — payroll is a cost, and everything else in that list generates revenue. That double role is exactly what makes people decisions so strategically interesting, and it's why "how much do they cost?" is such a bad opening question.

Notice something else too. None of those boxes is a department. A single person on a single shift might be doing three at once: making the product, serving the customer, and quietly carrying the know-how of how the place actually works. From the inside, staff looks like a function. From the customer's side, your staff are the company. Every seam the Parts of a Company section warned about is a place where two members of staff hand work to each other.

Staff carry what the accounts can't see

Look at a wage bill and you see hours. That's the Labour chapter's view, and it's a useful one — hours are perishable and they can be measured. But what you actually lose when someone leaves is far more than hours.

  • Tacit knowledge — the know-how that was never written down. Which oven runs hot. Which supplier's delivery arrives early, and why that matters. Which customer wants their loaf sliced.
  • Relationships — with customers, suppliers, the bank, the inspector. These belong to a person, not to your business, however much you'd like to believe otherwise.
  • Judgement — the accumulated sense of when to bend a rule and when not to. It's what separates twenty years of experience from twenty years of attendance.
  • The training you already paid for — every hour you invested, walking out of the door.

None of that appears on a balance sheet. All of it lands in the profit and loss account eventually, usually at the worst possible moment. Which gives you the staffing version of the Suppliers chapter's test: if this person left on Friday, what would I lose by Monday?

Quality and service both start with people

Here's where the Product chapter pays off. A brand is a promise; the product is the keeping of it. Somebody keeps that promise every day, and it's a person, not a machine.

Two things follow. Your ceiling — how good the product can be — is set by what your people can do. The finest oven in the world is useless to a baker who can't tell when dough is ready. And your floor — how bad it gets on a bad day — is set by how well trained and well supported they are. A strong team at 90% effort beats a weak team at 100%, every week of the year.

Consistency is the harder half. Customers forgive an average product far more readily than an inconsistent one, and inconsistency is rarely a machine problem. It's a training problem, a rota problem, or a management problem wearing overalls.

Then there's the service side. The Customer Service chapter argued that satisfaction lives in the gap between what a customer expected and what they got. That gap is closed or opened by a person, in a two-second window, with no manager present, every single time. And here's the uncomfortable bit: people serve customers roughly the way they're treated themselves. Under-trained, over-stretched staff who get hung out to dry do not walk into a customer's day and make it better.

The money: cost, revenue and, above all, timing

Cost. For most service businesses and plenty of others, payroll is the largest single cost line. Remember the split from Costs: salaried staff are broadly fixed (you pay them whether it's busy or not), hourly staff are more variable. And the honest figure isn't the wage — it's the fully loaded cost: wages plus payroll taxes, training, equipment, uniforms, insurance, and the productivity dip while someone learns. Two people on identical salaries can cost you very different amounts.

Revenue. Staff don't only cost money, they earn it. Revenue per head is one of the bluntest and most useful numbers in business — Priya's bakery turning over £156,000 with four staff is earning £39,000 per person. Track it over time. Growth that doesn't move it usually means you're adding people simply to stand still.

Timing. This is the part that catches people out. Wages go out on a fixed rhythm — weekly, fortnightly, monthly — with no flexibility whatsoever. Your customers pay you whenever they get round to it. That mismatch is the working-capital squeeze the Financials chapter describes, and payroll is the least forgiving bill a business has, because it cannot be delayed and everybody notices immediately when it is.

You compete for people on the same street

There's a quiet symmetry that beginners miss. The Market chapter described a labour market with employers as buyers and workers as sellers, and the Competitors chapter warned that you're fighting the same firms on that street. Put them together: your best candidate is usually somebody else's employee, and your best employee is somebody else's candidate.

A rival who pays more, trains better, or offers flexibility can drain your team without ever taking a single customer from you — and when your head baker leaves for them, your product quality follows. Meanwhile the wider weather is set by others: inflation pushes wage expectations up, the Economy chapter decides whether your inbox is full or empty of applicants, and Tax and Regulations quietly attach employment law, minimum wage, working time and health-and-safety duties to every hire you make. There's no hire without those strings attached.

Worked example: what it costs to lose a head baker

Dario has been Priya's head baker for four years. A café chain two streets away offers him £2,000 a year more, and he takes it. Watch what actually walks out of the door.

What it cost to replace Dario Cost
Recruitment — advert, agency, and Priya's weekends reading CVs £900
Training the replacement, at reduced output £1,800
Lower output and higher waste during the ramp-up £2,400
Quality problems — replacement loaves, complaints, goodwill £700
Regulars who drifted during the six-week wobble £2,800
Total £8,600

And that's before you count the six weeks Priya spent doing the 4am bake herself, on top of everything else she was already doing.

Now the uncomfortable question. Would a £2,000 pay rise have kept him? Probably. Which means Priya spent £8,600 avoiding a £2,000 conversation — roughly four pounds for every one she saved.

Two honest caveats, because this isn't an argument for simply paying everybody more. You can't always keep people with money, and a business that pays above market for every role eventually prices itself out of its own market. But the arithmetic of replacement is almost always worse than the arithmetic of retention, and most owners never actually run the numbers.

Look at where those costs fall: Costs (recruitment, training, waste), Manufacturing (the ramp-up lag), Product (quality), Customer Service (complaints), Revenue (drifted regulars), Financials (the cash hit), and Competitors (the rival who hired him). One person, seven chapters.

The loop that compounds — or unwinds

Put it all together and you get the same reinvestment cycle that has run through this whole book, with people at the centre of it.

Invest in staff — decent pay, proper training, enough people on shift, the authority to solve problems — and they deliver better product and better service. Better service keeps customers, and keeping customers is far cheaper than winning them. That shows up as revenue and profit, which funds the next round of investment in staff. Turn by turn, it gets stronger.

Run it backwards — squeeze the training, stretch the rota, ignore the rota requests, hire in a panic — and it unwinds just as reliably. It's slower, which is precisely why it's harder to spot. This is the management loop from the opening chapter, and it's why leading deserves a job of its own: it's the one that leaves no paper trail, and the one employees notice most when it's missing.

One genuinely cheering consequence. Culture is the one competitive advantage a small business can build that a bigger rival can't simply buy. A competitor can clone your prices this afternoon and your product by next season. Copying the way your people treat each other, and therefore your customers, takes years — and by then you've moved on.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Ask what each person is worth, not what they cost. The question isn't the salary; it's the contribution they generate and the knowledge they hold.
  • Count the replacement cost before you need it. Recruitment, training, ramp-up, waste and drift usually add up to several times the pay rise that would have prevented it.
  • Measure revenue per head. It's the simplest productivity signal you have, and it works at every size of business.
  • Remember payroll's timing. You pay your people on a schedule that doesn't wait for your customers to pay you. That's a planning problem, not a surprise.
  • Build the culture on purpose. How staff are treated is how customers get treated, and the chain runs through every seam in the company.
  • Plan capacity ahead of demand. Hiring, training and reaching full productivity all take weeks — the same lag that applies to machines and deliveries applies to people, and it bites hardest when you're busy.
  • Watch the market you're hiring in. Wages, rival employers, demographics and employment rules all move, and none of them consult you first.

Summary

  • Staff are the factor that operates every other factor — materials, machines, money and systems are all inert until a person acts on them.
  • Staff drive both sides of the income statement: payroll is a cost, and product, service, sales and relationships all generate revenue.
  • People carry tacit knowledge, relationships and judgement that never appear on a balance sheet but leave with them — so ask what you'd lose by Monday if someone walked out on Friday.
  • Staff set the quality ceiling and the consistency floor, and they are the service: satisfaction is a gap closed or opened by a person every day. How you treat staff is how they treat customers.
  • The money has three faces: payroll's cost, the revenue staff generate, and the timing mismatch between paying wages and being paid.
  • You compete for people against the same rivals you compete for customers against — and wage expectations, employment law, tax and the wider economy all move the goalposts.
  • Replacing someone usually costs many times more than retaining them; retention isn't sentiment, it's arithmetic.
  • Culture is the one advantage a smaller business can build that a larger rival cannot simply buy — and it's the management loop from the opening chapter, turning steadily in either direction.
