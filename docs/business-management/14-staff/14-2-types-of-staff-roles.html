14-2 Types of Staff Roles

Part of 14 Staff

Ask a small business owner to describe their team and you'll get a list of names. Ask what each of them is actually for — what they decide on their own, what they're measured on, what breaks if they leave — and the list suddenly gets shorter.

That gap is what this subsection is about. Not job titles, but roles.

A role is a bundle, not a title

A role is the bundle of work, decisions and expectations attached to a position in a business — independent of whoever happens to be filling it.

Break that open and three things are inside:

  • Tasks and duties — what actually gets done. Bake the bread. Ring the customers. File the return.
  • Authority — what this person may decide without asking. Refund £20? Yes. Refund £2,000? Not alone.
  • Expectations — the standard it's held to, and how anyone would know. On time, no waste, no complaints, under budget.

Two people with the same job title can hold completely different roles. Plenty of bakers bake; one of them is also the person who decides the morning's production quantities. And two people with different titles can hold the same role: at a three-person firm, the "office manager" and the "operations lead" are often doing one job between them.

This is the same distinction the Labour chapter drew between people and effort. Staff are the people; labour is what they supply; a role is what you've agreed the effort is for.

Why bother sorting roles into types at all? Because the type predicts three things you care about:

  • What it costs — and whether that cost rises with sales or sits there in a quiet month.
  • How easily it's covered — could you replace it next week, or not for six months?
  • How it's managed — what you can honestly measure it on.

Get the type wrong and you'll measure a creative role on output, a production role on charm, and a support role on nothing at all. That last one is how headcount quietly creeps.

Four questions sort every role

You can slice a workforce a dozen ways. Four cuts do nearly all the work, and every real role sits at a point on all four at once.


Change any one and you change the cost, the hiring pool and the management style.

Cut 1: By function — what the work actually is

This is the cut most people mean when they say "what do you do?" And it's the same set of parts you met in The Parts of a Company, now with names attached.

Function Core or support Typical roles Sensible measure
Operations / production Core Baker, machinist, driver, chef Output per hour, waste, on-time delivery
Sales and marketing Core Salesperson, account manager, marketer Revenue, conversion rate, cost per sale
Product and development Core Designer, developer, engineer Launches shipped, cycle time, adoption
Finance Support Bookkeeper, credit controller, analyst Accuracy, days to get paid, cash position
People (HR) Support Recruiter, trainer, payroll Time to hire, turnover, training completed
Procurement Support Buyer, stock controller Cost per unit, supplier reliability, stockturn
Admin, legal and IT Support Receptionist, paralegal, IT support Uptime, response time, compliance

Two reminders from earlier chapters. Core and support is not a ranking — it's a statement about whether the role touches the product or the customer directly. In a bank, IT is core; in a bakery, it's a laptop somebody sorts out on a Sunday. And support roles grow in steps, not smoothly. A firm with three people needs no finance function. A firm with thirty needs a person doing nothing else.

Cut 2: Line and staff — the distinction that confuses everyone

Here's a wrinkle worth knowing, partly because it's a genuine piece of management vocabulary and partly because it's a delightful accident that it appears in a chapter called Staff.

Line roles produce or deliver the thing the customer pays for, and they carry authority over the chain of work. The head baker is a line role. So is the salesperson and the van driver. Line managers can direct the people below them.

Staff roles — in this specific sense — advise, support and check. The bookkeeper, the HR adviser, the health-and-safety officer, the in-house lawyer. Their authority comes from expertise, not command. An accountant can tell you that your margins are heading the wrong way; they can't order the bakery manager to change the recipe.

So "staff" is doing two jobs in this book. In everyday use it means the workforce — all the people. In organisation theory it means the roles that support the line. A line baker and a staff accountant are both "staff" in the first sense and on opposite sides in the second. If a consultant ever looks at you oddly when you use the word, that's why.

Why it matters practically: line roles tend to grow with volume; staff roles tend to grow with complexity — new regulations, new tax rules, new systems, new risks. That's a legitimate reason for them to exist, and it's also why they can multiply faster than revenue unless somebody owns the headcount decision. This is the Costs chapter's fixed-cost problem in human form: a salaried support role is a cost you carry whether you sell one loaf or a thousand.

Cut 3: By level — and how the job itself changes as you rise

Sort roles by how far their decisions reach and you get four levels. The Core Jobs Every Manager Does section showed that the mix of planning, organizing, leading and controlling shifts as you climb. Here's the same shift from the role's point of view.

Level Typical title What they decide Judged on
Front line Baker, sales assistant, driver How today's task gets done Output, quality, customer feedback
Supervisory Shift lead, team leader Who does what, today Team output, problems surfaced early
Functional Head of operations, finance manager How the function runs this quarter Targets, budget, staff retention
Senior Managing director, operations director Direction, resources, structure The whole business

Notice what this table quietly says: a promotion changes the job, not just the pay. The best baker in the building is not automatically a good shift lead, because the shift lead's day is mostly other people's problems. Firms get this wrong constantly, and it costs them a good baker and a bad manager.

Which is why most growing businesses need somewhere for excellent doers to go that isn't management. An individual contributor track — senior baker, principal engineer, lead designer — lets someone get better and better at their craft without being pushed into a role they'll hate. That's not kindness; it's the same talent-market logic the Competitors chapter applies to customers. You compete for good people against the firms on your own street, and the ones who can offer a real future keep them.

Cut 4: By who they touch — customer-facing and back-office

Some roles meet the customer. Some meet the customer's problem second-hand.

Customer-facing roles carry the promise your brand made — the augmented product layer from the Product chapter, delivered live by a human being. Counter staff, drivers, support agents, account managers. These roles are where satisfaction is won or lost, which means their skill, training and mood are strategy, not sentiment.

Back-office roles serve the people who serve the customer. A production planner, a bookkeeper, a warehouse picker. Their "customers" are internal — and internal service is just as real. A credit controller who takes three weeks to answer a query creates two angry customers, only one of whom is external.

And there's the honest complication: almost everyone is customer-facing sometimes. The baker who steps out to explain why the order is late is doing front-line service that day. This is the same seam problem flagged in The Parts of a Company: from inside, six teams are doing six jobs; from outside, it's one experience with your name on it.

Cut 5: By being — and by what they're best at

Two shorter cuts to finish.

How the role is attached to the business. Employee, contractor, agency temp, apprentice, volunteer, family member, owner. You've met the full ladder in the Labour chapter — from commitment and consistency at one end to flexibility and variable cost at the other — so we won't re-teach it here. The role-design question is simply: which attachment does this particular job actually need? A role that carries your quality standard needs someone with a reason to still be there next year. A role that covers a summer rush doesn't.

Specialist or generalist. A specialist does one thing deeply; a generalist does many things adequately. Small firms are built on generalists, because ten hats can't be filled by ten people. Growth changes that, for exactly two reasons the Parts of a Company section named: volume (one person can no longer hold the work) and conflict (sales wanting speed and finance wanting control need separate owners). The moment your generalist "person who does everything admin-y" splits into a bookkeeper and a receptionist, you've paid for specialism with coordination — a new seam to manage.

Every role needs three things designed into it

Before you post a job advert, a role needs answers to three questions:

  1. Purpose — what is this role for? One sentence, in terms of the business outcome, not the tasks.
  2. Measure — how will we know it's going well in a month? One or two numbers, from the table above.
  3. Boundary — what can this person decide alone, and what must come to someone else?

Miss the third and you get either paralysis — everyone asking permission — or chaos, with a counter assistant refunding £200 because nobody said not to. Miss the second and you get the classic complaint: "they're lovely, but I've no idea if they're doing a good job." Miss the first and you've hired a set of tasks with no owner.

This is the organizing job from the management cycle, applied to people: clear roles, clear responsibility, clear authority. When two people each think the other is ordering the flour, that isn't a personality clash. It's a role-design failure.

Worked example: the roles at Priya's bakery, at four sizes

One person. Priya holds every role at once — production, sales, procurement, finance, cleaning. She isn't a generalist by preference; she's a generalist by arithmetic. The Labour chapter's point applies: she supplies an enormous amount of effort with no staff at all.

Three people. Dario bakes (operations, line). Mel bakes and covers the counter (operations plus customer-facing). A freelance bookkeeper files the returns (finance, staff role, contracted). Note what just happened: the first role Priya bought in was a support role, not a production one — because the bookkeeping was the work she was worst at and most likely to get wrong.

Eleven people. Now roles have split and become visible.

Role Function Line or staff Facing Good day looks like
Head baker (Dario) Operations Line Back 100 loaves, under 2% waste
Shift lead Operations Line, supervisory Back Rota covered, problems raised early
Counter staff Service and sales Line Front Queue moving, regulars greeted
Wholesale account manager Sales Line Front New café contracts signed
Bookkeeper (2 days) Finance Staff Back Numbers right, paid on time
Part-time buyer Procurement Staff Back Flour in, price held, no stockouts

Six roles, six different measures — and note that the shift lead produces nothing at all. That's indirect labour in the Labour chapter's language, and it's the first role a small business resists hiring. So here's the arithmetic Priya runs when she finally takes one on.

A shift lead costs about £2,200 a month, fully loaded with tax and contributions — call it £2,800. That's a pure cost, twelve months a year, whether the oven is hot or not. What it buys is roughly 25 hours a month of Priya's own attention: she stops covering shifts, stops fixing rota gaps, stops being the person who unlocks the door. She spends that attention opening a second site, which — on the numbers in the Manufacturing chapter — contributes around £1,600 a month once it passes break-even.

So the role isn't justified by what the shift lead produces. It's justified by what Priya can now do instead. That's the same "hire, automate or outsource" trade-off from the Labour chapter, and it's why a role's value has to be judged against the alternative — not against a wage bill sitting alone in a spreadsheet.

Forty people, two sites. Now there are functional heads, a named finance person, a part-time HR role handling rotas and hiring, and — Priya's new problem — a shortage of generalists. Everyone is a specialist, and nobody knows how the whole thing fits together. That's the seam problem arriving with a payroll attached, and her fix isn't a new role. It's a person whose role is to sit across the boundaries.

Three traps worth naming

Promoting your best doer into a role they don't want. The craftsperson and the supervisor are different jobs. Offer a path that doesn't require leaving the craft.

Letting support roles grow unwatched. Every staff role is a fixed cost, and each new rule, system or regulation spawns a plausible reason for one. Justify each by what it prevents or enables, not by how busy the person looks.

Designing roles around people rather than work. It's comfortable in the short term and disastrous in the long term, because when the person leaves the role leaves with them and nobody can say what's missing. Design the role, then find the human — not the other way round.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Think in roles, then in people. A role is what the business needs; a person is who supplies it. Confusing the two is how you end up with a job nobody can describe.
  • Use the four cuts as a checklist every time you create, review or replace a role: what work, how far it reaches, who it touches, how it's attached.
  • Sort line from staff, and know which one grows with volume and which with complexity. Both are legitimate; only one tends to creep.
  • Build a path for specialists. If your best doer has nowhere to progress except management, you'll lose a good doer or create a poor manager — sometimes both.
  • Match role types to demand certainty. Permanent where demand is steady, flexible where it isn't. The Costs chapter's fixed-versus-variable split, applied to headcount.
  • Give every role a purpose, a measure and a boundary. That's the organizing half of the management cycle, and it's the cheapest performance improvement available to you.
  • Watch the seams, not just the boxes. Most staffing problems show up as friction between roles — Sales promising what Operations can't deliver — not as failure inside one.
  • Measure the mix, not just the headcount. Wage cost as a percentage of revenue, output per labour hour, revenue per front-line role. A team whose shape drifts out of step with the strategy will look busy and quietly lose money.

Summary

  • A role is the bundle of tasks, authority and expectations attached to a position — separate from the person filling it, and more useful than a job title.
  • Four cuts sort every role: by function (operations, sales, finance, people, procurement, admin), by level (front line, supervisory, functional, senior), by contact (customer-facing or back-office), and by attachment (employee, contractor, agency, volunteer, owner).
  • Line roles deliver the product and carry authority down the chain; staff roles advise, support and check, wielding expertise rather than command. Both are "staff" in the everyday sense — a word doing two jobs.
  • Line roles grow with volume; support roles grow with complexity, which is why they need watching. Every one is largely a fixed cost.
  • Promotion changes the job, not just the pay. Excellent doers need somewhere to go that isn't management.
  • Specialists and generalists suit different stages of growth. Roles split because of volume and conflict — and every split creates a new seam to manage.
  • Every role needs a purpose, a measure and a boundary. Most staffing failures are design failures, not people failures.
  • The strategist's habit: choose the type of role deliberately, price it against the alternative rather than in isolation, and review whether your mix still matches what the business is trying to be.
