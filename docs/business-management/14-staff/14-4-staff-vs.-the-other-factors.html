14-4 Staff vs. The Other Factors

Part of 14 Staff

The factor that touches everything

Here's the odd thing about staff as a business factor: it doesn't sit next to the other factors so much as underneath all of them. A supplier relationship only exists because someone negotiates it. A machine only produces because someone operates it. A sale only closes because someone asks for the money. Take the staff out and every other factor in this book becomes a noun with nothing happening to it.

So the honest answer to "how does staff compare with the other factors?" is that staff is the only factor with agency. Raw materials, machinery, cash and even regulations are inert — they do what they're told (or what physics and law dictate). Staff choose. They can care or not care, learn or stagnate, stay or walk out the door on a Friday. That makes staff a multiplier on everything else: great staff can make a mediocre strategy work, and poor staff can wreck an excellent one.

There's a second asymmetry worth holding onto. Every other factor tends to wear out or depreciate. A delivery van loses value from the day you buy it. Staff, used well, appreciate — a junior hire becomes a senior one, and the asset you paid £28,000 for is suddenly worth £60,000 of capability to you without you buying anything new. That's why the comparisons below almost always come back to the same question: are your people amplifying the other factors, or quietly leaking value out of them?

Staff vs. Company and Management

The Company chapter treats a company as a legal and structural thing — a name, a constitution, a set of parts. Staff is what turns that structure into a living organism. You can register a company in an afternoon; you cannot assemble a functioning team in an afternoon. The "parts of a company" that chapter describes (operations, finance, marketing and so on) are really just labels for groups of staff doing particular jobs.

Management deserves special attention here because it's a separate factor in this book, and the line between the two is genuinely blurry. The cleanest way to draw it: management is the system and the decisions; staff are the people who execute them. Your best salesperson is staff. The person who decides which territories to cover, how to price, and how to coach that salesperson is managing — even if it's the same human being wearing both hats. In a five-person business, everyone is both. As you grow, the two roles separate, and the separation creates a classic tension: managers optimise for the numbers, staff optimise for getting through the day. A strategist watches for that gap, because it's where instructions go in one end and something different comes out the other.

Culture is the connective tissue. It's the unwritten answer to "how do we do things here when nobody's watching?" — and it's set far more by who you hire and promote than by anything written on a wall.

Staff vs. Labour — the distinction that matters most

This is the comparison students most often fumble, so let's be precise. Labour is the broad, economy-wide supply of human work: the pool of people able and willing to work, the hours available, the skills on offer, the going wage. It's a market condition, like the weather. Staff are the specific individuals you have actually recruited into your organisation, with names, contracts, roles and relationships.

Think of it as fishing. Labour is the lake — how many fish, of what size, and how hungry they are. Staff are what's in your boat. You can't change the lake, but you choose where to cast, what bait to use, and what you do with the catch once it's aboard. A tight labour market (low unemployment, few candidates) drives up your staff costs whether you like it or not; a loose one gives you choice you didn't have last year.

The strategist's move is to treat labour supply as a planning input and staffing as a decision. If labour is scarce in your region, you have three levers: pay more, train people others won't train, or redesign the work so it needs fewer people. Only the third is permanent.

Staff vs. Market and Competitors

Your staff are your only sensory organs in the market. Market research reports tell you what happened; a salesperson or support agent tells you what's happening today. In the Market chapter, you learned about buyers, sellers and the space between them — that space is filled by people. Front-line staff are the ones who hear the objection that never makes it into the survey.

Against Competitors, staff are simultaneously your weapon and your vulnerability. Competitors don't just compete on price and product; they compete for the same labour pool, and often for your people specifically. Losing a star account manager to a rival can cost you the accounts she was managing, because in many businesses the relationship lives in a person, not in a CRM. That's a strategic risk most balance sheets never show.

A practical habit: whenever you assess a rival, ask two extra questions. Who are their best three people, and are those people happy? You'll often learn more about a competitor's next 18 months from a recruiting ad than from their annual report.

Staff vs. Product, Sales and Customer Service

These three factors are almost pure staff output. A product's quality is the accumulated care of everyone who designed, made and checked it. A sale is a conversation someone had. Customer service is staff behaviour — there is no other ingredient.

This is where the service-profit chain idea earns its keep: better-treated staff produce better customer experiences, which produce better retention and revenue, which funds better staff treatment. It runs in reverse just as fast. Cut pay, stretch shifts, ignore complaints, and the cynicism shows up in your customer reviews within weeks.

Worked example. Two cafés on the same street, same beans, same £3.20 flat white. Café A pays baristas £11.50 an hour, trains them for two weeks, and lets them comp a drink when something goes wrong. Café B pays minimum wage, trains for two days, and makes staff ask a manager before any refund. Café B's wage bill is maybe 6% lower. But its staff turn over every four months, so it's permanently training, its regulars never see a familiar face, and its review score is 3.8 against Café A's 4.6. Café A earns more per square foot. The "saving" wasn't a saving.

Staff vs. Suppliers, Raw Materials, Manufacturing, Distribution and Stores

These are the operational factors, and staff are the hands on every one of them.

  • Suppliers: the difference between a good and a bad purchasing relationship is usually one competent buyer. A buyer who knows the market can capture 5–8% where an inexperienced one accepts the first quote.
  • Raw materials: materials don't waste themselves. Scrap, spoilage and shrinkage are staff behaviours — and they're measurable, which makes them fixable.
  • Manufacturing: yield, downtime and quality defects are people problems wearing machine costumes. A skilled operator notices the wobble before the scrapped batch.
  • Distribution: drivers are your brand at the customer's door. A delivery that arrives late and thrown over the gate undoes a year of marketing.
  • Stores: store staff determine conversion rate, merchandising quality and shrinkage. Two identical shops with different teams will produce different revenue per square foot, and the difference is not the fixtures.

Notice the pattern: in every case, the technical factor sets the ceiling and the staff set the achievement. A machine rated at 400 units an hour produces what its operator and maintenance crew let it produce.

Staff vs. Financials, Revenue and Costs

This is where staff stops being a warm, human topic and becomes arithmetic.

For most service businesses, staff is the cost base. Picture a 12-person design agency with an average fully-loaded cost of £55,000 per head — salary, employer contributions, equipment, desk, software. That's £660,000 of a £900,000 cost base: roughly three-quarters of everything the business spends. A 10% productivity gain across the team is worth about £66,000 — which, in a business running at a 10% net margin, is the equivalent of winning £660,000 of new revenue.

That symmetry is the whole argument. Financials are the scoreboard, Revenue is what staff effort converts into, and Costs are mostly staff effort itself. It also explains why hiring is the highest-leverage decision most managers make and why they make it so casually.

Two numbers every strategist should track: revenue per employee (are people producing more or less over time?) and staff turnover cost (recruiting, onboarding, and the productivity dip while a replacement learns). As a rough illustration, replacing someone on a £30,000 salary might cost you £10,000–£20,000 all-in — which means a team of 40 losing eight people a year is quietly burning £100,000+ that never appears as a line item called "churn."

Careful, though: cheap staff and expensive staff can both destroy profit. The failure mode is paying below-market wages and calling the resulting savings "margin," when you've actually bought worse service, higher turnover and a smaller revenue line.

Staff vs. Economy, Tax and Regulations

Three external factors shape your staff more than you'd like.

Economy. Unemployment, wage inflation, migration policy and interest rates all move the price of people. When the economy runs hot, staff have options and you pay for retention. When it cools, you gain choice but your customers tighten spending at the same time — so cheap hiring rarely arrives when you need it most.

Tax. Staff come with a tax tail. There's the income tax and social contributions you withhold from their pay, and the employer-side payroll taxes you pay on top, plus obligations around benefits, pensions, and reporting. A hire costing £40,000 in salary may cost £46,000–£48,000 in real money. Any staffing plan that ignores the tax wedge is a plan with a hole in it.

Regulations. Employment law is the densest rulebook most small businesses ever touch: minimum wage, working hours, breaks, health and safety, discrimination, dismissal procedure, parental leave, union rights. Get it wrong and the cost is legal, financial and reputational. But notice the flip side — compliance is itself a staff task. Somebody in your business has to know these rules, and if nobody does, you're relying on luck.

Now zoom out: the Economy, Tax and Regulations chapters all describe constraints that apply to every factor. Staff is simply the factor where they bite hardest, because you're dealing with human beings who have rights, families and alternatives.

Putting it together: how a strategist uses this

When you look at staff through the lens of the other factors, four habits follow.

  1. Price the whole person, not the salary. Total cost includes tax, benefits, equipment, training, management time and turnover risk.
  2. Look for multiplier roles. A great operations manager or buyer can lift five other factors at once. Those hires deserve disproportionate care.
  3. Treat turnover as a leak, not a fact of life. Measure what it costs you in recruiting, lost productivity and lost customer relationships.
  4. Ask where staff are the bottleneck. If suppliers, machinery and demand are all fine but output is flat, the constraint is almost always a person — or the way a person is managed.

Summary

  • Staff is the only factor with agency; every other factor is inert until a person acts on it, which makes staff a multiplier on all the rest.
  • Unlike machines and materials, staff appreciate with good use — skills compound.
  • Labour is the economy-wide pool; staff are the people you actually hired. Labour sets your cost; staffing is your decision.
  • Management is the system and the decisions; staff execute. Both matter, and the gap between them is where strategy quietly fails.
  • Staff deliver Product, Sales and Customer Service almost single-handedly, and determine how well Suppliers, Raw Materials, Manufacturing, Distribution and Stores actually perform.
  • In most service businesses, staff is the cost base, so productivity and turnover are financial numbers, not HR trivia.
  • Economy, Tax and Regulations hit staff harder than any other factor — and somebody on your team has to be responsible for knowing them.

Read this alongside the other "vs. the other factors" sections and a pattern appears: staff keeps showing up on both sides of the equation. That's not a flaw in the model. It's the point. Everything in a business is run by people, which is why getting the people right is the closest thing to a guaranteed return a strategist can find.
