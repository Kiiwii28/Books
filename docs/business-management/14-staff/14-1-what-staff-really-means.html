14-1 What Staff Really Means

Part of 14 Staff

Staff are not the same thing as hours

Ask a bakery owner how many staff she has and she'll answer instantly: "three, plus me." Ask her who works in the building on a Saturday morning and the number jumps. Ask her who she's legally responsible for and it changes again.

So before we go any further in this chapter, let's pin the word down — because "staff" gets used for at least three different things, and the answers only diverge exactly when it starts to cost you money.

The definition worth keeping

Staff are the people who work inside a business and supply its labour — employed, contracted, volunteering, or family helping out.

Four pieces of that are doing real work.

People. Not hours, not roles, not headcount on a spreadsheet. Actual humans with names, lives, ambitions and a Monday morning. This is the whole reason the word exists separately from labour.

Work inside the business. They're part of your operation, not standing outside it doing deals with you.

Supply its labour. Being there isn't enough — they're there to contribute effort, physical or mental. That's the thread running back to the Labour chapter: labour is the effort; staff are the source of it.

Employed, contracted, volunteering, family. That last phrase is deliberately loose, because the everyday meaning of "staff" is looser than the legal one. Hold that thought — we'll come back to it.

Three words that keep colliding

If you've read the Labour chapter, you've already met this triangle. It's worth one more look from this side, because almost every muddle about workforce management starts here.

Word What it actually is Example
Labour The effort itself — a flow of hours Dario's eight hours shaping dough
Staff The people who supply that effort Dario
Management The work of pointing effort at a goal Priya setting the production target

Dario is staff. His Tuesday is labour. What Priya does about it — the rota, the target, the quality standard — is management.

Two wrinkles worth noticing, because they're where the tidy version breaks down.

Managers are staff too. A supervisor on a payroll is a member of staff who happens to spend their day doing the four management jobs from the opening chapter. So "management versus staff" is a real distinction within an organisation — it's how we talk about who decides what — but it isn't a distinction between two kinds of human being.

Owners might be staff, or might not. Priya owns the bakery and bakes in it, so she's both. A shareholder who never visits isn't staff in any sense. And here's the trap: when the person who owns the business also works in it, that fact can hide for years — fifty unpaid hours a week quietly propping up a business that looks profitable on paper. The Labour chapter's worked example found exactly that.

So who actually counts?

This is the boundary question, and there's no single clean answer — which is precisely why it's worth thinking about rather than assuming.

Person Colloquially staff? Legally employed? What they are
Dario, full-time baker Yes Yes Staff, plainly
Agency temp covering a sick day Yes — she's in your kitchen No — the agency employs her Staff you direct but don't employ
Bookkeeper, two days a month Probably yes Usually no A regular, semi-inside helper
Plumber fixing the oven No No A supplier
Priya's brother-in-law, free on Sundays Yes No Labour without a contract
Priya herself Yes, and no No Owner and worker at once

The honest summary: "staff" is a role inside the business, not a legal category. In everyday speech, anyone who turns up regularly and works under your direction is staff. In law, that's decided by employment law — not by what you call them, what their contract says, or how you think of them.

Which matters, and not in an abstract way. The legal line decides who you withhold tax for, who has employment rights, who you must insure, and who you owe health-and-safety duties to — the whole apparatus of the Regulations chapter. Call someone a contractor because it's cheaper and simpler, while actually treating them as staff, and you haven't simplified anything. You've stored up a problem.

The practical test, if you want one: does this person work inside my business, under my direction, on an ongoing basis? If yes, treat them as staff in every sense that matters, whatever the paperwork says.

The internal party

Here's the idea that makes staff strategically interesting rather than just administratively fiddly.

Everyone else your business deals with stands outside it. Customers buy from you and leave. Suppliers sell to you and leave. Lenders, regulators, landlords — all outside, all at arm's length. Staff are the only group on the inside.


Two consequences fall out of that.

First, staff are the only people who see the mess. A customer sees one bad experience. A supplier sees one late payment. Only staff see the whole thing — the broken process, the supplier who's always late, the product everyone complains about, the shelf nobody ever restocks. That's intelligence sitting inside your building, and most businesses never ask for it.

Second, staff carry your promises. Everything the Product and Customer Service chapters insisted on — consistency, the brand as a promise, service that happens at every touchpoint — is delivered by a person. Nobody buys a promise from a company. They buy it from whoever is standing in front of them.

Worked example: how many staff does Priya have?

Back to the bakery. Priya says "three staff." Let's write out who actually works in her business in an ordinary week.

Who What they do Staff?
Priya Bakes, orders, plans, locks up Yes, in every way except the payroll
Dario Head baker, full-time Yes — employed, insured, taxed
Mel Baker, full-time Yes — employed
Two Saturday students Counter and cleaning Yes — part-time, employed
An agency temp, when someone's ill Covers the shift Inside her kitchen, but the agency employs her
Bookkeeper Two days a month A regular, but formally self-employed
Brother-in-law Fixes the oven, unpaid Labour, not staff

Now look at the consequences of that blur. Her obligations, her insurance, her tax paperwork and her right to tell someone what to do are all different across those rows. Her rota is one thing; her legal exposure is another; her actual "team" in the everyday sense is a third.

And notice the strategic shape underneath. She has a core of two permanent people who hold the recipes and the standards, a flexible fringe of temps and freelancers for the spikes, and a grey zone of helpers and relatives. That's not sloppiness — it's a sensible response to demand that rises and falls. It just needs to be a deliberate response, not an accidental one.

The only input that gets better with use

One more thing, and it's the reason this chapter exists separately from Labour.

Machines wear out. A delivery van depreciates from the moment it's driven off the forecourt. Flour goes stale. Every other thing a business owns gets worse over time, or at best stays the same.

Staff are the exception. A trained baker who's been in your kitchen for three years is not just an older version of the person you hired — she's a faster, more accurate, more confident one. Her judgement has improved. She spots problems earlier. She can train the next person. She is, quite literally, an asset that appreciates.

That's the productivity loop from the Labour chapter, seen from the other end: good staff get better, get better at making things, and make the business better at everything downstream. And it runs in reverse just as easily — a demoralised team, a stretched rota, a training budget cut to save a few pounds this quarter, and your only appreciating asset quietly starts depreciating instead.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Count people, not just posts. Headcount tells you about cost; knowing who these people are tells you about capability, risk and loyalty. They're different questions.
  • Be precise about the boundary — for the right reason. The everyday sense of "staff" is generous; the legal sense is not. Know which one you're talking about, because Tax and Regulations deal in the second.
  • Never treat the owner's labour as free. It's the most common hidden subsidy in small business, and it makes weak businesses look strong.
  • Ask your staff what's broken. They're the only people who can see the seams between your departments — the handoffs the Parts of a Company section warned about.
  • Treat retention as strategy, not sentiment. Staff are the one input that can leave and take what they know to the competitor down the road. That's the Competitors chapter and the Labour chapter meeting on the same street.
  • Remember what you're actually managing. You can schedule labour and you can budget headcount. You can only ever lead people. That's why the four management jobs include one that has nothing to do with numbers.

Summary

  • Staff are the people who work inside a business and supply its labour — whether they're employed, contracted, volunteering, or family helping out.
  • Three words that get muddled: labour is the effort, staff are the people who supply it, management is what directs it. Managers are staff too, and owners may or may not be.
  • "Staff" is a role inside the business, not a legal category. The legal line — employment rights, payroll tax, insurance, safety duties — is set by law, not by what you call someone.
  • The test: does this person work inside my business, under my direction, on an ongoing basis? If so, treat them as staff regardless of the contract label.
  • Staff are the only internal party a business has — everyone else (customers, suppliers, owners, regulators) stands outside. That's why staff see the mess others never see.
  • Their work reaches every other chapter: they build the Product, deliver the Customer Service, make the Sales, run Manufacturing and Distribution, and make up the largest cost line in most service businesses.
  • Staff are the only input that improves with use. Every other asset depreciates. Invest in the one that doesn't.
