20-1 What Tax Really Means for a Business

Part of 20 Tax

Of all the money that leaves a business, one kind leaves without asking permission. You choose your suppliers, your wages, your rent, your van. Tax is the only bill on the list that somebody else writes.

Which is exactly why it deserves its own definition before we go any further.

The definition worth keeping

Tax is a compulsory payment to government, calculated under rules you don't get to negotiate.

Four pieces are carrying that sentence.

  • Compulsory. Not optional, not a suggestion. If you owe it, you owe it, whether or not you agree with how it's spent.
  • To government. National, regional or local.
  • Under rules. A published formula decides what you owe — a rate, a base, a threshold, a deadline.
  • You don't negotiate. No haggling, no "what's your best price", no walking away.

That last point is what makes tax unlike every other cost in this book. Remember the test from the Suppliers chapter — how easily could I walk away? Run it against the tax authority and the answer is: you can't. So the usual strategy of shopping around, squeezing and switching simply isn't available here.

Three roles tax plays in a business

Beginners see tax as one thing — a bill. It's actually doing three different jobs at once, and mixing them up is where most tax trouble starts.

It's a cost. Whatever form it takes, it comes off your results, in the same place rent and wages do. That means it belongs in your pricing, your budgeting and your break-even arithmetic, exactly like the flour costs from the Costs chapter. A price set without tax in it isn't a price; it's a guess.

It's a job you do for the state. This one catches people out. With sales taxes and payroll taxes you aren't really the taxpayer — you're the collector. You add tax to a customer's bill, or deduct it from a wage, hold it for a few weeks and hand it over. That money was never yours. Treating it as yours is one of the classic ways a small business dies.

It's a rule that shapes behaviour. Taxes change what's worth doing. A tax on sugary drinks changes what a drinks maker puts in the can and what a customer pays for it. A tax break on new machinery changes when a business invests. Tax isn't just arithmetic performed on your profit — it's one of the forces reshaping your market, sitting right alongside suppliers, competitors and regulation.

Not one bill, but several — on different bases

We'll sort the individual taxes properly in the next subsection. What matters now is the shape of the thing: your tax bill isn't a single number that arrives at year-end. It's several streams, charged on different bases, on different schedules, and each one behaves like a different kind of cost.

What the tax is charged on How it behaves Cousin in the Costs chapter
Profit Only owed if you make money A share of what's left over
Sales Rises with every transaction Variable, but collected, not borne
Payroll Rises with every hour you buy Sits on top of wages
Property and licences Owed whether you trade or not Fixed cost

That table is worth keeping. A tax on profit rises and falls with success. A tax on premises doesn't care whether anyone walked through the door. And a tax on sales is mostly somebody else's money passing through your hands. Three completely different animals wearing the same word.

Worked example: one month at Priya's bakery

Illustrative numbers, and your country's rates will differ — but the shape is the same everywhere.

Priya takes £12,000 in a month. Ingredients cost £3,600, wages £3,400, rent and everything else £2,500. So her profit before tax is £2,500.

Now watch what happens to that money.

Stream Amount Whose money is it, really?
Sales tax collected at the till ~£2,000 The state's — Priya just holds it briefly
Payroll tax withheld from wages, plus employer contributions ~£900 The state's, plus a genuine cost of employing people
Tax on her profit ~£500 Hers, until it isn't
Left for Priya ~£1,100 Hers

Look at the top two rows and then at the total. Of the £12,000 that came through the door, more than £2,900 was never hers at all. It arrived, it sat in her account for a few weeks, and it left.

That's why the single cheapest discipline in business is this: keep collected tax in a separate account. The business owner who spends the sales tax money on a new oven in March and meets the quarterly payment in April has just discovered, painfully, that the money was never theirs to spend. No interest, no negotiation, no friend at the tax office — just a problem they created by treating a temporary deposit as income.

Tax and profit are not the same as tax and cash

There's a second trap, and it's subtler. Tax is calculated on recorded transactions — but cash is about money actually moving.

You invoice a wholesale customer £3,000 in March. The sale counts, the profit counts, and the tax on it counts. But the customer pays you in June, and in between you've paid tax on money you haven't received yet. That's the Financials chapter's working-capital problem, arriving with a government stamp on it.

Timing also runs the other way. Payroll taxes are usually due within weeks, sales taxes quarterly, and profit tax perhaps a year later, sometimes in instalments. So your tax calendar has its own rhythm, completely separate from your sales rhythm. Miss it and penalties arrive whether or not your customers have paid you.

A good strategist plans tax by the calendar, not by the year-end.

Within the rules, you do have choices

Tax is compulsory, but the amount and the timing are not entirely fixed. You can't make it vanish, but you can decide how much of it you incur and when — legally.

  • Your legal structure. A sole trader and a limited company are taxed differently, on different bases, at different rates. This is a real reason people incorporate, and it connects straight back to the Types of Companies chapter.
  • Where and how you operate. Different regions, different rates, different rules about where tax is owed.
  • Timing. When you buy equipment, when you take income, when you make a pension contribution — all of these move tax between years.
  • What you claim. Allowances and reliefs exist. Unclaimed, they're simply money you gave away.
  • How you register. Thresholds, schemes and elections all change the arithmetic.

The line you must not cross is between avoidance — arranging your affairs within the rules as written — and evasion — hiding income, falsifying records, paying staff off the books. Evasion isn't clever accounting. It brings penalties, interest and, for directors, something worse: the limited liability wall from the Companies chapter has a tax-shaped hole in it, and people do lose their homes through it.

There's also a quieter cost. Tax compliance is part of keeping your promises — to staff, to customers, to the suppliers who trust you. A business caught cheating is a business whose brand promise has been broken, and that's a Product problem as much as a legal one.

Who actually ends up paying?

Here's the question that turns tax from an admin chore into a strategic one. When a tax goes up, who really bears it?

The Market chapter has the answer. If your demand is elastic — customers have lots of close alternatives — you can't simply add the tax to your price. They'll walk to the café down the road, and you'll absorb the cost in a thinner margin. If your product is genuinely differentiated — a specialty item, a wedding cake nobody else in town makes — you can pass more of it on, and the customer bears it instead.

Same tax. Completely different outcome depending on where you sit in the Types of Competition chapter. This is why tax rises hurt commodity businesses far more than differentiated ones.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Budget it like any other cost. Know your rates, your bases and your calendar. A tax bill that surprises you isn't a tax problem, it's a planning failure.
  • Separate collected tax immediately. It isn't your cash. The discipline costs nothing and prevents the most common self-inflicted wound in small business.
  • Choose your structure deliberately. It decides how much tax you pay and when — and it's a decision from Types of Companies, not an accident of paperwork.
  • Price with tax in mind. Ask what the customer pays, what you keep, and what leaves before you do.
  • Never let a tax decision happen by default. "We've always done it that way" is not a tax strategy.
  • Run the loop. Plan for the payments, organize the records and the separate account, lead by treating compliance as part of the business's promise, and control it with a monthly check that what you've set aside matches what you actually owe.

Summary

  • Tax is a compulsory payment to government, calculated under rules you can't negotiate. Unlike every other cost, you can't shop around or walk away.
  • It plays three roles at once: a cost that comes off your results, a collection duty where you hold someone else's money briefly, and a rule that shapes behaviour across your whole market.
  • It's not one bill but several streams — on profit, sales, payroll and property — each charged on a different base and behaving like a different kind of cost.
  • Collected tax was never yours. Keep it separate. Spending it is the classic way small businesses fail.
  • Tax and cash are different. You can owe tax on profit you haven't been paid yet, and payment deadlines follow their own calendar.
  • Within the rules you have real choices — structure, location, timing, claims. Avoidance is legal; evasion isn't, and the limited liability wall has a tax-shaped hole in it.
  • Who really bears a tax depends on your market. Elastic demand means you absorb it; differentiation means you can pass it on.
  • The strategist's habits: budget it, set it aside, choose deliberately, price for it, and check it monthly.
