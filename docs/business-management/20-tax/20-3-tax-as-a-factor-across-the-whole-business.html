20-3 Tax as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 20 Tax

Tax has a reputation as an annual event: something that happens to the accounts in the spring, involving a shoebox, an accountant, and a bill nobody enjoys opening. That picture is wrong in a way that costs businesses real money.

Tax isn't an event at the end of the chain. It's a factor that attaches itself to almost every decision you make along the way — what you charge, what you make, where you buy, where you sell, who you hire, which building you rent, and what legal shape your business wears.

You've already met what tax is and the main types a business pays. Now let's follow it through the business, the same way we followed raw materials, labour, manufacturing, stores and sales.

Tax is a cost — but a strange one

Start with the part that fits what you already know. Tax is a cost, and costs come off profit pound for pound — so it belongs in the Costs chapter alongside flour and wages. But it's three different costs wearing one name.

A share of profit. Whatever your country calls it, this one is calculated on what's left after everything else. It doesn't change any individual decision much, because it scales with the outcome. It does mean one subtle thing: if you pay 20% tax on profit, a pound of genuine extra cost really costs you 80p. Just remember the arrow only points one way. Spending a pound to save 20p is not a saving.

A tax on transactions. Sales tax, VAT, goods and services tax — charged on what you sell, collected from your customer, and handed to the state. You're a middleman here, not the payer. Hold that thought; it's the single most important idea on this page.

A tax on activities. Property taxes on premises, payroll taxes on wages, duties on imported goods, licence fees. These attach to things you do or own, not to what you earn. Many are fixed — you pay them whether or not a single customer walks in.

That third category is the sneaky one, because a fixed tax behaves exactly like rent. It sits inside your break-even calculation, in the arithmetic you met in the Manufacturing chapter, quietly raising the volume you need before you make a penny.

It reaches into what you charge and what you make

When a tax lands on your product, somebody has to swallow it — you, the customer, or a bit of both. Which one it is depends on elasticity, the idea from the Market chapter.

A tax on something with close substitutes tends to come out of your margin, because raising the shelf price just sends buyers to the shop next door. A tax on something people won't switch away from gets passed along with much less grumbling. Same tax, different outcome, and the difference is entirely about how substitutable you are.

Taxes also change what you choose to make. When a levy attaches to a specific ingredient — sugar, alcohol, plastic packaging, fuel — it stops being a finance question and becomes a Product decision: reformulate, resize, repackage, or retire the line. A packaging tax doesn't just cost a bakery money; it pushes it towards paper bags, and paper bags mean a different Supplier.

It reaches into where you buy and where you sell

Duties and import taxes add to landed cost — the number the Suppliers and Raw Materials chapters care about. A tariff can flip a sourcing decision overnight. The cheap mill two borders away stops being cheap, and the local one you'd dismissed becomes the sensible option again.

Location gets pulled in too. Property taxes are charged on premises, so every extra site carries its own fixed tax bill. That's one reason online stores and delivery-only kitchens can undercut shopfronts — not because they're cleverer, but because they carry fewer fixed costs, tax among them. It's the Stores chapter and the fixed-versus-variable split, arriving with a bill attached.

And crossing a border brings registrations, place-of-supply rules and paperwork. Expanding your distribution footprint expands your tax footprint — the point the Distribution chapter made when it warned that growing through distributors rather than direct sales is often a tax decision as much as a commercial one.

It reaches into who you employ and what you own

Advertise a job at £12 an hour and your true cost may be closer to £13.80 once employer contributions are added. That's the Labour chapter's fully-loaded cost, and it's why the honest question is never "can we afford the wage?" but "can we afford the hire?"

It also tempts people into a dangerous shortcut: calling staff "contractors" to avoid payroll taxes. Employment status is decided by law, not by the label on the contract — a Regulations issue that arrives later as back taxes, penalties and interest on the whole arrangement.

The flip side is real too. Many tax systems deliberately reward behaviour the government wants — apprenticeships, training, research, hiring people who've been out of work. Read the tax rules carefully and you'll find the state telling you what it would like you to do. Whether you accept the offer is a strategy decision.

Capital equipment follows the same pattern. Tax systems usually let you deduct the cost of machines over time, which nudges the automation arithmetic from the Manufacturing chapter. It never turns a bad investment into a good one, but it can shift a break-even enough to matter.

Tax is cash you're holding for somebody else

Here's the idea that sinks otherwise healthy businesses.

Money collected as sales tax, or withheld from wages on behalf of the state, is not your money. It's a loan from the tax authority with a due date and a penalty attached.

Priya's loaf sells at £4.00. At a 20% sales tax, £3.33 of that is hers and £0.67 belongs to the state. On a busy Saturday she takes £800 across the counter — and roughly £133 of it was never hers to begin with. If it lands in the general account and quietly pays for flour, wages and a new display case, then the end of the quarter arrives and the bill is still due.

That is the Financials chapter's warning in its purest form: profit is an opinion, cash is a fact, and a tax bill is a fact with a deadline.

Worked example: Priya's second shop

Priya is thinking about opening a second site. Here are the monthly fixed costs.

Per month Ignoring property tax Including property tax
Rent £1,800 £1,800
Wages £3,200 £3,200
Utilities and insurance £400 £400
Property tax on premises — £1,200
Total fixed costs £5,400 £6,600
Contribution per loaf £2.80 £2.80
Break-even loaves 1,929 2,357
Roughly, per day 64 79

Nothing opened. Nothing baked. No wage changed. A fixed tax simply moved the bar by fifteen loaves a day, every day, before the second shop earns a penny of profit.

And that's only the first question. The second is bigger: if premises carry that weight, is a shop the right move at all, or would a pre-order collection point and a wholesale route to cafés carry less? That's the Stores and Distribution decision — and tax just walked into the room and sat down at the table.

It even changes who your competitors are

A rival with a different legal structure pays differently. Cooperatives, non-profits and firms spread across several jurisdictions all face their own treatment, and a large competitor has a tax team a two-person business can't afford. Some of that is legitimate design, some is distortion, and you have to compete inside it regardless.

There's also the harder version. A rival whose low prices are built on not paying what they owe has an advantage you cannot legitimately match — and shouldn't try to. The Regulations chapter's penalties are the least of it; customers, staff and lenders all react to a business that gets caught.

One line is worth drawing clearly. Arranging your affairs to pay what the law requires is ordinary and expected. Lying about them is a crime. Between the two sits a grey zone that's legal but reputationally expensive — and a brand is a promise, which the Product chapter was firm about.

Running it like a management job

Go back to the four jobs from the opening of this book, and tax responds to all of them.

  • Planning. Know your deadlines cold. Forecast the bill. Decide your legal structure before you need to — the Company chapter's sole trader versus limited company choice is a tax decision as much as a legal one.
  • Organizing. Keep records as you go, not in a panic. Your accountant is an indirect Supplier, and the "how easily could I walk away?" test applies to them too.
  • Leading. The owner sets the standard. If "make the numbers look a bit better" is said out loud once, it becomes the culture, and the penalties land on the business.
  • Controlling. Move the collected tax to a separate account monthly, reconcile it, and check the bill against the plan. Same loop, same rhythm, different bill.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Steer it, don't just file it. Any big decision — a price, a site, a supplier, a hire, a machine, a legal structure — has a tax dimension. Ask the question while the decision is still live, not after it's baked in.
  • Don't let the tail wag the dog. A bad decision made for a tax saving is still a bad decision. Tax should bend a choice at the margin, never make it.
  • Treat collected tax as somebody else's cash. Separate account, monthly transfer, never spent on stock.
  • Know the calendar. Penalties and interest are the most avoidable cost in business.
  • Stay well clear of the line — and remember that reputation is an asset, not a line on a form.

Summary

  • Tax isn't a year-end event. It's a factor threaded through price, product, sourcing, location, hiring, equipment and legal structure.
  • It behaves like three costs at once: a share of profit, a transaction tax you collect for the state, and fixed activity taxes that behave like rent.
  • Elasticity decides who really pays a tax on your product — you or your customer — and taxes aimed at ingredients turn into product-design decisions.
  • Duties, registrations and property taxes shape where you buy, where you hold stock and where you open, which is why your tax footprint grows with your distribution footprint.
  • Payroll taxes make the true cost of a hire higher than the wage, and employment status is a legal fact, not a label you choose.
  • Tax you've collected is not profit. It's cash with a deadline, and confusing the two is how profitable businesses run out of money.
  • Tax compliance is also competitive: rivals who don't pay what they owe can't be legitimately matched, and the line between planning and evasion is one to stay well clear of.
  • Manage it with the loop — plan the deadlines and the structure, organize the records and the adviser, lead by setting an honest standard, and control it by setting the cash aside every month.
