20-2 Types of Taxes a Business Pays

Part of 20 Tax

A business doesn't pay tax. It pays taxes — plural, six or seven different ones, collected by different authorities, on different things, at different times. And here's the part that catches people out: some of them aren't even your money. You just happen to be the one who hands them over.

Sorting them into families is worth doing, because each family behaves differently in your accounts. One bites your profit. One is already baked into the price your customers pay. One is a fixed cost you carry whether you sell a thing or not. Knowing which is which is the difference between understanding your business and simply fearing a letter from the tax office.

The distinction that sorts everything: whose money is it?

Before the list, meet the idea that makes the list usable. For every tax, ask two questions:

  • Who pays it legally? (Whoever the law makes responsible for handing it over.)
  • Who actually bears it? (Whose standard of living goes down because the tax exists.)

Very often these are different people. Sales tax is a perfect example. The customer pays it, you collect it, and you hand it over. You were never out of pocket — but you were, for a few weeks, holding money that wasn't yours. That's a cash flow story, not a cost story, and the two live in different chapters of this book.

Compare that with employer payroll taxes. There's nobody else to push those onto. The tax office wants a percentage on top of every wage you pay, and it comes out of your account. That one is unambiguously your cost — which is why the Labour chapter told you that the wage on the contract is never the full cost of the person.

So the families below are sorted by what they tax. But keep asking the second question as you read; it decides what each tax actually does to your business.

The five families of business tax

1. Taxes on profit

The headline one. In most countries a company pays tax on its profit — what's left after costs have been deducted — not on its revenue. In the UK it's corporation tax; in the US, the federal corporate income tax; elsewhere, equivalents with different names and different rates. Illustrative rates in the 15–30% range are common, with smaller profits often taxed at a lower rate.

Three things are worth understanding about this family:

It's a tax on the residual. If you make no profit, you generally pay none — though losses usually carry forward to offset future profits, which is a quiet relief for a business in a bad year.

Costs come out first. Genuine business expenses — materials, wages, rent, equipment, interest — are deducted before profit is measured. This isn't a loophole; it's the definition. Taxing a business on money it spent on flour would be taxing it on nothing.

It doesn't change marginal decisions. Because it only applies if you've already made money, it rarely changes whether a particular sale is worth making. A profitable sale is still profitable at 75p in the pound. What it does change is how much you keep, and therefore how much you have to reinvest.

2. Taxes on sales

VAT, GST, sales tax — the tax added to what the customer pays. The mechanics vary enormously. In much of the world, VAT and GST are multi-stage taxes: each business in the chain charges tax on what it sells and reclaims the tax it paid on what it bought, so only the value added at each step is taxed. In the US, sales tax is typically charged once, at the final retail sale.

For most businesses the practical picture is the same:

  • You charge tax on your sales and hold the money.
  • You reclaim tax on your business purchases.
  • Every so often you send the difference to the tax authority.

The amount you send is often much larger than your profit, which is why the discipline matters. This is money passing through your account, and if you pay it out as though it were trading income, you'll be handing over cash you never earned. Compare it with the Financials chapter's working capital warning — this is the same trap wearing a different coat.

There's usually a registration threshold. Small businesses below a set turnover may not have to register at all. Above it, you're in the system whether you like it or not — and cross that line mid-year and your prices, your admin and your cash flow all change at once.

3. Taxes on employing people

This family is where most small business owners get their biggest surprise. Employing someone triggers at least three separate obligations:

  • Employer payroll taxes — social security, national insurance contributions, or whatever your country calls the levy on top of wages. This is a real cost, above and beyond the wage.
  • Employee income tax, withheld. You don't pay it, but you're legally required to deduct it from your staff's pay and hand it over. (The UK calls this PAYE.)
  • Employee social contributions, likewise deducted and remitted.

Add mandatory pension contributions, apprenticeship levies and insurance in many countries, and the picture is clear: the wage on the contract is only part of the bill. A job advertised at £30,000 might cost closer to £34,000 once employer taxes land. That's the Labour chapter's "fully loaded cost" made concrete — and it's why automation, outsourcing and part-time contracts look different once you price them properly.

And notice the double role you're playing. You're a taxpayer on the employer's share, and a tax collector on the employee's share. Get the second one wrong and the shortfall comes out of your pocket.

4. Taxes on property and assets

Business rates — or commercial property tax, or rates — is a tax on the premises you occupy, charged annually and based on the property's assessed value rather than your profits.

This is the family that behaves most like a fixed cost, and it's worth flagging the strategic consequence. You pay it in a brilliant year and in a terrible one. It doesn't care how much you sell. That makes it exactly the kind of cost the Stores chapter described as "a bet on volume" — and it's why a shop lease or a warehouse purchase commits you to something you'll be paying for regardless of whether the customers turn up.

Alongside it sit transaction taxes on buying property — stamp duty, transfer taxes and the like — and capital gains tax on the profit you make when you sell an asset that has grown in value. That connects straight into Financials, because it turns a simple decision to sell a building or a subsidiary into an arithmetic problem.

5. Taxes on specific goods and transactions

Finally, the narrow ones. These don't tax profit or people — they tax particular products or particular movements of goods.

  • Excise duties — charged per unit on alcohol, tobacco, fuel and gambling. They're built into the price you pay your supplier, which means they arrive in your accounts as a higher input cost, invisible and unlabelled.
  • Sin taxes and environmental levies — the sugar levy, plastic packaging taxes, carbon charges, landfill taxes. These are increasingly a feature of the modern tax landscape and they can quietly reshape a product's economics. If your packaging contains virgin plastic, a levy may have just become one of your raw material costs.
  • Customs duties and import tariffs — charged when goods cross a border. This is the point where your supply chain meets Regulations and the wider Economy; a tariff change can alter your sourcing decision overnight, which is exactly why the Suppliers chapter told you to think about how easily could I walk away?
  • Licence fees and permits — technically fees rather than taxes, but they behave the same way: an obligatory payment for the right to trade.

A worked example: Priya's tax stack

Let's put numbers on it. Priya's Bakery Ltd has a year like this:

£
Sales (before VAT) 200,000
Ingredients and supplies 60,000
Wages (gross, paid to staff) 80,000
Rent 18,000
Business rates 4,000
Energy and other overheads 16,000
Profit before tax 22,000

Now here's everything the tax system takes, and whose money it actually is:

Tax Amount Whose money?
VAT collected on sales (20%) 40,000 The customer's
VAT reclaimed on purchases −12,000 Reduces what she owes
Net VAT remitted 28,000 Never Priya's — but it lives in her bank account until it's due
Employer payroll tax (illustrative 15% of wages) 12,000 Priya's cost
Employee income tax and social contributions withheld 13,000 Her staff's — she deducts and remits
Corporation tax (25% of profit) 5,500 Priya's cost
Business rates 4,000 Priya's cost, already deducted above
Fuel duty and other embedded duties ~2,000 Priya's cost, hidden inside invoices

Add up what genuinely comes out of her own pocket: roughly £21,500 — the employer payroll tax, the corporation tax, the business rates and the embedded duties. Her profit was £22,000. In other words, the tax bill on her own account is almost exactly as large as her profit. That is not an unusual year for a small business.

And add up what she merely handles on behalf of others: £28,000 of net VAT plus £13,000 of staff deductions — around £41,000. That money was never hers, yet she is responsible for it, accounting for it, and paying it on time.

Two lessons fall out of that table, and they're the ones worth carrying forward:

Costs and cash are different things. The £41,000 is not a cost. It might be a cash flow problem.

Wages cost more than the wage. The £80,000 line in her accounts actually drains £92,000 once employer taxes are added.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Know which family each tax belongs to. A tax on profit behaves nothing like a tax on premises, and treating them as one undifferentiated "tax bill" hides where your money is really going.
  • Separate cost from cash flow. VAT and staff deductions are not your cost — but they are your liability, and they land on a date you don't choose. Hold them somewhere you won't spend them.
  • Price the full cost of labour. Employer taxes are part of what a person costs, and any comparison between hiring, automating or outsourcing is wrong if it leaves them out.
  • Remember the fixed ones when you commit to premises. Rates are paid in the quiet months too, which is the Stores chapter's warning about fixed costs, arriving as a bill.
  • Watch the narrow taxes. Excise, levies and tariffs hide inside your input prices. A new environmental levy is a Raw Materials problem with a policy name.
  • And run the loop on it. Forecasting tax is planning, keeping records and deadlines is organizing, getting help where you need it is sensible leading of your own attention, and reconciling what you owe against what you set aside is controlling — the same four jobs from the opening of this book, pointed at the tax office.

Summary

  • A business pays several different taxes, and they behave differently. Sorting them into families stops the whole thing blurring into one frightening lump.
  • The most useful question about any tax is whose money is it — because taxes you collect (VAT, staff deductions) are liabilities and cash flow, while taxes you pay (employer payroll taxes, corporation tax, rates) are genuine costs.
  • Taxes on profit — corporation tax and its equivalents — apply only when you've made money, with costs deducted first and losses usually carried forward.
  • Taxes on sales — VAT, GST, sales tax — are charged to customers and remitted by you. They're not your cost, but they are your cash flow and your administration, and there's usually a registration threshold below which small firms stay out.
  • Taxes on employing people include employer payroll taxes (your cost), plus income tax and employee contributions you deduct and remit. The wage on the contract is never the full cost of the person.
  • Taxes on property such as business rates behave like fixed costs — paid whatever you sell — plus transfer taxes and capital gains tax when assets change hands.
  • Taxes on specific goods and transactions — excise duties, environmental levies, customs duties, licence fees — arrive embedded in your input costs or at the border.
  • Rules, rates and names vary hugely between countries, so treat every figure here as illustrative and check the specifics where you trade.
  • Strategically: price the full cost of labour, hold collected tax separately, remember the fixed taxes when you commit to premises, and keep an eye on the narrow levies quietly reshaping your input costs.
