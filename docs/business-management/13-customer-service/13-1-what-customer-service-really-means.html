13-1 What Customer Service Really Means

Part of 13 Customer Service

Every business in this book eventually hands something to somebody. A loaf, a laptop, a haircut, an hour of advice. And in that handover there's a question the customer answers silently, every single time: was that worth it?

Customer service is how you influence that answer. Not the whole answer — the price, the product, the queue, the parking all have a vote. But service is the part you control most directly, and it's the part customers remember when they decide whether to come back.

So it's worth getting the definition right before anything else in this chapter makes sense.

The definition worth keeping

Customer service is everything a business does to help a customer get what they came for — before, during and after the sale — and to keep the promise it made to get them there.

Four pieces of that sentence are doing real work.

Everything. Not "the complaints desk". Not "the team in the headsets". Everything the customer experiences as they try to do business with you: whether the website loads, whether the sign is legible, whether the van arrives on time, whether the invoice is right, whether anybody picks up the phone.

Before, during and after. Most people think service starts when something goes wrong. Wrong. The clearest act of service Priya performs all day is opening at 7am, before anyone has asked for anything. The moment after the sale — did it arrive, did it work, would they buy again — is the one most businesses ignore entirely.

Help the customer get what they came for. This is the bit people get wrong most often, and it's the important one. Service isn't being pleasant. It's solving the problem. A shop that smiles beautifully while being out of stock has not served anyone.

Keep the promise. Here's where the Product chapter comes back. A brand is a promise; the product is the keeping of it. Customer service is that keeping — performed live, in front of the customer, by a human being, usually under time pressure.

It's not a department

The single most expensive misconception in business is that customer service lives in a team.

Look at where a customer actually meets your business over one order. They see your advert, find your website, read reviews, phone with a question, receive a quote, pay, wait, receive the goods, open the packaging, discover a problem, email about it, wait for a reply, and finally get a refund. Count the departments in that sentence. Marketing, sales, finance, distribution, stores, product, and yes, the service team — all of them delivering service or damaging it, whether or not anyone calls it that.

The Parts of a Company section called these handoffs seams, and warned they're where companies break. Customer service is what the customer experiences at each seam. From the inside it looks like six teams doing six jobs. From the outside it's one experience with your name on it, and nobody in the customer's head is distinguishing between them.

That's worth sitting with. Two departments can each do their job correctly and still produce terrible service, because the gap between them belongs to nobody. Service is what happens between the boxes on the org chart.

The gap between what you promised and what they got

Here's the definition you can actually measure: satisfaction lives in the gap between what the customer expected and what they experienced.

Two ways to close a gap. Raise what you deliver — faster delivery, better answers, fewer mistakes. Or lower what you promise — honest lead times, clearer photos, plainer terms. Both are legitimate. Both are somebody's job.

But notice which one gets forgotten. Almost every service failure I can think of starts upstream, in a promise made by somebody else. A salesperson who promises Tuesday when the van can only reach Thursday — that's the Sales chapter, and it just created a customer service problem in a different department. A website that shows a product as in stock when it isn't — that's Stores. A marketing campaign that promises a standard the Manufacturing line can't hold.

This is why over-promising is so corrosive. It doesn't just fail the customer; it sets your own staff up to look incompetent for something they didn't do.

Three things service isn't

It isn't being nice. Manners help — they're cheap and customers notice. But a warm, apologetic person who can't fix your problem is worse than a brisk one who can. Kindness without capability is just an apology in advance.

It isn't "the customer is always right." They're not, and treating them as though they are destroys the judgement of the people serving them. They are always the customer, which is a different thing entirely. Sometimes the most valuable service you can give is an honest "this isn't the right product for what you're describing" — the same diagnosis-first instinct the Sales chapter argued for.

It isn't free. Service costs money. Staff hours, stock replaced, refunds, delivery, training, the systems that track it all. That's the honest cost of doing business — and, as you're about to see, one of the best-value costs you'll ever carry.

Why it pays: the arithmetic nobody does

Here's the number that changes how you think about service.

One of Priya's regulars buys a £4 loaf twice a week. That's £8 a week, roughly £416 a year. If she stays five years, that one customer is worth over £2,000 in sales — and around £1,400 in contribution at Priya's margins.

Now suppose that customer gets home with a stale loaf. Priya has two options.

Replace it, no argument. Cost: one loaf, of which the ingredients come to £1.20. She also gains a small moment of proof that her shop stands behind its bread.

Argue about it. Cost: nothing today. And a customer who tells four people about the shop with the stale bread and the shrug.

Look at the ratio. £1.20 spent to protect a £1,400 relationship is about 1,100 to 1. There is almost no marketing budget in the world that can buy customers that cheaply — which is the point the Revenue chapter keeps circling: the cheapest sale you will ever make is the one from a customer you already have.

And it compounds the other way too. A handled complaint is often better for loyalty than a flawless transaction, because the customer now knows what happens when things go wrong. A customer who has never seen you solve a problem has never actually seen you work.

There's a cost side to this as well, and it's one most businesses never measure: cost to serve. Two customers paying the same price can cost wildly different amounts to look after. One orders in bulk, pays on time and never calls. Another orders singles, asks three questions, returns half of it and pays late. Same revenue, very different contribution — the Costs chapter's distinction between a sale and a profitable sale, showing up at the service desk.

Where service actually comes from

If service is the whole business's behaviour, it can't be installed by a script. It comes from two places, and both are management choices.

From your staff. Whoever is closest to the customer decides, in a two-second window, whether to solve the problem or follow the rule. What makes them choose solve is being trusted to. Give people the authority to make a £20 decision without asking, and you'll be astonished how rarely they abuse it and how often they save a £2,000 relationship. That's the Staff and Labour chapters: effort is perishable, it can't be stored, and it belongs to someone who chose to bring it to work today.

From your management. Because people serve customers the way they're treated. The four jobs from the opening of this book land here almost perfectly — planning what the service standard actually is, organizing who is on when and what tools they have, leading by backing staff up when a customer is unreasonable, and controlling by measuring it. Staff who are under-trained, over-stretched and hung out to dry do not walk into a customer's day and make it better.


That loop is the whole argument of this subsection in one picture, and it's the same compounding loop you've met throughout the book. Good management buys good staff; good staff deliver good service; good service keeps customers; keeping customers pays for good management. Run it backwards — squeeze the training, stretch the rota, ignore the complaints — and it unwinds just as reliably, only more slowly, so it's harder to spot.

Service as a competitive weapon

One last thing, because it matters for strategy. The Competitors chapter split competition into price and everything-that-isn't-price. Customer service is one of the strongest weapons in the second category — and it's very hard to copy.

A rival can clone your price overnight and copy your product in a season. Copying your service means rebuilding the culture, the training, the systems and the trust. That takes years, and by then you've moved.

This is why service is the natural answer for a small business competing with a bigger, cheaper one. You can't out-scale them. You can absolutely out-serve them.

Summary

  • Customer service is everything a business does to help a customer get what they came for — before, during and after the sale — and to keep the promise it made to get them there.
  • It isn't a department. It's what the customer experiences at every touchpoint and, above all, between your departments — at the seams the Parts of a Company section warned about.
  • Satisfaction lives in the gap between expectation and experience. Close it by delivering more or promising less — and remember that most service failures are created upstream by over-promising in sales and marketing.
  • Service is not being nice, not "the customer is always right", and not free. It's solving the customer's problem, and it's a real cost — but a remarkably good-value one.
  • The arithmetic is the argument: a £1.20 loaf spent protecting a customer worth £2,000-plus is one of the cheapest investments in business. Keeping a customer costs far less than winning one.
  • Watch cost to serve as well as revenue. Two customers paying the same price can be very different propositions once you count what it takes to look after each.
  • Service comes from staff who are trusted and from management that treats them well — the four management jobs, pointed at the customer. It's a compounding loop, in both directions.
  • Strategically, it's one of the few advantages a smaller business can build that a bigger rival cannot simply buy.
