13-3 Customer Service as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 13 Customer Service

Every chapter so far has asked you to look at one part of the business at a time. The product, the price, the stock on the shelf, the van, the wage bill. Customer service refuses to stay in its lane.

It's the one factor that runs through all of them, because it's where every promise your business made gets tested against what actually happened. Your brand is a promise, your product is the keeping of it — and service is what you do on the days when the keeping doesn't go to plan. A business that only serves customers well when nothing has gone wrong isn't offering service. It's offering luck.

So let's do what we've done with raw materials, labour and distribution: pull back and watch where service shows up across the whole business — and what it quietly costs and earns.

Service is the third layer of the product

Remember the three levels of a product from the Product chapter: the core benefit the customer actually wants, the actual product they receive, and the augmented product wrapped around it. Service is the augmented layer. Delivery speed, returns, how easy it is to ask a question, whether anyone picks up the phone.

That's not a soft point. In any market where rivals have converged — and in most markets they do — the actual products are nearly identical. Two loaves of sourdough, same flour, same oven, same price. The fight moves upstairs to the augmented layer, which is where service lives. This is the Types of Competition chapter telling you why: in monopolistic competition, rivals are close substitutes, not identical goods. Service is one of the cheapest ways to stop being a substitute.

The retention engine

Here's the idea that makes service a financial topic rather than a nice-to-have. The Sales chapter put it bluntly: one sale is a transaction, a customer is an asset. The first sale costs money to win — advertising, an introductory offer, hours of somebody's time. The second one costs almost nothing.


Run that loop in reverse — service failures, lost regulars, a reputation that slips — and it compounds just as reliably downwards. Which brings us to the arithmetic.

Worked example: what a regular is worth to Priya

Priya sells a loaf at £4.00 that costs her £1.20 in ingredients, leaving £2.80 of contribution toward rent, wages and profit.

Now meet Sam, a regular. She buys one loaf a day, five days a week, forty-eight weeks a year.

Revenue Contribution
One loaf, once £4.00 £2.80
Sam, for a year (240 loaves) £960 £672

Priya's whole bakery turns over enough to leave her roughly £50 a day — about £18,000 a year. Twenty-five regulars like Sam are worth £16,800 of contribution. In other words, her regulars are the business. Everything else — the market stall, the delivery app, the café contract — is how she finds more of them.

Now run the other direction. Suppose indifferent service drives away five regulars over the year. That's £3,360 of contribution gone. At £2.80 a loaf, Priya would have to bake and sell an extra 1,200 loaves — twelve full days of production — just to get back to where she started. And that's before counting the bad reviews, which the Competitors chapter reminds us are public signals that steer the next customer away before she ever gets the chance to serve them.

Set that against what fixing it costs. Replacing a stale loaf costs £1.20. A £1-an-hour pay rise for the person on the counter — the one who remembers Sam's name and slices her loaf without being asked — is about £1,400 a year. That's less than the contribution from three Sams.

She can replace a stale loaf for £1.20. She can't replace Sam for anything close to that.

Where service shows up in the numbers

Service sits on both sides of the income statement, exactly like labour.

Revenue. Repeat purchases and referrals, obviously. But also pricing power — the Market chapter's elasticity. A customer who trusts you compares you less on price, which makes your demand less elastic, which means you can nudge prices without losing the queue. Service is a pricing strategy wearing an apron.

Costs. Note the counter-intuitive bit: good service is usually cheaper than bad service. It costs less to answer the phone than to handle the complaint it became. Less to replace one stale loaf than to lose a regular. Less to train a counter assistant properly than to hire her replacement twice a year. Only a fraction of service cost appears as a "service budget" — most of it is hidden in the returns, refunds, remakes and goodwill discounts that never needed to happen.

Financials. Refunds, credits and warranty claims are all working-capital events, and every one of them is a service failure that got invoiced. The Financials chapter will tell you how to account for them; the strategist's job is to notice how many of them are self-inflicted.

It's delivered by people, so it's a labour and management problem

Front-line staff are the brand as far as most customers are concerned. Which makes service a Labour problem and a Management one, not a communication exercise.

Two consequences:

Service quality follows staff quality, and staff quality follows how you treat them. The Labour chapter made the point that labour is the only input that can choose, learn and leave. Service is where that bites hardest. A rival paying more, training better, or rostering more humanely drains your service quality along with your people — and the customers follow a little later. Staff turnover shows up in your reviews about six months before it shows up in your sales.

Service also needs authority, which is an organizing problem. If every exception has to be approved by a manager, your service runs at the manager's walking speed. Deciding who can say yes to what — a free replacement, a small refund, a swapped shift — is exactly the organizing job from the management cycle. So is the planning half: what standard are you actually promising? And the controlling half: repeat-purchase rate, complaint volume, response times, review scores. Pick three or four and check them monthly, like any other control measure.

Where else it hides in plain sight

  • Stores. Layout, queue length, opening hours, whether anyone makes eye contact. Availability is service — the third level of the product, again.
  • Distribution. Delivery windows, tracking, and returns — reverse logistics, often the most expensive leg of all. Most "where is my order?" calls are a distribution failure wearing a service costume.
  • Sales. Every promise made at the point of sale becomes a service commitment. Over-promising in sales is the single most common cause of a service crisis.
  • Product. Complaints are free market research. The complaint that recurs is a product brief.
  • Suppliers. A supplier who lets you down becomes your service failure in your customer's eyes. They will never blame the flour mill.
  • Regulations. Consumer rights, returns, refunds, complaint handling and data protection all set a floor beneath your service promise. You can exceed the rules, never fall short of them.
  • The Economy. In a downturn, customers scrutinise every purchase and switch more readily after one bad experience — yet service budgets are usually the first thing cut. That's backwards, for the reasons in the tables above.

Two cautions

Service can't rescue a bad product. A sincere apology and a painless refund are still a refund. If the thing doesn't work, fix the thing. Service keeps the promise; it doesn't make one.

And you can over-serve. Gold-plating service for customers who don't value it burns the same money that could have gone into product or price. Match the service level to what buyers will actually pay for — the same contribution logic that runs through the Costs chapter. A luxury showroom experience on a commodity product is a cost with no revenue attached.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Treat service as a product decision, not an afterthought. It's the augmented layer, and it's where converged markets are actually won.
  • Price the loop honestly. Know what a regular is worth over a year, then compare that with the cost of retaining them. The gap is usually enormous, and it's almost always in service's favour.
  • Look for it in the wrong places. Returns, remakes, refunds and walkouts are service costs hiding in your cost lines.
  • Invest in the people who deliver it. Pay, training and hours are service strategy. Turnover is a leading indicator of service decline.
  • Give the front line the authority to fix things. Every exception that needs a manager's signature is a service delay with a price.
  • Watch your competitors, because this ratchets. Once a rival offers free returns or next-day delivery, it becomes the expectation — and you must match it or explain why not.
  • Close the loop. Set a service standard, organise so it can be delivered, lead the people delivering it, and control it with a few honest numbers.

Summary

  • Customer service is the one factor that runs through the whole business, because it's where every promise made elsewhere gets tested.
  • It is the augmented product — the layer where competition happens once actual products converge.
  • Service turns a transaction into a customer, and the retention loop compounds: repeat revenue, referrals, contribution, reinvestment.
  • The arithmetic is brutal and one-sided. A regular is worth many multiples of a single sale, and good service is usually cheaper than bad service — it just hides its costs in avoided refunds rather than in a budget line.
  • It appears on both sides of the income statement: it drives revenue and pricing power, and it shows up as returns, remakes, credits and lost regulars.
  • Delivery depends on people and management: staff quality, staff retention, and how much authority the front line actually has.
  • It surfaces everywhere — Stores, Distribution, Sales, Product, Suppliers, Regulations, the Economy — and it ratchets upwards as rivals raise the floor.
  • Two limits: service can't fix a bad product, and over-serving customers who don't value it is just an unmeasured cost.
  • The strategist's habit: price the retention loop, then spend on service up to the point where the next pound earns more than a pound back.
