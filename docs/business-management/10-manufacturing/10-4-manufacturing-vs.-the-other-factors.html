10-4 Manufacturing vs. The Other Factors

Part of 10 Manufacturing

Manufacturing is the one factor in this book that actually makes something. Everything else either prepares for it, feeds it, sells what comes out of it, or keeps score. That's why this comparison matters more than most: get manufacturing right and the whole model hums; get it wrong and you can have brilliant marketing, loyal customers, and a motivated team, and still watch your margins disappear into a factory that can't make the thing at a price anyone will pay.

Here's the mental model. Manufacturing sits in the middle of a web. Inputs flow in from the left, outputs flow out to the right, and four groups of factors press on it from every side.


Let's walk around that web, one cluster at a time.

Upstream: Suppliers and Raw Materials

These two are manufacturing's food and water. If you read the Suppliers and Raw Materials chapters, you already know that a raw material is only useful once someone converts it, and manufacturing is the converter.

The relationship is brutally direct. Incoming quality becomes outgoing quality. If your steel coil arrives with inconsistent thickness, your stamping press produces inconsistent parts, your assembly line jams, and your customer opens a box containing a wonky product. No amount of final inspection fixes a problem you imported at the gate — it just moves the discovery later and makes it more expensive. This is why manufacturers audit suppliers far more aggressively than other kinds of businesses do.

Then there's timing. If you run just-in-time production — keeping almost no raw material on site and having deliveries arrive hours before use — then your factory's reliability is chained to your suppliers' reliability. One late truck, one port strike, one supplier who quietly switched to a cheaper sub-supplier, and the line stops. A stopped line is the most expensive object in business: the rent, the depreciation, the supervisors, and the electricity keep burning while nothing comes out.

And finally, cost. In most physical manufacturing, raw materials are the single largest slice of cost of goods sold — often 40% to 70%, depending on the industry. That means a 10% move in a commodity price can wipe out a margin that took you three years to build. Manufacturing decisions are therefore also material decisions: choosing aluminium over steel, or recycled plastic over virgin, is a manufacturing choice with strategic consequences for cost, weight, regulation and even marketing story.

Inside the walls: Company, Product, Labour, Staff, Management

The company is the container. It decides whether to manufacture at all — the classic make-or-buy decision. A company can own factories (asset-heavy, slow to change, high barriers to entry) or contract the work out to someone else (flexible, lower capital, thinner margins and less control). Neither is right in the abstract; it depends on whether manufacturing is where your advantage lives. If it is, own it. If it isn't, rent it. Everything in the Company chapter — legal structure, ownership, mission, how the business is split into parts — sets the boundaries inside which manufacturing operates.

The product is the blueprint manufacturers have to obey. Here's a rule of thumb worth remembering: roughly 70–80% of a product's manufacturing cost is locked in at the design stage, before a single unit is made. A designer who specifies a screw where a snap-fit would do has just added permanent cost and time. This is called design for manufacturability, and it's one of the highest-leverage conversations in any business. Product proliferation matters too: every extra colour, size or variant multiplies changeovers — the stoppages when a line switches from making one thing to another. Ten SKUs can be far more than ten times as troublesome as one.

Labour and staff are who does the work. Manufacturing lets you make a fundamental trade: people or machines. Labour is variable — you can hire for a busy month and lay off in a slow one (within the law, and within your conscience). Automation is fixed — you pay for the robot whether it runs or not, but the cost per unit becomes tiny at high volume. That trade-off sits at the heart of manufacturing strategy, and it changes the shape of your risk: automate and you've swapped wage risk for capital risk. Staff also shapes the shop floor's culture, safety record, and skill pipeline — a factory with no apprentices is a factory with a problem in eight years' time.

Management is where it all gets decided. Batch sizes, shift patterns, quality systems, whether to run lean or build inventory buffers, which machine to buy — these are management calls. Modern management practice was largely invented on factory floors (scientific management, then lean, then Six Sigma), and the vocabulary still shows: takt time, kaizen, standard work, overall equipment effectiveness. If you want to learn management, learn a factory.

Downstream: Distribution, Stores, Sales, Customer Service

Once goods exist, they have to move. Distribution and manufacturing negotiate constantly over the same trade-off: few big plants produce cheaply but pay more freight; many small plants ship cheaply but produce at higher cost. Packaging, pallet heights, case packs — all designed jointly. A plant that fills a truck perfectly has just given its distribution team free money.

Stores impose practical demands: barcodes, case quantities sized to a shelf, packaging that survives being thrown around a stockroom. Finished goods inventory piles up between the factory gate and the shop floor, and that pile is cash you've spent but not yet collected.

Sales and manufacturing have the most fraught relationship in business. Sales wants every variant, immediately, in any quantity, with a promotion next week. Manufacturing wants long, steady, predictable runs. A big promotion can spike demand, and manufacturing either builds ahead (tying up cash in inventory) or works overtime (paying a premium). This is why mature companies run sales and operations planning — a monthly meeting where the forecast meets the plant's real capacity. Without it, sales promises lead times the factory cannot keep, and customer service spends the rest of the quarter apologising.

Which brings us to customer service. Manufacturing quality is customer service's biggest cost line. Every defect that reaches a customer becomes a return, a refund, a warranty claim, a support call, a bad review. Manufacturers track this as the cost of poor quality — and it's usually far larger than the cost of preventing the defect in the first place. Good plants also keep spare parts flowing for years after a product dies, because a customer who can't repair your product won't buy your next one.

The scoreboard: Costs, Revenue, Financials

If you remember one relationship from this subsection, make it this one: manufacturing is where cost is created. The Costs chapter lists fixed and variable costs, direct and indirect. A factory is the perfect illustration of all of them at once.

  • Variable: materials, piece-rate labour, packaging, energy per unit.
  • Fixed: the building, the depreciation on the machine, the plant manager, the insurance.
  • Semi-fixed: supervisors, maintenance, quality control.

This split produces the most important number in manufacturing: contribution per unit — the price minus the variable cost of making it. Every extra unit sold contributes that amount toward the fixed costs. Once fixed costs are covered, every additional unit is nearly pure profit. That's the whole logic of scale, and it's why a factory with an idle line will accept a low-margin order rather than let the line sit still.

Financials show all of this. The plant appears on the balance sheet as property, plant and equipment, shrinking each year through depreciation. Inventory — raw materials, work in progress, finished goods — is cash frozen in place and drags on your cash conversion cycle. Buying a machine is a capital decision measured in payback period and return on investment. And on the income statement, gross margin is essentially the manufacturing scorecard: revenue minus cost of goods sold.

Revenue, meanwhile, is capped by capacity. You cannot sell what you cannot make — a lesson that has sunk many fast-growing brands. When demand outruns production, you either invest in capacity (cash out now, revenue later), raise prices (testing whether customers value you enough), or hand the work to a contract manufacturer (revenue today, margin and control tomorrow). Choosing between those three is a strategy decision, not a production decision.

The world outside: Market, Competitors, Economy, Tax, Regulations

The market sets the volume you can hope for, and volume sets everything else. Economists call the volume at which unit costs stop falling the minimum efficient scale. If your market is too small to reach it, building a factory is a trap — you'll be permanently undercut by someone bigger. Geography matters too: markets are local, so plants usually are as well.

Competitors turn manufacturing capability into a weapon or a millstone. A factory is often the hardest advantage for a rival to copy — tooling, tacit know-how and scale take years and money to replicate. But capacity is also a treadmill: once you build it, you must fill it, and filling it often means price cuts that start a war. Watch for a strange modern twist, too — your contract manufacturer may also make products for your competitor. Your supplier and your rival can be the same company.

The economy prices your decisions. Interest rates determine what a machine really costs; energy prices feed the furnace; currency moves change the price of imported equipment; and the business cycle delivers the oldest mistake in manufacturing — adding capacity at the peak of demand, so that the new plant opens just as the recession begins.

Tax shows up as capital allowances and depreciation rules (which change how attractive a machine is), research and development credits for process innovation, tariffs and duties on imported components, property taxes on land, and environmental levies. Regulations govern emissions, effluent, waste, worker safety, product safety, labelling, and building codes. In manufacturing these aren't paperwork — they're design constraints, and they can be turned into advantages: a company that meets a stricter standard early can sell into markets its competitors can't enter yet.

Worked example: should Kestrel build its own line?

Kestrel makes stainless steel water bottles. Right now it buys finished bottles from a contract manufacturer overseas at £6 each and sells them for £18. Demand is 60,000 bottles a year and growing. The owner is tempted to build a production line at home.

Start with the machine: £400,000, expected to last eight years. Straight-line depreciation is £50,000 a year. Making a bottle in-house would cost roughly £4 in materials, labour and energy — saving £2 per bottle against the £6 she currently pays. So:

  • Each bottle made in-house contributes £2 more than an imported one.
  • Break-even on the machine: £50,000 ÷ £2 = 25,000 bottles a year.
  • At 60,000 bottles, the extra contribution is £120,000 a year — comfortably ahead.

Now check the other factors before celebrating. Raw materials: steel prices swing; the contract manufacturer absorbed that risk, Kestrel now carries it. Market: demand is seasonal, but depreciation runs all year. Product: Kestrel sells four colours — changeovers could eat into that £2 saving. Labour: someone must be hired, trained, insured and covered during holidays. Regulations: food-contact certification and workplace safety obligations arrive overnight. Tax: capital allowances may soften the £400,000. Competitors: two rivals already run their own plants at far higher volumes and will always beat Kestrel on pure cost. Financials: £400,000 of cash is gone, and the payback is around three-and-a-half years if everything goes to plan.

The verdict isn't obvious — and that's the point. Manufacturing decisions are rarely about manufacturing alone. They're bets on volume, price stability, and your ability to sell what the line produces. A good strategist runs the numbers and reads the surrounding factors before signing.

What this means for you as a strategist

Three habits separate a strategist from an operator here.

First, always ask what manufacturing is for. Is it the source of your advantage — something customers pay extra for and rivals struggle to copy — or is it a utility you should outsource to whoever does it cheapest? Answer that honestly and half your decisions make themselves.

Second, treat capacity as a promise you've made to the rest of the business. Sales, distribution and revenue planning all rest on what the plant can actually deliver. Overpromise and you'll burn cash on overtime and lose customers to stockouts. Underinvest and you'll cap your own growth.

Third, remember that cost is a consequence, not a cause. Your unit cost falls out of design choices, batch sizes, supplier terms, automation levels and volume. Change any of those and the cost changes. That's why manufacturing sits at the centre of the profitability equation — not because factories are romantic, but because they're where the money is actually made or lost.

Summary

  • Manufacturing is the conversion engine at the centre of the business; every other factor either feeds it, sells its output, or keeps score.
  • Upstream, suppliers and raw materials determine quality, reliability and the largest slice of your cost base.
  • Inside the business, the company decides whether to make or buy, the product design locks in most of the cost, labour and staff supply the hands (or the automation replaces them), and management runs the whole operation.
  • Downstream, distribution, stores, sales and customer service translate production into revenue — and their demands (case packs, lead times, forecasts, quality) flow straight back to the plant.
  • Financially, manufacturing is where costs are created and revenue is capped: contribution per unit, gross margin, depreciation, inventory and capital investment all trace back to the factory floor.
  • Outside the walls, the market sets the volume you need for minimum efficient scale, competitors benchmark you, and the economy, tax and regulations shape whether investing in production is wise, affordable and legal.
  • Make-or-buy, capacity and cost position are not production questions. They are strategy questions that happen to be answered with machines.
