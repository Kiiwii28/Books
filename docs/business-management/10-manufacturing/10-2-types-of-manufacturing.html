10-2 Types of Manufacturing

Part of 10 Manufacturing

You've just met manufacturing as the act of transforming inputs into something a customer will pay for. The awkward part is that "manufacturing" covers a person hand-rolling sourdough at 4am and a chemical plant that has run non-stop for eleven years without being switched off. Those two businesses share a word and almost nothing else.

That matters, because the type of manufacturing you run decides your cost structure, how much capital you need up front, what skills you hire, how you compete, and — the bit beginners miss — how badly a fall in demand hurts you. Let's sort the landscape into the four lenses that do the most work.

Lens one: volume and variety

The single most useful axis runs from doing one unique thing at a time to making the same thing forever. Four classic types sit along it.

Job production makes one thing at a time to a customer's specification. A bespoke dining table. A wedding cake. A bridge. A film. Each job is different, the work is skilled, the equipment is general-purpose, and the whole thing is usually organised around the individual order.

Batch production makes a run of identical units, then stops and switches. A tray of croissants, then a tray of buns. Five hundred red T-shirts, then five hundred blue. You get repetition — and therefore some speed and consistency — but every changeover costs time and money.

Mass production makes large volumes of a standardised product on dedicated equipment, with the work broken into small steps. Cars, phones, tinned beans. High volume, low variety, low unit cost — but a line that only knows how to make one thing.

Continuous production never stops at all. Steel, paper, chemicals, electricity, refined oil. Output is measured in tonnes or litres rather than units, the plant runs 24/7, and the capital cost is enormous.


Read that left to right and three things move together: volume rises, variety falls, and unit cost tends to fall — because you're spreading bigger fixed costs over more units. Hold that thought; it's the hinge of everything below.

Lens two: when does production start?

The second lens isn't about how you make things but when you commit to making them — and it's really a question about risk.

Type You build after… Who carries the stock risk Typical product
Make to stock A forecast You Bread, soft drinks, phones in a shop
Make to order A confirmed order The customer Bespoke furniture, printed T-shirts
Assemble to order An order, from standard parts Shared Dell-style laptops, kitchens
Engineer to order A signed design and spec The customer Bridges, special-purpose machines

Make-to-stock gives customers instant availability and lets you run long, cheap production runs — at the price of guessing demand right. Guess high and you're funding a warehouse full of unsold goods, which is cash you've spent and can't use. That's the Financials chapter's working-capital squeeze, arriving via the factory.

Make-to-order flips it: almost no finished stock, no discounting to clear a warehouse, and a customer who is happy to wait. But your lead times get longer, your capacity is harder to smooth, and your Customer Service promise now depends on how fast you can turn a job around. This is where the Market chapter's supply-and-demand logic meets your order book.

Lens three: discrete or process?

There's a quieter distinction that changes the whole physics of a plant. Discrete manufacturing counts things — you can point at unit number 4,712 and say it's finished. Assembly, machining, electronics, most furniture.

Process manufacturing can't count in units. You measure it by weight, volume or concentration: a tonne of ammonia, a batch of yoghurt, a reel of paper. You can't un-mix it, you can't rework a bad batch, and quality has to be controlled during production rather than inspected afterwards. This is also where the Regulations chapter bites hardest — food safety, pharmaceutical licensing and chemical handling rules shape the plant, not just the paperwork.

Lens four: who actually does the making?

You don't have to own a factory. A contract manufacturer builds your product to your specification, and you put your brand on it. Plenty of well-known companies own almost no production capacity at all; supermarkets' own-label goods work exactly this way.

That's the make-or-buy question from the Suppliers chapter, and the trade is the familiar one. Outsourcing buys you flexibility, lower capital and access to someone else's scale — but you hand over control of quality, lead times and, to some degree, your product knowledge. Keep a core capability in-house and outsource the rest, and you get the best of both; outsource the thing that makes you special and you've handed a rival the recipe.

Two more modern shifts are worth naming. Additive manufacturing (3D printing) has made small-batch, customised production economic where tooling would once have been ruinous — job production getting cheaper. And automation has pushed the other way, letting mass production happen at volumes that used to need continuous plants.

A worked example: the numbers behind the choice

Here's why the type matters commercially. Imagine a furniture maker choosing between staying a flexible job shop and investing in a dedicated automated line. Price is £100 a unit either way.

Per month Job-shop setup Automated line
Fixed costs £5,000 £25,000
Variable cost per unit £40 £20
Contribution per unit £60 £80
Break-even volume 84 units 313 units
Profit at 500 units £25,000 £15,000
Profit at 2,000 units £115,000 £135,000

Look at what the table is really saying. Below roughly 1,000 units a month the flexible workshop wins. Above it, the line wins — decisively. The automated route is a bet on volume, and it's a bet paid for in advance.

This is the Costs chapter's fixed-versus-variable split with a factory attached. And it explains why heavy manufacturers are so exposed when demand drops: their costs barely move, so a 20% fall in orders can wipe out the entire profit. Job shops bend; mass producers break. If you remember the product life cycle, you'll see the pattern — a product in growth justifies dedicated kit, and a product in decline turns that same kit into a millstone.

The type isn't permanent — and you rarely have just one

Two things to keep in mind.

First, manufacturers drift along the spectrum as products mature. Rowan and Ash build one-off dining tables (job). If one design sells brilliantly, they'll start making it in runs (batch). If it becomes a bestseller across the country, they'll invest in tooling and pump them out (mass). Nothing changed about their skill — the market moved them along the line. The dangerous part is that each step adds fixed cost, which is comfortable in growth and terrifying in decline.

Second, most real businesses run several types at once. Priya's bakery makes a bespoke wedding cake (job), bakes trays of the same buns (batch), and could supply a supermarket own-label line (mass — done by a contract manufacturer). Same ovens, three different economic games, three different sets of decisions about labour, kit and stock.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Name your type honestly. Job, batch, mass and continuous demand different plant, different skills and different management. Borrowing the tactics of one for the reality of another is how businesses quietly lose money.
  • Match the type to the demand you actually have, not the demand you hope for. High fixed cost only pays off with sustained volume. Flexibility costs money per unit but keeps you alive in a soft quarter.
  • Let the Life Cycle guide your investment. Add capacity ahead of growth, but think twice before buying dedicated kit for a product that's already mature.
  • Choose your trigger deliberately. Make-to-stock buys availability with cash and risk; make-to-order buys safety with lead time. Whichever you pick, it lands on your Revenue, Financials and Customer Service all at once.
  • Decide what stays in-house. Outsourcing is a legitimate route to scale, but keep the capability that makes customers choose you.
  • Then run the loop. Forecast demand, set up the plant and the rota, lead the team through the changeovers, and control it with two or three numbers — output per hour, cost per unit and waste. Manufacturing types change; the management cycle doesn't.

Summary

  • Manufacturing sorts along a volume–variety spectrum: job, batch, mass and continuous production. As volume rises and variety falls, unit costs tend to fall — and fixed costs rise.
  • A second lens is when production starts: make-to-stock, make-to-order, assemble-to-order and engineer-to-order each trade availability against risk.
  • A third is what you're transforming: discrete manufacturing counts units; process manufacturing works in bulk and must control quality during production rather than after it.
  • A fourth is who makes it: in-house versus contract manufacturing, the make-or-buy decision from the suppliers chapter.
  • The type drives your break-even point. Flexible setups break even early; automated lines only win above a threshold volume, and bleed badly below it.
  • Manufacturing types are not permanent. Products drift from job to batch to mass as they mature, and most businesses run several types simultaneously.
  • The strategist's move: match the type to real demand, let the product life cycle steer investment, and measure cost per unit, output per hour and waste.
