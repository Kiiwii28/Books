10-3 Manufacturing as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 10 Manufacturing

Manufacturing is where the business stops being an idea and becomes a thing you can weigh, stack, ship and sell. That makes it easy to file away as "the factory bit" — a set of machines in a building, someone else's problem if you outsource it. That framing is a trap.

Manufacturing is the conversion point of the whole business: raw materials and labour go in one end, a sellable product comes out the other, and the choices you make here echo into your costs, your quality, your cash, your customers and your competitive position. You've already met what manufacturing is and the different shapes it takes — job, batch, mass, continuous. This subsection is about what those choices do to everything else.

The conversion engine

Here's the picture worth holding. Manufacturing sits in the middle of the loop you've seen throughout this book, and it's the point where inputs turn into something the market will pay for.


Look at the arrow coming back in at the end. Profit buys better equipment, more capacity, tighter tolerances, less waste — which makes the next round of manufacturing cheaper or better. That's the compounding loop from the opening chapter, and manufacturing is where it physically happens. A manufacturer that improves a little every cycle quietly leaves its rivals behind; one that doesn't, quietly falls behind while looking busy.

It sets your cost per unit — which sets your whole strategy

Most of your cost per unit is decided on the factory floor, not in the accounts. Three levers do the work:

Scale. Spreading fixed costs — the building, the machine, the supervisor — over more units is the single biggest cost lever a manufacturer has. It's also directly why the Costs chapter's fixed-and-variable split matters here: you can't judge whether a bigger machine is worth buying until you know what it does to the variable cost per unit and how many units you'll actually sell.

Yield. Yield is how much saleable output you get from your inputs. If 5% of your loaves come out wrong, that 5% is materials, energy and labour you paid for and can't sell. Improving yield from 95% to 98% doesn't sound dramatic — until you notice it's pure margin, achieved without a single extra customer. In materials-heavy businesses, yield improvements are usually the cheapest profit available.

Automation. Replacing hours of effort with a machine trades a variable cost for a fixed one. That's a great deal when volumes are high and steady, and a terrible one when demand wobbles — because the machine's cost doesn't care whether anyone ordered anything. It's the same trade you met in the Labour chapter's ladder of commitment, just made of steel instead of contracts.

This is where the make-or-buy decision lives. Every part you could make in-house is a choice between closing a gap yourself (with all the fixed cost and capability that implies) and paying a supplier to close it for you. Buy, and you stay flexible and capital-light; make, and you control quality, cost and availability — but you own the problem when demand falls.

It sets your quality ceiling — and your floor

Craft and consistency are different things, and manufacturing decides both.

The ceiling is set by what your process can achieve: the tolerance, the finish, the purity, the consistency. Design a product your line can't hold to spec and you'll spend your life apologising. This is a Product decision made in the factory — the design and the process have to be planned together, not one after the other.

The floor matters just as much. Customers forgive an average product more easily than an inconsistent one. A bakery whose loaves are excellent on Tuesday and soggy on Friday doesn't have a quality average problem — it has a predictability problem, and predictability is what a brand actually is: a promise about what the next one will be like. Variation is the enemy, and variation is a manufacturing control problem.

That makes manufacturing a revenue lever, not just a cost centre. Consistency is what lets you charge a premium, hold a review score, and keep customers coming back — which is exactly the pricing power the Competitors chapter keeps pointing at.

It sets your speed, your ceiling and your flexibility

Your manufacturing capacity is the hard limit on how much you can sell. A line that can produce 4,000 units a week makes it pointless to hire a sales team that could sell 10,000 — you'd just generate disappointed customers.

Three timing facts follow from manufacturing, and they all reach into other chapters:

  • Lead time. How long between an order arriving and goods leaving. Long lead times force you to hold stock (cash you can't spend), and they make you slow to react when demand shifts — the Economy chapter moves faster than most production lines.
  • Batch size and changeover. Big batches are efficient but inflexible. Small batches let you offer variety and respond to demand, at the price of more changeover time and higher unit cost. Your product range decision is partly a manufacturing decision in disguise.
  • Where the plant sits. Production is usually centralised where it's cheap; customers are scattered. That gap is what Distribution exists to close, and building a plant far from your buyers is a permanent tax on every unit you sell. Cheap, heavy goods — cement, bottled water, bread — are made near the customer for exactly this reason.

It ties up cash before you've sold anything

Manufacturing is the most cash-hungry activity in most product businesses, and beginners are surprised by it. Three reasons:

Work in progress. Materials you've bought and wages you've paid, sitting half-finished on a bench. That's money spent with no revenue attached — yet.

Capital expenditure. Machines, tooling and buildings are paid for up front and depreciate over years, which links straight into Financials. That's why an investment decision made in January shows up as a modest annual cost for the next decade, not as a one-off hit.

The cash conversion gap. You pay for materials and labour now; your customers pay you in 30, 60 or 90 days. Manufacturing lengthens that gap, which is why a fast-growing, profitable manufacturer can still run out of money. The Financials chapter covers this properly, but note the direction: manufacturing decisions cause working-capital problems as often as they solve them.

Worked example: Priya buys a second oven

Priya's bakery is at capacity — 100 loaves a day, sold out by noon, queue out of the door. She considers a second oven and a part-time baker, at a combined extra fixed cost of £40 a day (finance, gas and the extra shift).

Her extra capacity: 40 more loaves a day. If she sells them at an average of £3.40 — some over the counter at full price, some wholesale to cafés — and the extra materials and direct labour cost £1.80 a loaf, then each extra loaf contributes:

£3.40 − £1.80 = £1.60

Break-even: £40 ÷ £1.60 = 25 loaves a day.

So she needs to sell 25 of her 40 extra loaves just to cover the new oven. Everything above that is profit — and the margin on those last 15 loaves is fatter than anything she makes today, because the fixed costs are already covered. That's the Costs chapter's contribution logic, applied to a genuine manufacturing decision.

Now watch the ripple. The extra production needs more flour, so she renegotiates with her supplier on volume. It needs more trays, more boxes, and somewhere to put the extra stock. It needs the van route re-planned, because the wholesale loaves go out at 6am — a Distribution change. And it needs someone checking yield daily, because if waste creeps up from 4% to 8% on double the volume, the oven stops paying for itself.

One decision. Five parts of the business. That's what "a factor across the whole business" means.

The trade-off you can't escape

Manufacturing forces a choice between three things, and you can only push hard on two:

You want more of… It usually costs you…
Low cost per unit (scale, automation, long runs) Flexibility and range
Flexibility (small batches, quick changeovers, custom work) Cost per unit
Consistency and precision (tight tolerances, inspection) Speed and cost

A custom furniture workshop and a flat-pack factory are both "manufacturers", and they've simply chosen opposite corners of that triangle. Neither is wrong. What is wrong is promising all three and delivering none — a strategy problem that shows up as an operations problem.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Treat manufacturing as a revenue decision, not just a cost centre. It sets what you can promise, how fast, and how consistently — and customers pay for all three.
  • Know your cost per unit and how it behaves. Fixed versus variable, at current volume and at double the volume. That's the difference between a decision and a guess.
  • Chase yield before you chase customers. Waste, rework and spoilage are money already spent with no revenue attached — the cheapest margin in the building.
  • Match capacity to demand, deliberately. Under-capacity loses sales; over-capacity pays for machines that aren't working. Both are planning failures.
  • Run the management loop on the factory floor. Plan the capacity, organize the line and the suppliers, lead the people who keep it running, and control it with two or three numbers — cost per unit, yield, and on-time output. Measure them weekly, not at year-end.
  • Remember the tail. Manufacturing decisions turn up later wearing other clothes: a capex decision in Financials, a stock problem in Stores, a lead-time problem in Distribution, a compliance duty in Regulations, a cost shock in Costs. Choose with the whole chain in mind, not just the workshop.

Summary

  • Manufacturing is the conversion point of the business: materials and labour in, sellable product out — and it's where the profit-reinvestment loop physically turns.
  • It sets cost per unit through scale, yield and automation — and the make-or-buy choice decides how much of that you own versus rent from a supplier.
  • It sets the quality ceiling and the consistency floor, which determines your brand's promise and your pricing power.
  • It sets your capacity limit, lead times and flexibility, which constrain what sales can sell and how fast you can respond.
  • It is cash-hungry: work in progress, capital expenditure and long customer payment terms all bite before revenue arrives.
  • It forces a trade-off between low cost, flexibility and consistency — pick your corner on purpose.
  • Strategically, it's a driver of revenue and cost at the same time. Manage it with numbers, not habit: cost per unit, yield and on-time output, checked on a rhythm.
