08-3 Distribution as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 08 Distribution

Distribution is easy to file away as "the delivery bit" — something that happens after the product is made and before the money arrives. That framing is a trap. In reality, distribution is a decision that reaches backwards into how you design products, forwards into what customers you can even reach, and sideways into your cash flow, your tax bill and your staffing. Change one distribution choice and you usually change five other things about the business.

This subsection is about that ripple effect. We've already looked at what distribution is and the channels available; now let's treat it as a factor that runs through the whole business, the same way we treated raw materials, labour and manufacturing.

The physical thread that ties the business together

Picture the business as a single physical thread running from your suppliers' loading docks to your customer's front door. Every function you've studied sits along that thread.


The important word in that diagram is thread. Cut it anywhere and the whole line goes slack. A manufacturing plant that can produce 10,000 units a week is useless if outbound logistics can only move 4,000. A brilliant product that takes three weeks to arrive loses to a mediocre one that arrives tomorrow. Distribution sets the ceiling on what the rest of the business is allowed to achieve.

Upstream: suppliers and raw materials

Distribution starts before you make anything. That's inbound logistics — getting materials from suppliers to your operation.

Three factors from earlier chapters collide here:

  • Distance. A supplier two hours away can be reordered weekly. A supplier two oceans away needs a six-week buffer of stock sitting in a warehouse, which is cash you can't spend elsewhere.
  • Bulk and weight. Raw materials that are heavy, bulky or perishable — sand, milk, fresh produce — are expensive to move relative to their value. That pushes production towards the source. This is exactly why cement plants sit next to limestone quarries.
  • Reliability. The less reliable the inbound flow, the more safety stock you must hold. Distribution risk quietly becomes an inventory cost.

So your choice of supplier isn't just a purchasing decision. It's a distribution decision wearing a different hat.

Middle: manufacturing and product design

Manufacturing and distribution are two halves of one system. Where you put the factory determines your outbound logistics cost forever after; a plant far from customers is a permanent tax on every unit sold.

Distribution also feeds back into product design. A product that's awkward to ship — fragile, oddly shaped, requiring refrigeration — costs more to distribute than to make. Designers who ignore this create products that are technically excellent and commercially hopeless. The classic fix is designing for cube efficiency: how much product fits into a standard pallet or container. Squeeze 20% more units into the same space and you've cut your freight cost per unit by roughly a fifth without touching the factory.

Tie this to the product life cycle too. In the launch phase, small volumes and premium prices tolerate slow, expensive distribution. In the mature phase, volumes are huge and margins are thin, so distribution must become ruthlessly efficient. Distribution strategy has to move as the product ages.

Downstream: stores, sales and customer service

Outbound distribution decides where you are allowed to sell. Staying with direct delivery to your own town limits you to one market. Adding a national courier opens a country. Adding a third-party fulfilment network opens continents — with the trade-off that you lose some control of the experience.

This is where distribution and sales become the same conversation. A salesperson promising a two-day delivery is making a distribution commitment. A distributor's margin is a sale you never see.

And customer service is now largely a distribution story. "Where's my order?" is the most common support question in retail. Fast, accurate, trackable delivery is service; late or damaged delivery is a complaint. Returns — reverse distribution — are the same thread running backwards, and they're often the most expensive leg of all.


You can have two of those three at full strength. Pick fast and cheap and you stay local. Pick fast and wide-reaching and it costs a fortune. Pick cheap and wide and customers wait. Good strategists choose deliberately rather than drifting into a default.

Financials: revenue, costs and cash

Distribution shows up on both sides of the income statement.

On revenue: distribution is what turns a local product into a national one. Widening distribution widens the addressable market — the same product, the same brand, many more buyers. It's one of the few growth levers that doesn't require inventing anything new.

On costs: distribution typically accounts for a meaningful slice of the sale price, and the useful metric is cost-to-serve — the total cost of getting one order to one customer, including picking, packing, freight and returns. Two customers paying the same price can be wildly different in cost-to-serve. A customer ordering one small item to a remote address might lose you money; a customer ordering in pallet quantities might be your most profitable account. Rational distribution pricing starts here.

On cash: goods in transit are goods you've paid for but can't sell yet. Slow shipping lanes and long lead times lock up working capital. Speeding up distribution is often a cheaper way to free up cash than borrowing more.

Notice how this links straight back to the Financials and Costs chapters: distribution isn't a line item you manage in isolation, it's a set of levers that move revenue, cost and cash at the same time.

People and management

Distribution is labour-intensive in a specific way: warehouses, drivers, pickers, planners, customs clerks. That ties it directly to the Labour chapter — is that workforce permanent, agency, or automated? Automation of a warehouse is a heavy upfront cost that trades labour cost for fixed cost, which only pays off above a certain volume.

It also creates a management problem, because distribution cuts across departments. Manufacturing wants long production runs; sales wants urgent exceptions; finance wants lower inventory; the customer wants it tomorrow. Someone has to own the trade-offs. Companies that treat distribution as a shared responsibility often find nobody is actually accountable for it — and that's when costs creep and service slips.

The outside world: economy, tax, regulations and competitors

Distribution is unusually exposed to external factors, because it involves moving things across distances and borders.

  • Economy: fuel prices, freight rates and interest rates all hit distribution hard. When shipping costs spike, every business relying on long-distance distribution feels it immediately — and businesses with local supply chains become temporarily more competitive.
  • Tax: where your goods are held and where your customers are can create tax obligations, from customs duties and import tariffs to sales tax or VAT registration in new regions. Expanding your distribution footprint can therefore expand your tax footprint. This is a genuine reason small firms grow through distributors rather than direct sales.
  • Regulations: transport safety rules, driver hours, food and pharmaceutical handling standards, packaging and recycling rules, emissions zones. Each one raises the bar to entry — which protects established players and punishes newcomers.
  • Competitors: distribution is one of the most visible competitive battlegrounds there is. Once a rival offers free next-day delivery, that becomes the market's expectation, and everyone else must match it or explain why not. Distribution promises tend to ratchet upwards and never come back down.

A worked example

Imagine a small coffee roastery, Beans & Co. It starts by delivering to twelve local cafés in its own van. Distribution is cheap, personal and fast — and completely capped. It cannot grow beyond a day's drive.

Stage two: it adds a national courier. Sales triple, but freight cost per bag is high, delivery takes three days, and customers complain about stale coffee. The founder realises the product (bag size, packaging, roast date labelling) needs redesigning to suit the new distribution.

Stage three: it moves to a third-party fulfilment centre that stores stock in three regions. Delivery drops to next day, freight cost per unit falls because the centre ships in bulk, and the business can now sell nationwide. But now inventory is spread across three sites, cash is tied up in stock, the business has tax registrations in new regions, and it depends on a partner it doesn't control.

Every step widened revenue. Every step also added cost, complexity and risk somewhere else in the business. That's the nature of distribution as a factor: you never make one decision, you rebalance the whole system.

The strategist's view

Great businesses often win on distribution rather than on product. A slightly worse product that's everywhere beats a brilliant product nobody can get. So ask yourself a few questions as a strategist:

  • Does my distribution reach the customers my strategy needs, or does it quietly cap my ambition?
  • Is my cost-to-serve understood per customer and per channel, or averaged into a comfortable blur?
  • Can my distribution survive a fuel spike, a new regulation or a competitor's new promise?
  • Who in the business actually owns the trade-offs between speed, cost and reach?

Summary

  • Distribution is not one activity at the end of the chain — it's the physical thread connecting suppliers, manufacturing, stores, sales and the customer.
  • Upstream, it shapes supplier choice and inventory levels; downstream, it decides which markets you can reach and what service you can promise.
  • It moves revenue (wider reach), costs (cost-to-serve) and cash (inventory in transit) simultaneously, linking straight back to your financials.
  • It depends heavily on labour and requires clear management ownership, because it forces trade-offs between departments.
  • It's exposed to the economy, tax and regulation, and it's one of the most visible fronts in competition.
  • The core strategic trade-off is speed versus cost versus reach — choose two, and choose them on purpose.
