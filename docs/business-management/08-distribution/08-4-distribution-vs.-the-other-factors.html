08-4 Distribution vs. The Other Factors

Part of 08 Distribution

Distribution is the factor that touches almost every other one. It is the physical, financial and informational bridge between the things you buy and make and the people who pay you. That makes it unusual: most factors are either inputs (suppliers, raw materials, labour), outputs (revenue, customer service) or constraints (tax, regulations, economy). Distribution is the pipe that runs through all three. Pull on distribution and something else moves — a cost, a promise, a tax bill, a competitor's opening.

Distribution vs. Company

The company is the container; distribution is the shape of the container. A small company often is its distribution: the founder's van, a laptop and a courier account. As the company grows, distribution turns into fixed assets — leases, warehouses, trucks, software — and those decisions alter what kind of company you now are. Recall the earlier chapter on company types: a consultancy barely distributes anything, a brewery distributes constantly, and a retailer is essentially a distribution business with a shopfront bolted on. Every channel decision is also a structural decision about the company, because channel commitments are hard to reverse. Signing a five-year warehouse lease to win a customer today is how a flexible company becomes a brittle one.

Distribution vs. Competitors

Channel is one of the last honest moats. Anyone can copy your product description in a week; it takes years to build shelf space, exclusive distributor relationships or a delivery network that reliably hits a two-hour window. When you size up rivals in the competitors chapter, look at how they reach buyers, not just what they sell: direct, wholesale, marketplace, franchised, or a hybrid. Ask whether the channel is crowded or open. If every competitor fights for the same supermarket aisle, the shelf is the battleground and the retailer captures the profit. If you can build a route they can't copy — a route density, a cold chain, a local depot — you have leverage. The flip side: shared distributors and common carriers mean your competitor's promotion can quietly eat your stock allocation, and price wars are often fought in the channel before they reach the customer.

Distribution vs. Market

Market is buyers, sellers and the space between them — and distribution is that space made physical. A market you cannot reach is not a market you serve. This is why distribution defines your real addressable market size, not the total number of people who might conceivably want your product. It also drives segmentation: convenience buyers need you nearby; bulk buyers will wait a week for a pallet. In marketing language, distribution is the "place" in the four Ps, and it is the P most people under-invest in because it is invisible until it fails. Strategically, the question is: what is the cheapest route that makes me genuinely present in the market I have chosen?

Distribution vs. Product

Product characteristics largely dictate distribution design. Weight, volume, fragility, perishability and value density — value per kilogram — decide almost everything. Diamonds fly; sand is quarried locally. Fresh milk needs a cold chain measured in hours; canned milk needs a warehouse. Where a product sits in its life cycle matters too: a new product usually launches through selective, high-touch channels, then broadens as it matures and margins compress. Packaging is a distribution decision wearing a marketing costume — the pallet configuration and case size often save more money than the artwork. And don't forget returns and after-sales: if a product is likely to come back, design the inbound route before you design the outbound one.

Distribution vs. Suppliers

Suppliers sit upstream, and their location and reliability set your inbound logistics. A supplier who delivers to your door has taken on distribution cost — which is reflected in their price, so compare total landed cost rather than unit price. Techniques like drop-shipping (the supplier ships straight to your customer) and vendor-managed inventory (the supplier keeps your shelf stocked) move the distribution job without eliminating it; it is still your promise to the customer that gets broken when the shipment is late. A single-source supplier three continents away is a distribution risk before it is a price opportunity.

Distribution vs. Raw Materials

Raw materials are usually the worst distribution problem in the business: heavy, bulky, low value density, sometimes perishable or hazardous. That is why processing plants tend to sit next to where the raw material comes from — sugar beet refineries near fields, sawmills near forests — while finished goods travel further because they are worth more per kilogram. The rule of thumb: move the material that loses value in transit as little as possible, and move the finished good as far as the customer demands. Your raw material choice therefore locks in part of your distribution network before you have made a single sale.

Distribution vs. Labour

Distribution is one of the most labour-hungry factors: drivers, pickers, packers, loaders, planners. Labour availability and wage rates shape where you put a depot more than land prices do. In tight labour markets, distribution costs rise and automation becomes attractive; in slack ones, handballing is often cheaper than capital. The gig economy blurred this line by turning a fixed labour cost into a variable per-delivery cost — brilliant for flexibility, risky for service consistency. When you model a channel, model the labour market underneath it, not just the fuel and the boxes.

Distribution vs. Manufacturing

This is the tightest pairing in the book. Plant location is a distribution decision: one big efficient plant with long hauls, or several smaller plants with short ones. Batch size and lead time determine how much finished stock you must hold, and where. Flexibility in manufacturing lets you postpone — assemble, label or package late, close to the customer, so you ship generic components instead of finished variants. Choose your manufacturing strategy and you have chosen most of your distribution cost; choose your distribution strategy first and you constrain your manufacturing options just as firmly. Whichever you pick, decide them together.

Distribution vs. Stores

A store is a distribution channel that happens to have a door. Physical stores give reach, trust and instant availability, but they duplicate the inventory you also hold online. Modern practice merges the two: click-and-collect, ship-from-store, stores as micro-fulfilment hubs, returns accepted anywhere. That flexibility is powerful and dangerous — the moment your website undercuts your own store, you have channel conflict inside your own company, and your retail partners notice too. Store count is also coverage: more stores means denser distribution and shorter last-mile legs, usually at higher fixed cost.

Distribution vs. Sales

Sales sells the promise; distribution keeps it. A sales team incentivised purely on signed orders will happily sell into channels that are expensive to serve or impossible to fulfil, and the fallout lands on customer service and the returns budget. Align incentives with the true cost to serve. On the upside, sales is your best demand signal: territory data and order history feed the forecasts that decide stock positioning, route frequency and depot size. Sales territories and distribution routes are two views of the same map and should be drawn by the same people.

Distribution vs. Customer Service

Delivery speed, reliability and communication are customer service for most physical businesses. The whole discipline can be summarised as a trade-off frontier: you can have cheap, fast or reliable — stretch for all three and something breaks. Set a service level you can afford (for example, 95% of orders delivered next day), then design the network backwards from it. Returns logistics is the part everyone underestimates: a generous returns policy is a service feature and a distribution cost, and it should be costed as one. Great distribution is invisible; bad distribution is the only thing the customer remembers.

Distribution vs. Staff

Labour is the work; staff are the people you employ to do it. In distribution the distinction decides your business model. Own the fleet and warehouse and you carry recruitment, training, safety and retention obligations — plus control. Outsourcing to a third-party logistics provider converts those into a contract and a variable rate, at the cost of control and transparency. Either way, the capability you need is real: planners who can model routes, supervisors who can run a safe shift, and someone accountable for the number.

Distribution vs. Management

Channel design is a management decision, and it exercises every core job from the opening chapter. Planning: which channels, which geographies, which service levels. Organising: which parts you own and which you contract. Leading: getting performance out of partners and drivers you don't employ — influence without authority is the hardest skill in distribution. Controlling: measuring cost per order, on-time delivery and stock accuracy. Managers who treat distribution as a logistics chore rather than a strategic lever leave margin on the table every single day.

Distribution vs. Financials

Distribution is where accounting meets reality. Stock in transit is working capital tied up and invisible on a warehouse count. Owned warehouses and vehicles are capital expenditure with depreciation and maintenance; a 3PL contract is operating expenditure that keeps the balance sheet light. Either choice changes your return on capital, your break-even point and your cash conversion cycle — the number of days between paying suppliers and being paid by customers. Cutting inventory to free up cash without touching service levels is one of the most reliable ways to improve a business's finances without selling a single extra unit.

Distribution vs. Revenue

Revenue is units sold times price. Distribution determines how many units you can sell (availability) and, often, what price you get (a premium department store and a discount marketplace are different prices for the same product). So distribution is a revenue factor disguised as a cost factor, which is why it keeps getting underfunded by people reading only the cost line. Small operational choices compound here: a delivery slot that suits the customer, a subscription that ships on a schedule, a stock position that never runs dry. Revenue is also unforgiving — a stockout doesn't just lose today's sale, it teaches the customer to buy elsewhere. Recall the revenue chapter: the same product through a different channel is effectively a different revenue stream with a different margin.

Distribution vs. Costs

Distribution typically eats somewhere between 5% and 20% of revenue, and the last mile — the final leg to the customer — is usually the single most expensive part. So route density, drop size and minimum order values matter enormously: delivery cost per item falls as you deliver more items to the same place. That is the whole logic behind free-shipping thresholds and clustered delivery days. Separate fixed network costs (depots, systems) from variable costs (fuel, per-drop labour), because they behave differently when volumes rise or fall. And remember the service trade-off: every promise of speed you add pushes cost up faster than it pushes revenue.

Distribution vs. Economy

Distribution is unusually exposed to the wider economy. Fuel prices move delivery costs directly; interest rates change the cost of holding inventory and of financing vehicles; wages set driver and warehouse pay; exchange rates and trade flows decide whether importing is sensible. In a downturn, customers trade down to cheaper channels and slower delivery options, so your channel mix shifts without you changing a thing. Economic geography — where people and production actually are — quietly sets your network's optimal shape. Build slack into distribution plans, because this is the factor that punishes just-in-time thinking hardest when a shock arrives.

Distribution vs. Tax

Movement creates tax events. Cross a border and there may be customs duty, import VAT and clearance paperwork; the duty rate can decide whether you source locally or import. Hold stock in a state or country and you may create a tax presence — nexus — that obliges you to register and collect sales tax or VAT, which is why some businesses deliberately distribute from fewer locations. Transfer pricing between your own entities, excise duties on fuel and alcohol, and marketplace rules that make the platform collect tax on your behalf all sit on top of the physical network. Design the channel, then check the tax consequence — not the other way round.

Distribution vs. Regulations

Regulation shapes what distribution is legally allowed to look like: transport licences and driver hours, vehicle weight and emissions rules, cold-chain and food-safety requirements, dangerous-goods handling, packaging and recycling obligations, distance-selling and returns rights, and cross-border documentation. Two strategic points follow. First, compliance is a fixed cost, so heavy regulation tends to favour large incumbents with compliance teams and squeeze small entrants — unless the regulation creates a new market (reusable packaging, reverse logistics, EV fleets). Second, regulation changes the economics of geography: a low-emission zone can make one depot viable and another obsolete overnight.

Putting it together

Distribution rarely wins or loses a business on its own. It multiplies whatever else you have. Great product plus weak distribution equals a well-kept secret. Great distribution plus a mediocre product equals a fast-growing churn problem. The strategist's job is to notice that distribution is the factor where product, manufacturing, stores, sales, service, costs, tax and regulation all meet — and to make one coherent set of choices there rather than nineteen separate compromises.

Summary

  • Distribution is the connecting tissue of the business: inputs flow in, promises flow out, money flows back.
  • With the company, it decides structure and flexibility; channel commitments are hard to unwind.
  • With competitors and market, it is both a moat and the definition of your reachable market.
  • With product, raw materials and manufacturing, it is a joint decision — value density, shelf life and plant location set most of the cost.
  • With suppliers and labour, it is about who carries the work: inbound terms, wage rates, insourcing versus outsourcing.
  • With stores and sales, it is about channel mix, conflict and incentives — sell what you can serve.
  • With customer service, staff and management, it is about hitting a service level someone is accountable for.
  • With financials, revenue and costs, it converts working capital and last-mile economics into profit or its opposite.
  • With economy, tax and regulations, it operates inside rules and shocks you don't control — so build slack, check the tax, and treat compliance as both a barrier and an opening.
  • The strategic takeaway: get distribution broadly right and its cost becomes a moat; get it wrong and it quietly taxes every other factor in the business.
