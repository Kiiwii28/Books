08-2 Types of Distribution Channels

Part of 08 Distribution

One product, several doors

A loaf of bread, a dining table, a software licence — each one can reach its buyer in more than one way. You can hand it over yourself. You can sell it to a shop that sells it on. You can sell it to a wholesaler who supplies the shop. You can hand it to an agent who finds the buyer for a commission. Or you can push it through an online marketplace and let a platform take its cut.

Each of those routes is a distribution channel: the path a product takes from the producer to the final buyer, including everyone who handles it along the way. And the type of channel you choose decides things that never appear on your price list — who finds the customer, who holds the stock, who carries the risk, who gets paid when, and who owns the relationship once the sale is done.

Here's the reassuring part before we go any further: you don't have to pick one. Most healthy businesses run several channels at once, because different customers, products and regions need different doors. That mix is sometimes called an omnichannel or multi-channel approach, and choosing it well is one of the most profitable decisions a manager makes.

The first big split: direct or indirect

Direct channels — economists call them zero-level — put you face to face with the final buyer. Your own shop counter, a market stall, your own website, a subscription box, a factory outlet, your own sales team knocking on doors.

Indirect channels put somebody else in between. A retailer, a wholesaler, an agent, a platform.

Direct channel Indirect channel
Who finds the customer You Your channel partner
Margin per unit Highest — no one takes a cut Lower — the partner's cut comes first
Reach Limited by your own footprint and hours Extends as far as your partners reach
Cost to build You pay for shops, sites, staff The partner has already paid for them
Control of the experience Total Shared, sometimes barely yours
Data about buyers Yours Often sits with the partner
Cash Immediate You're waiting on someone's payment terms

Neither column is "better". They're a trade: direct buys you margin and control at the price of reach and investment; indirect buys you reach at the price of margin and control. Everything else in this subsection is a variation on that trade.

Counting the links: how long is the channel?

The simplest way to sort channel types is by length — the number of intermediaries between you and the buyer. A short channel has one or none; a long channel has three or four.


The longer the chain, the further the producer sits from the person who actually eats, uses or rides the product. Longer channels reach more buyers and smaller ones — a village shop can't buy a pallet of bread, but it can buy four loaves from a wholesaler. The price is that each link takes its own cut, and each link is another place where information, quality and speed can get lost.

Who the intermediaries actually are

The names blur across industries, but the roles are stable. The most important distinction — and the one beginners miss — is between merchants and agents.

Merchants buy the goods. They take ownership, pay you, and carry the risk that the stock doesn't sell. Wholesalers, retailers, industrial distributors and importers are all merchants. Once they've bought, the unsold loaf is their problem, not yours.

Agents never own the goods. They introduce, negotiate or close a sale and earn a commission or fee for it. Sales agents, brokers, commission-based interior designers, travel agents, and most online marketplaces work this way. The stock stays on your shelf and the risk stays on your books.

That single distinction decides who you invoice, who you chase for payment, and who is left holding the problem when the product doesn't move. Beyond it:

  • Retailers sell to final consumers (your Stores chapter lives here).
  • Wholesalers buy in bulk, break it down, and supply retailers.
  • Distributors do the same job in business markets, usually with more services attached — installation, servicing, credit.
  • Aggregators and platforms match buyers and sellers and take a fee: delivery apps, comparison sites, online marketplaces.
  • Franchisees sell under your brand using your system, as a licensed operator rather than a buyer.

Digital channels didn't change the physics

Online selling gets talked about as if it invented something new. It didn't. It lowered the cost of closing the same gaps you met in the Market chapter — space, time, information, trust, quantity and risk.

An online store closes the space gap without a building. A marketplace closes the information and trust gap with reviews and payment protection. Subscriptions close the quantity gap by delivering a small order every week. A delivery app closes the time gap at 9pm on a Tuesday.

Two patterns are worth naming because they recur:

  • Disintermediation — cutting out a link. Sell direct from your website and the retailer is gone.
  • Re-intermediation — a new link appearing. Those delivery apps, payment providers and marketplaces are intermediaries too; they just arrived later and take their cut in software rather than warehousing.

The gap rarely disappears. It just gets filled by somebody new — which is why every producer ends up choosing between who handles the gap, not whether anyone does.

How many outlets? Intensive, selective, exclusive

Channel type isn't only about length. It's also about how many outlets you use, and that choice follows almost mechanically from what kind of product you're selling.

Strategy How many outlets Fits which product type Example
Intensive As many as possible Convenience goods Milk, bread, chocolate, soft drinks
Selective A chosen set of good outlets Shopping goods Clothes, furniture, appliances
Exclusive One or very few, often per area Specialty goods Wedding cakes, luxury watches, franchise territories

You've met this logic already in the Product chapter: convenience goods are bought fast and compared on availability, so they need to be everywhere; specialty goods are sought out and rarely substituted, so rarity is part of the value. Put a £40 artisan loaf in a discount supermarket and you haven't expanded your market — you've muddled what your product is for.

Business-to-business channels look different

When the buyer is another business, the channel types change shape. You'll see:

  • A direct sales force, visiting buyers, quoting and negotiating (common for capital goods and big contracts).
  • Industrial distributors, holding stock of parts and consumables so customers can order in small amounts quickly.
  • Agents and manufacturer's representatives carrying several complementary product lines into the same buyers.
  • Original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) — your product becomes a component inside theirs, which is the Raw Materials and Suppliers chapters viewed from the other side.
  • Tenders and framework contracts, where the buyer chooses from an approved list rather than browsing.

Business buyers are fewer but larger, so a single channel relationship can carry a much bigger share of your revenue — and, correspondingly, much bigger risk if it ends.

Worked example: four doors at Rowan and Ash

Rowan and Ash, the furniture workshop from the Suppliers chapter, build dining tables. Each table costs them around £500 in materials and direct labour. They currently sell through four different types of channel, and the numbers explain why they keep all four.

Channel Type Price received Contribution per table Tables a month Monthly contribution
Own showroom Direct, zero-level £1,200 £700 3 £2,100
Interior designer Agent, one-level (20% commission) £1,120 £620 2 £1,240
Retail gallery Merchant, one-level, wholesale £650 £150 10 £1,500
Online marketplace Platform, commission + delivery £1,035 £535 4 £2,140
Total 19 £6,980

Look at what the table really says. The gallery gives the worst margin per table by far — £150 against £700 direct. But it takes ten tables a month, pays in batches, and doesn't consume a single hour of the owners' selling time. The designer brings the least volume but the most price-insensitive customers. The marketplace reaches buyers they'd never meet.

And the showroom produces the best margin per table but also the highest cost per sale, because every direct sale spends the owners' afternoon.

The honest lesson: no channel wins on every measure, and the right question isn't "which channel is best?" but "what does each channel earn me, after its cut, per hour and per table?" Notice too that the four channels together fill their production capacity of roughly twenty tables a month. Channel choice is a capacity decision as much as a marketing one.

What actually changes when you pick a channel

Once a channel is chosen, it reaches into nearly every other part of the business:

  • Costs and revenue. The channel's cut is a cost, and it changes what price is realistic. That's the Costs and Revenue chapters meeting your distribution decision head-on.
  • Cash and risk. Merchants buy stock from you — which converts your inventory into cash fast, at the price of a thin margin. Agents leave the stock on your books. That's a Financials question wearing a distribution costume.
  • Your competitors. Sell wholesale and you're suddenly bidding against industrial bakers on price and reliability, not against the café two doors down on atmosphere. The Competitors chapter made this exact point: channel changes the competitive set.
  • Customer experience. Whoever hands over the product owns the moment of truth — delivery speed, returns, a friendly hello. That's Customer Service, whether or not it's your staff doing it.
  • Labour. A direct channel needs sales staff, drivers and shop workers; an indirect one needs account managers. The Labour chapter follows from the channel you chose.
  • Rules and taxes. Cross-border channels bring duties, labelling rules and import paperwork — the Tax and Regulations chapters, arriving by van.

Choosing, and then checking

A short checklist covers most channel decisions:

  1. Who is the buyer, and where do they already shop? Follow the customer, not the fashion.
  2. What does the product need? Fresh, bulky, fragile, regulated and high-value products each rule channels out.
  3. Can you afford it? Direct channels cost money up front; indirect ones cost margin.
  4. What do you get after the cut? Contribution per unit — not revenue.
  5. Who owns the relationship? If the answer is "not you", make sure the reach is worth it.

Then close the management loop. Set a contribution target per channel, measure it monthly, and be willing to close a door that isn't earning its keep. Good strategists don't drift into a channel because everyone else uses it — they choose it deliberately, price it honestly, and check that it's paying.

Summary

  • A distribution channel is the path a product takes from producer to final buyer, including everyone who handles it.
  • The first split is direct (zero-level) versus indirect: direct buys margin, control and data; indirect buys reach and someone else's infrastructure.
  • Channels are also sorted by length — zero, one, two, three levels — and longer channels add reach at the cost of margin, speed and connection to the customer.
  • Intermediaries are either merchants (who buy the goods, take ownership and carry the risk) or agents (who earn a commission without ever owning the stock). That distinction decides who holds the risk and who you invoice.
  • Digital channels closed the same old gaps more cheaply; they didn't abolish intermediaries, they produced new ones.
  • Intensive, selective and exclusive coverage follows from product type — convenience goods go everywhere, specialty goods go almost nowhere.
  • Business-to-business channels run through sales forces, distributors, agents, OEMs and tenders.
  • The right question is never "which channel is best?" but "what does each channel earn me, after its cut, per unit and per hour?" — and whether you're measuring it.
