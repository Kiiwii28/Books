08-1 What Distribution Means

Part of 08 Distribution

Hand someone a perfect loaf of bread and ask what it's worth. In a locked cupboard, nothing. On a counter with a customer standing in front of it, four pounds. Same loaf, same recipe, same baker. The only thing that changed is where it was and who could get to it.

That gap — between a product being made and a product being bought — is what distribution exists to close.

The definition worth keeping

Distribution is everything that happens to a product between it leaving production and a customer using it: the moving, storing, splitting, sorting, informing and handing over that puts the right goods in the right place at the right time.

Three things are worth pulling out of that sentence.

It's an activity, not a place. A shop is a place. Distribution is the work — the van route, the warehouse, the wholesaler breaking a pallet into singles, the website that takes the order, the app that tracks the parcel.

It starts when the product is finished and ends when the customer has it. Everything before that is covered elsewhere in this book — raw materials, manufacturing, suppliers. Everything after is customer service, repeat purchase, and revenue. Distribution is the middle act.

It's deliberately broad. Most people hear "distribution" and picture a delivery van, the same way they hear "market" and picture a fruit stall. Both pictures are true and both are about a tenth of the story.

If you've met the classic four Ps of marketing — product, price, place, promotion — distribution is place. It's the one people forget, and often the one that decides whether the other three make any money.

The mismatch at the heart of it

Here's why distribution has to exist at all. Making and buying don't naturally line up. They're mismatched in four ways, and each one is a job somebody has to do.

  • Place. Production happens in one spot, or a handful of spots. Consumption happens in thousands of streets, kitchens and offices.
  • Time. Your oven runs at 4am; your customers want bread at 8am. A farmer harvests in August; people eat in February.
  • Quantity. A mill wants to sell flour by the tonne. A customer wants one loaf. Somebody has to split the difference.
  • Assortment. Nobody wants everything from a single maker. A shopper wants bread from one baker, milk from a dairy and coffee from a roaster — and wants all three in one trip and one payment.


Every distribution system ever built — a Roman road, a corner shop, a courier network, a vending machine — exists to shrink one or more of those four gaps. This is the same idea you met in the Market chapter, where buyers and sellers are separated by space, time, quantity and trust, and a market is the arrangement that bridges them. Distribution is that bridging, viewed from the seller's side.

What is actually involved

Strip away the specific businesses and the same handful of functions show up in every distribution system:

  • Moving — physically getting goods from one place to another.
  • Storing — holding them until someone wants them, which is how you beat the time gap.
  • Breaking bulk — turning a pallet into a case, a case into a loaf, a loaf into a slice.
  • Sorting and combining — gathering many producers' goods into one place so a buyer can shop once.
  • Informing — letting buyers know what's available, and letting producers know what's selling.
  • Transferring ownership and taking payment — the boring, essential plumbing that makes a sale a sale. This links straight to the Financials chapter.
  • Supporting after the sale — delivery, returns, installation, replacement.

Now the part that trips people up. You can outsource these functions, but you cannot delete them. If you don't store your own stock, someone stores it for you. If you don't deliver, the customer travels instead. If you don't sort an assortment, the buyer makes four trips to four shops and pays for it in time.

That's the honest version of the "middleman" question from the Market chapter. A wholesaler, a distributor, a delivery app or a retailer charges you money — but only earns it when the function they perform costs you less than doing it yourself. Remove them and the work doesn't vanish. It just moves, often onto your customer, who is less willing to do it than you'd hope.

What distribution is not

It isn't just transport. Driving goods about is one function out of seven. A business that moves things brilliantly but stores them badly, prices them badly or runs out of them constantly has a transport business, not a distribution system.

It isn't just shops. A shop is one route to a customer. So is a website, a market stall, a wholesaler, a vending machine, a courier, a locker, a phone call and a van in a lay-by. Which route you pick is the subject of the next section; here, just note that a shop is a type of channel, not a definition.

It isn't the same as inbound logistics. Bringing materials in from suppliers is upstream work — covered in the Suppliers and Raw Materials chapters. "Distribution" is usually used for the outbound half: getting your finished product out to buyers. The two halves are mirror images, and the same four mismatches apply to both.

And it applies to services, not just goods. A plumber's van, a salon's location, a barber's booking system and a consultant's video call are all distribution decisions. Services can't be stored, so the time gap has to be closed by appointment systems and capacity planning instead of inventory. That's the Product and Customer Service chapters knocking on the door.

Worked example: one loaf's journey

Take Priya's sourdough and follow it from the oven to somebody eating it.

Step What happens Who does it
Baked at 4am Production Priya
Loaded into trays and a van Handling and packing Priya
Delivered to a café at 6:30am Moving Priya
Sits on the café's shelf Storing The café
Sold at counter, with coffee Breaking bulk, assortment, payment The café
Eaten at 9am Consumption The customer

Priya's loaf costs her £1.20 in ingredients. She sells it to the café at £2.60. The café sells the equivalent onward at around £4.00 once it's on a plate with coffee. So the loaf nearly doubles in price between the oven and the customer — and Priya keeps almost none of that increase.

It's tempting to see that as someone taking a cut. Look again at the table. The café moved it across town, stored it, broke a baker's batch into single servings, combined it with everything else the customer wanted, took the risk that it might not sell, and handled the payment. That work is distribution, and the price gap is what the market pays for it. Remember the loop you've seen throughout this book — every function a business performs has to be paid for by somebody, out of the price the customer agrees to.

Now try a second example, because it makes the point even harder to ignore. A bottle of water. Extracting, treating and bottling it costs a few pence. The bulk of the price you pay is packaging, transport, shelf space and someone's willingness to keep it cold and handy. For gravel, cement, bottled soft drinks and bread, distribution often costs more than production. That's why industries built on cheap, heavy, bulky things — brewing, quarrying, soft drinks — tend to cluster their plants near their customers rather than their ingredients. Raw materials set the quality ceiling; distribution sets the reach.

The choice you can't easily unmake

Most business decisions can be reversed next quarter. Distribution decisions are different. Sign a shop lease, buy a van, appoint an exclusive distributor, list on a platform that takes 25% of every sale, or commit to a cold chain — and you've made a decision for years, not weeks.

And it doesn't just decide your costs. It quietly decides five more things:

  • How much of the customer's price you keep — the more hands between your oven and the customer, the more mouths sharing the money.
  • Who your competitors are. This is the point from the Competitors chapter worth repeating: the same product sold wholesale competes with industrial bakers on price and reliability; sold over a counter it competes with the café next door on atmosphere. Different channel, different fight.
  • How much you know about your buyers. Sell direct and every conversation teaches you something. Sell through three layers and you're managing a relationship with a distributor, not a customer.
  • How fast you can grow. Every channel has a ceiling — a counter serves a street, a website serves a country.
  • How much cash is tied up. Stock sitting in a warehouse or in transit is money you've already spent and can't use yet, which is exactly the working-capital squeeze the Financials chapter warns about.

One more thing, and it's the cheerful version: distribution doesn't only serve demand, it can create it. Deliver to villages that never had a shop, open a website that serves a country, put a coffee machine where no café would survive — and you've made a market that didn't exist before. Distribution is one of the few functions that can genuinely enlarge the pie rather than just slice it differently.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Availability is part of the product. Remember the three levels of a product: the augmented layer — delivery, returns, convenience — is where most competition actually happens. Distribution builds that layer, so it isn't an afterthought, it's the offer.
  • Run the four mismatches as a checklist. Place, time, quantity, assortment. For each one, decide: do we close this ourselves, or pay somebody else to close it? Write down the cost of both. That single exercise usually reveals where your margin is leaking.
  • Don't try to delete a function. If you stop storing, splitting or informing, somebody still does it — your supplier at a price, or your customer at the cost of a lost sale.
  • Back-of-envelope it before you commit. Every channel has a cost per unit sold. Compare them like-for-like, including commissions, delivery, packaging and the cost of holding stock. Then check it against what customers will actually pay — the same discipline you'd apply to any other price decision.
  • Manage it like the loop from the opening of this book. Plan the route and the volumes, organise the vans, warehouses and people, and then control it with two or three numbers: cost per delivery, on-time rate, and stock-turn. Distribution is the easiest part of a business to run blind, and the most expensive when you do.
  • Treat the channel as strategy, not plumbing. It's the single biggest lever on your margin, your reach, and who you're up against.

Summary

  • Distribution is everything that happens between production and the customer using the product — moving, storing, breaking bulk, sorting, informing, transferring ownership and supporting after the sale.
  • It exists because making and buying are mismatched in place, time, quantity and assortment. Every distribution system ever built closes one or more of those gaps.
  • It is not just transport and not just shops. Transport and retail are single functions and single routes within a much wider activity — and services need distributing too.
  • Functions can be outsourced but never deleted. A middleman is worth paying only when they close a gap more cheaply than you could.
  • Distribution often costs more than making the product, especially for cheap, heavy or bulky goods — which is why plants cluster near customers, not ingredients.
  • Channel decisions are sticky and structural: they set your margins, your cash, your reach, your knowledge of customers, and who your competitors are.
  • Distribution can also create demand, not merely satisfy it, by putting goods where no market existed before.
  • The strategist's habit: ask how your product gets from where it's made to where it's wanted, what that costs, and what would happen if it stopped.
