12-3 Sales as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 12 Sales

Everything in this book so far has been building towards something that hasn't happened yet. The materials were bought, the labour was paid, the product was made and stored, the shop was rented and lit. None of that is worth anything until somebody hands over money. Sales is that moment — and it's the moment the whole business gets judged.

That's why sales isn't a department that sits at the end of the chain, tidying up after everyone else. It's a factor that runs through the entire business: it's where cost turns into cash, where the market talks back to you, where promises get made that other people have to keep, and where a single discount can quietly undo a month of careful work.

Let's look at it as a strategist would.

Sales is the junction where cost becomes cash

Think of your business as a loop. Everything upstream — suppliers, raw materials, labour, manufacturing, stores — is spending money. Sales is where money starts coming back the other way.


Read the two arrows leaving Sales, because they're the whole point of this subsection.

The lower arrow is money. Sales converts stock into bank balance — the working capital question from the Financials chapter, resolved one transaction at a time. A business with brilliant operations and weak sales is a beautifully organised way of losing money slowly.

The upper arrow is information. Every sale teaches you something: which product people reach for, which price they'll tolerate, what they say when they walk away, what the rival down the road has just started doing. Sales is the business's ears. It's the only place in the company where you meet the market directly rather than theorising about it.

And note the arrow back into costs. Profit funds the next round of capability, and the feedback reshapes what you make next. That's the compounding loop from the opening chapter of this book — the loop only turns because sales keeps feeding it.

Sales is where the market answers back

The Market chapter taught you that price is set at the meeting point of supply and demand, and that elasticity decides whether a price rise makes you richer or poorer. Sales is where all that stops being theory.

A salesperson who comes back from a Tuesday morning saying "nobody would pay £4.40, but three people asked if we did a smaller loaf" has just handed you two chapters' worth of intelligence. The Product chapter picks up the smaller loaf. The pricing question goes back to Revenue and Costs. And the reason customers wouldn't pay goes straight to the Competitors chapter — because somewhere in that market, someone is offering an alternative they preferred.

This is why sales sits so close to the product life cycle. Products don't slide from growth into maturity because of a graph; they slide because salespeople start hearing "we've already got one" instead of "how much?". The sales conversation is the earliest warning system you have, and it's free.

A sale is not free

Here's the trap. Sales feels like the good news department — the money arriving after all the spending. Beginners treat it as though it costs nothing to make a sale happen.

It doesn't. Every sale carries its own cost: commission, advertising, a discount, a fee to a platform, the hours someone spent chasing it, the loss when a customer never pays. The number that matters is not what you sold, it's what the sale left behind — the contribution, from the Costs chapter, after every cost of getting it is paid.

That's the honest test of any sales activity:

Does one more pound of sales leave more than one pound of cost behind it?

If a channel costs you 25% in commission, that's not a scandal — but it has to be measured, not waved away. If a salesperson costs £30,000 a year plus commission, they need to generate enough contribution to cover their own base before they've earned you a penny. Sales is a cost centre and a revenue engine, and the strategist's job is to keep checking which one it's currently being.

The promise you make at the till

A sale is a commitment. The moment you take the money, you've promised something — a quality standard, a delivery date, a returns policy, a level of after-sale help.

Every one of those promises lands on someone else's desk:

  • Lead times promised in the sale become Manufacturing schedules and Distribution routes.
  • Stock promises become Stores decisions — sell it today or hold it for tomorrow?
  • Service promises become Customer Service commitments.
  • Payment terms agreed in the sale become Financials problems. Offer 60 days to win an account and you've just lent that customer sixty days of your cash, interest-free.

This is where sales and operations most often collide, and it's the seam from the Parts of a Company section: sales is paid to say yes, and the rest of the business is judged on whether yes was deliverable. A sale you can't fulfil isn't revenue — it's a complaint on a delayed payment schedule.

Sales reaches into every chapter

A quick sweep, because this is the standing habit worth building: any factor this central should touch everything.

  • Product — sales tells you what to make next and what to retire.
  • Market — sales is where supply and demand actually meet.
  • Competitors — your salespeople meet them daily; they're your best source of battlecard material.
  • Costs, Revenue and Financials — sales generates revenue, carries cost, and drives cash timing. Days sales outstanding is a sales decision wearing an accounting costume.
  • Labour and Staff — the salesforce is a workforce like any other: perishable hours, training curves, and a commission structure that shapes behaviour whether you designed it deliberately or not.
  • Distribution and Stores — every sale is a movement of goods and a reduction in stock.
  • Customer Service — a badly made sale is a support ticket waiting to happen, and a well-served customer is next year's sale.
  • Tax and Regulations — you collect sales tax or VAT on behalf of the state, and consumer protection, cooling-off rights and licensing rules all attach to the act of selling.
  • Economy — the sales pipeline is the earliest indicator you'll ever get that the wider economy is turning, long before it shows in your accounts.
  • Management — planning targets, organising territories and systems, leading the team, controlling the numbers. The four jobs, applied to selling.

Worked example: the discount that ate the profit

Priya's bakery is quiet in January, so she runs a 10% off everything week. It feels free — it's just a smaller profit, surely?

Per day Normal 10% off, same customers
Loaves sold 100 100
Price £4.00 £3.60
Revenue £400 £360
Ingredients £120 £120
Contribution £280 £240
Wages, rent, energy £230 £230
Profit £50 £10

A 10% discount took 80% of her profit. Not 10% — 80%, because the discount comes off the top while every cost stays exactly where it was.

To get back to £50 of profit, she needs contribution of £280 again. At £2.40 per loaf, that's 117 loaves — a 17% increase in volume just to stand still. And that assumes every extra loaf is a genuinely new customer, not a regular who would have paid full price anyway.

This is sales as a factor in one table:

  • The Market chapter's elasticity tells you whether customers actually respond to the discount.
  • The Costs chapter's contribution maths tells you what each loaf has to earn.
  • The Financials chapter tells you whether the cash is worth having anyway.
  • And the Competitors chapter tells you what happens when the shop down the road matches you — which they will, within about a week.

A discount is a sales decision. It is also, simultaneously, a pricing decision, a cost decision, and a competitive move. That's what "a factor across the whole business" means.

Three mistakes to avoid

Treating sales as a scoreboard rather than a system. Revenue is the outcome. Conversion rates, average order value, cost per sale and days to payment are the levers you can actually pull. Champion the levers.

Letting the sales target exist in isolation. A target with no link to capacity, stock or margin isn't a plan — it's a wish with a number attached. The Manufacturing chapter will tell you your ceiling; the Costs chapter will tell you what each extra unit is worth.

Confusing a sale with a customer. One sale is a transaction. A customer is an asset. The Customer Service chapter exists because the second sale costs far less to win than the first — and because the cheapest sale you'll ever make is the one from someone who's already happy.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Follow the money through the sale. Revenue minus every cost of getting it — commission, discount, delivery, the cost of the credit you extended — is the only number that counts.
  • Use sales as your sensor. Train whoever sells to come back with information, not just orders. The pipeline tells you about the economy, your rivals and your product before the accounts do.
  • Match the promise to the capacity. Sales that outrun manufacturing, stock or distribution don't grow a business — they damage it, one broken promise at a time.
  • Pay for selling deliberately. Commission structures, targets and territories are management decisions, and whatever you reward is what you'll get — including discounting, if discounting is easiest.
  • Close the loop. Plan a sales target, organise the channels and the people, lead the team, then control it with three or four numbers — conversion rate, average order value, cost per sale, and cash collected. That's the management cycle from the opening chapter, pointed at the till.

Summary

  • Sales is the junction where cost becomes cash — the moment the whole business gets paid — and the point where everything upstream stops being spending and starts being worth something.
  • It works in two directions: money in, and information back out to Product, pricing and strategy. The second direction is the one businesses waste.
  • A sale is not free. Every sale carries its own cost, so judge sales by the contribution it leaves, not the revenue it reports.
  • Sales makes promises that Manufacturing, Stores, Distribution and Customer Service have to keep, and sets payment terms that Financials has to survive.
  • It touches nearly every other factor — Product, Market, Competitors, Costs, Revenue, Financials, Labour, Staff, Distribution, Stores, Customer Service, Tax, Regulations, the Economy and Management itself.
  • The most dangerous sales decision is the discount, because it cuts revenue while costs stay put. A 10% discount can cost 80% of the profit.
  • Manage it like a system, not a scoreboard: conversion, order value, cost per sale, cash collected — planned, organised, led and controlled.
