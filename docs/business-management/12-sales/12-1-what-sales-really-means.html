12-1 What Sales Really Means

Part of 12 Sales

Every business in this book — the suppliers, the ovens, the vans, the staff, the stock on the shelves — exists for one moment. Somebody decides to pay you. That moment is a sale, and until it happens, every cost you've incurred is just money that hasn't come back yet.

Which makes it odd that "sales" is one of the least precisely defined words in business. So let's pin it down.

The definition worth keeping

Sales is the work of getting a specific buyer to exchange money for what you offer — and the exchange itself, once it happens.

Four words in that sentence are carrying the weight:

  • Specific. Sales deals in individual people, not crowds. "The market wants sourdough" is a marketing statement. "Sam will buy three loaves on Friday" is a sales statement.
  • Work. Selling is an activity with hours attached. Somebody answers the enquiry, asks the questions, writes the quote, follows up, chases the payment. That's organizing and leading from the opening chapter, aimed at one person at a time.
  • Money. Enthusiasm is not a sale. A verbal promise is not a sale. A sale is money, or a binding commitment to pay it.
  • Exchange. Both sides walk away with something. The buyer gets the benefit — the thing the Product chapter insisted was the real purchase — and you get cash.

And notice the quiet word: voluntary. You can't command a sale the way you can set a machine's dial. That's exactly why the Management chapter gave leading a job of its own, and why the Market chapter's "trust" gap matters so much at the point of sale. Nobody has to buy from you. Your job is to make saying yes easy and worth it.

One word, three jobs

"Sales" is doing at least three jobs in everyday speech, and muddling them causes real confusion:

  • The activity. "I work in sales." "She's good at sales." The work of finding, persuading and closing.
  • The transaction. "We made twelve sales today." Individual completed exchanges.
  • The total. "Sales were up 4% last quarter" — which really means revenue, the total value of everything sold.

That last one is where the trouble starts. A business can have rising sales and falling profit, and it happens all the time: new customers won with discounts, sales staff on commission, free delivery thrown in, returns piling up. The transactions go up; the money left over goes down. Keep the three meanings apart and you'll spot that trap while it's still small.

Sales is not marketing

This pair gets blurred constantly, especially in small firms where one person does both before breakfast.

Marketing decides the conditions: who you're aiming at, what you say, what you charge, where you're available. It works on groups.

Sales is the last few metres. It works on a person, in a conversation, with a decision to be made.

The old shorthand still holds: marketing makes the phone ring; sales answers it and asks for the money. Priya's shop sign, her prices and her window display are marketing. The moment a customer hesitates at the counter and she says "the sourdough's good today, I baked it an hour ago" — that's sales.

Sales is not persuasion at any price

Beginners imagine selling as pushing: overcoming objections, wearing people down, closing hard.

Real selling starts with diagnosis. Does this actually solve the buyer's problem? Can we deliver what I'm about to promise? A good salesperson is entirely comfortable saying "this isn't the right one for you."

Because look at what a bad sale costs you downstream. You promise a delivery you can't hit and the Distribution team takes the blame. You sell a product that doesn't fit and the Customer Service chapter inherits the complaint. You over-promise and the review site does the rest. Remember from the Product chapter: a brand is a promise, and the product and service are the keeping of it. Nothing breaks that promise faster than a salesperson who sold something the business couldn't deliver.

That's why the Revenue chapter defines revenue as price × the number of completed sales. Sales is the activity; revenue is the scoreboard. What a sale actually requires

For a sale to be possible, four things have to be true at once:

  1. A need the buyer feels. Not a need you can see — a need they know about, today.
  2. The means and willingness to pay your price. This is the Market chapter's demand curve, applied to one person with one wallet.
  3. The authority to say yes. In consumer sales, that's usually the person in front of you. In business-to-business selling, it often isn't. You can have a wonderful conversation with someone who then has to ask their finance director.
  4. Trust that you'll deliver. Will the bread arrive? Will the table match the photograph? Will the software work?

Sales work is mostly aimed at numbers 1 and 4. And look what number 4 is: the trust gap from the Market chapter — the reason strangers can trade at all. At scale, brands and reviews close that gap. Person to person, a salesperson closes it.

Sales is also the business's ears

Here's the part that gets forgotten. A salesperson is the only person in the company who hears, dozens of times a week, exactly why people say yes and why they say no.

That's intelligence, and it's free. It tells the Product team what to change, tells the Competitors work what a rival is doing differently, and tells the buyer of Raw Materials which size or grade customers are actually asking for. A business that treats sales as a one-way push goes deaf on purpose.

Worked example: three sales at Priya's bakery

Let's price three very different sales Priya makes in a single week — and count what each one really leaves behind.

Sale Price Cost of the goods Cost of winning and serving it Left over
A loaf at the counter £4.00 £1.20 £0.15 £2.65
A loaf wholesale to a café £2.60 £1.20 £0.10 £1.30
A bespoke wedding cake £180.00 £55.00 £45.00 £80.00

Look at the wedding cake first, because it's the sale everyone admires. Big ticket, months of lead time, a tasting session, a delivery and an hour of setup. It leaves £80 — which, across roughly eight hours of Priya's attention, is about £10 an hour.

Now the counter loaf. Twenty seconds of somebody's time, no negotiation, no quote, no follow-up. The shelf, the smell and the sign did the selling. At 100 loaves a day, that's £265 a day of contribution from a product nobody would call a "big sale".

And the wholesale loaf? The thinnest margin of the three, per unit — but 400 loaves a month, one conversation, a contract, and a customer who pays on the same day every month. That's not a sale at all; that's a system.

The lesson isn't that cakes are bad. It's that a sale is worth what's left after everything it took to win and serve it — not the number printed on the receipt. The impatient version of this chapter would tell you to chase bigger tickets. The strategist's version tells you to count the cost of the sale itself.

That cost has a name worth remembering: the cost to serve. It's a direct cost like any other, and it belongs in the Costs chapter alongside the flour. Discounts, commission, delivery, returns, the hours spent quoting a job you don't win — all of it comes off the top.

Sales, run like a function

Sales isn't a talent you either have or don't. It's a function you manage, using the same four jobs you met at the start of the book:

  • Planning. How much do we need to sell, to whom, through which channels, by when?
  • Organizing. Who covers which customers, how enquiries get logged, who follows up, what happens after a quote goes out.
  • Leading. This is where leading is most visible in any business. Selling involves far more rejection than most work, and keeping people going through it is a real management job.
  • Controlling. Conversion rate, average order value, win rate, cost per sale. A handful of numbers, checked on a rhythm.

And one more thing, which reaches everywhere: a sales forecast is the input to almost every other plan in the business. How much stock to hold (Stores), how many people to roster (Labour), what to schedule on the line (Manufacturing), what to order from suppliers (Suppliers), and how much cash will be in the bank (Financials). Guess the sales badly and everything downstream is wrong.

Two cautions before we move on

Don't sell what you can't deliver. It's the fastest way to convert a happy customer into an expensive problem, and it lands on Customer Service, Distribution and your reputation at the same time.

Don't confuse activity with selling. A busy shop, a full inbox and a packed trade stand are inputs. Only the completed exchange counts, and only the profitable ones count twice.

Summary

  • Sales is the work of getting a specific buyer to exchange money for what you offer — and the completed exchange itself. It deals in people, not markets.
  • The word covers three things: the activity, the transaction, and the total (which really means revenue). Confusing them hides the difference between growing sales and growing profit.
  • Marketing creates the conditions; sales closes the deal. Marketing works on groups, sales works on a person.
  • Good selling is diagnosis first — matching a real need to what you can actually deliver — not persuasion at any price.
  • Sales is where the loop closes: everything upstream is a cost until a buyer says yes, and sales funds the next round of everything else.
  • A sale requires a felt need, the means to pay, the authority to decide, and trust — which is the Market chapter's trust gap, closed person to person.
  • Sales is also the business's ears: it hears why people buy and why they don't, and feeds that to Product, Competitors and purchasing.
  • Judge a sale by what's left after the cost of winning and serving it, not by the ticket price — as the bakery's £4 loaf quietly proves.
  • Sales is a managed function: planned, organized, led and controlled with a few honest numbers, and its forecast feeds nearly every other plan in the business.
