12-4 Sales vs. The Other Factors

Part of 12 Sales

Sales doesn't live on its own planet. It's the moment your business touches someone else's money, and that means every other factor in this book is either pushing a sale toward you or standing in its way. If you've read the earlier factor chapters — Product, Market, Distribution, Labour, Financials and the rest — you already know each one in isolation. This subsection does the more useful work: it puts Sales face to face with all of them, one at a time, so you can see the two-way relationships and, more importantly, know where to look when sales are strong, weak, or quietly destroying profit.

A simple way to hold it all in your head

Think of Sales as the point where two loops meet.


The right-hand loop is the supply side: cash from sales pays for the things that make more product. The left-hand arrow is the demand side: the market and your competitors decide how hard the sale is. Almost every comparison below is a variation on one of those two loops. And the strategy lesson hiding in the middle is this: Sales is where a plan either turns into money or is exposed as a wish.

Sales and the demand side

Sales vs. Market

The Market chapter defines a market as buyers, sellers, and the space between them. Sales is the mechanism by which your firm actually occupies that space. Market size sets your ceiling; segmentation tells you which doors to knock on. No amount of sales brilliance sells ten thousand units into a market of four hundred buyers.

The relationship runs both ways. The market constrains sales, but sales also reads the market. Willingness to pay, seasonal swings, and price elasticity aren't abstractions in a textbook — they're what a salesperson learns in twenty conversations. That's why sales feedback should feed straight back into your market assumptions. A common rookie mistake is treating the market analysis as finished once the business plan is written; sales is the ongoing audit of that plan.

Sales vs. Competitors

Almost every sale is won against an alternative, and "doing nothing" is one of those alternatives. Competitors hold your price down, tighten your payment terms, and force you to invest in features you'd rather skip.

The most valuable thing Sales gives back to competitive strategy is win–loss intelligence. Why did the customer choose them? Price, delivery time, trust, specification, or an existing relationship? If you're losing 80% of deals on lead time, the fix belongs in Manufacturing or Distribution, not in a new sales script. If you're losing on trust, that's a Company and Customer Service problem. The instinct to blame the sales team is usually the instinct to fix the wrong factor.

Sales vs. Product

Product is what you sell; sales is how it moves. The blunt truth: no sales process rescues a product nobody wants, and a genuinely great product with no sales effort dies quietly. This is the product–market fit conversation, and Sales is where it's graded in public.

Product life cycle stage changes the selling job completely. In introduction, you're educating and often losing money per unit. In growth, you're racing to grab share before rivals arrive. In maturity, you're competing on price, convenience, and relationship. In decline, you're harvesting, cross-selling, and milking the last profitable customers. Same product, four different sales strategies — and this is exactly why the Product chapter's life-cycle section matters to anyone carrying a quota.

Sales also feeds Product. Feature requests, recurring objections, and "why does it break when I do X?" are the cheapest research a business ever gets. Ignore them and you'll pay later for the R&D you could have had for free.

Sales vs. Customer Service

Customer Service protects the sales you've already made and produces the next ones. The economics are straightforward: a repeat customer has already had their acquisition cost paid, so their next purchase is far more profitable than a first purchase from a stranger. A cancellation costs you the future stream, not just the last invoice.

The friction point is ownership. Sales hands the customer over; Service keeps them. If Service quietly promises things Sales never sold — free callouts, extensions, endless patience — the cost creeps up while Sales gets the credit for a deal that wasn't actually profitable. Good businesses define the handover precisely and track promises made on both sides of the line.

Sales and the supply side

Sales vs. Suppliers

Suppliers decide whether the thing you sold is actually available, at the quality you implied, by the date you promised. A stockout is a lost sale with a bad aftertaste, and the customer doesn't blame your supplier — they blame you.

The connective tissue is the forecast. Sales forecasts drive purchasing, and a bad forecast creates one of two expensive problems: too little stock (lost revenue and disappointed customers) or too much (cash frozen in a warehouse). This is also where you learn the discipline of "sell what we can deliver." Salespeople who promise delivery dates without checking supplier lead times hand Customer Service a mess to clean up.

Sales vs. Raw Materials

Raw materials set your price floor and your volume ceiling. When a commodity input spikes — steel, coffee beans, cocoa, cotton, microchips — the cost has to go somewhere: into the price, into a thinner margin, into a smaller pack size, or into the customer's patience. Sales is the function that has to have that conversation.

Raw materials also shape what Sales is allowed to claim. If you switched to recycled aluminium or certified cocoa, that's a selling point. If a key material is in short supply and you're allocating stock, Sales becomes a rationing function, deciding which customers get served first. That's a strategic choice, not an administrative one — and it's worth asking which customers you'd regret losing.

Sales vs. Manufacturing

Manufacturing makes the thing; Sales empties the warehouse. Capacity, minimum order quantities, batch sizes, and changeover times all shape what can be promised. This is why mature businesses run sales and operations planning (S&OP) — a regular meeting where the sales forecast meets the factory's realistic capacity, and the two argue until they agree.

There's a classic tension here. The factory wants long, steady, high-volume runs. Sales wants to say yes to whatever the customer asks for. The compromise shows up as pricing: a customer who orders in the factory's preferred pattern should pay less than one who demands a bespoke run next Tuesday. If you charge the same, you're subsidising chaos.

It matters whether you make to stock, make to order, or assemble to order. A custom maker sells before it makes — the order funds the production — which is a wonderful cash position. A make-to-stock manufacturer must forecast, then persuade.

Sales vs. Labour

Labour is human work, and Sales is a particularly measurable form of it. The unusual thing about sales labour is that it can be partly self-funding: commission means part of the cost appears only when revenue appears. That makes a sales team easier to scale than most functions — and easier to let rot, if the culture becomes purely transactional.

Labour availability also constrains sales in a way people forget. Selling more than you can install, service, or deliver doesn't create value, it creates angry customers. And because sales skills are portable, sales turnover is expensive: when a salesperson leaves, part of the pipeline and much of the customer relationship walks out with them.

Sales vs. Distribution

Distribution is how the product gets to the buyer; Sales is often the handshake at the end and the relationship that keeps the channel alive. The channel you choose redefines what "sales" even means. A direct sales force, an exclusive distributor, a retail chain, a marketplace, your own website — each requires different skills, different margins, and a different cost per acquired customer.

Two things bite here. First, channel conflict: sell direct at a discount while your retailer is trying to sell the same product at full price and you've just damaged your retail channel. Second, fulfilment as part of the offer: for a growing share of buyers, delivery speed and reliability are the sale. Your distribution network is a sales argument whether you market it that way or not.

Sales vs. Stores

In retail, the store often is the sale — the point of sale is a physical place, with footfall, layout, and staff who close. Online, the store is a checkout page that never closes, which is why e-commerce stretched the old store hours into a 24-hour sales channel.

The data flows both ways. Sales data — conversion rate, basket size, what gets returned — tells the store what to stock and how to arrange it. Store location and layout influence how many sales opportunities exist in the first place. A store in the wrong place with a great team still loses to a mediocre store at a busy corner. Effort can't fully compensate for position.

Sales and the organisation

Sales vs. Company

The Company chapter covers what a company is: a legal entity, a structure, a brand, a set of owners. All of that shapes what Sales can do. Entity type determines who can sign a contract. Corporate structure determines who approves a discount. Brand positioning determines what you're allowed to say and charge.

This is where a lot of sales teams accidentally damage the asset they sell under. A company positioned as premium cannot let Sales discount aggressively whenever a month looks weak — each discount teaches the market your "premium" claim was theatre. Conversely, a company whose strategy is cost leadership should want Sales to compete hard on price. The sales behaviour that's right for one company is fatal for another, and only the Company chapter tells you which one you're in.

Sales vs. Staff

Staff are the people; Sales is one thing people do. The useful reframe is that everyone sells a little — the person answering the phone, the driver who arrives on time, the accounts clerk who doesn't send a confusing invoice. Service staff generate repeat orders without a pitch. A curt receptionist can undo a good campaign.

For the formal sales function, the levers are hiring, onboarding, training, territory design, and above all compensation. Pay commission on revenue and you'll get discounting, because discounting closes deals. Pay on gross margin and you'll get salespeople who defend price. This single design decision often matters more than any sales training programme you'll ever buy.

Sales vs. Management

Management sets targets, allocates resources, decides incentives, and reviews performance — and Sales is usually the most measured function in the business, which makes it both powerful and prone to gaming. Everything from the "Core Jobs Every Manager Does" — planning, organising, leading, controlling — gets tested here.

The management trap is fixating on the number. Push hard enough on a monthly revenue target and you'll reliably get sandbagging (deals held back for next month), discounting, and channel stuffing (selling to customers who didn't want it). Good management separates activity metrics — calls made, demos booked, proposals sent — from outcome metrics — revenue, gross margin, retention. Activity metrics you can coach. Outcome metrics you can only judge.

Sales and the money

Sales vs. Revenue

This is the closest pair on the list, and the one most often muddled. Revenue is the aggregate money the business earns from selling: units × price, less discounts and returns. Sales is the activity. Revenue is the scoreboard.

Two nuances matter. First, not every sale becomes revenue in the same period — deposits, subscriptions, and prepayments can be recorded later under revenue recognition rules, which is a Financials chapter topic with real consequences for how you're paid. Second, growing revenue is not the same as growing profit, and confusing the two is one of the most common ways healthy businesses die. A 10% price cut on a product with a 30% gross margin leaves you with 20% margin — meaning you need 50% more volume just to stand still in gross profit. If your sales incentive rewards revenue alone, you're paying people to run that race.

Sales vs. Costs

Sales spends money to make money: commissions, travel, samples, trade shows, marketing support, and — the big invisible one — discounts, which are a cost wearing a friendly mask. The key ratio is cost to acquire a customer versus what that customer is worth over their lifetime. If acquisition costs more than the customer will ever return, growth is just a more efficient way of losing money.

There's a legitimate exception worth knowing: marginal thinking. If you have spare capacity, an extra sale at a low price still adds profit as long as it covers the variable costs of serving it. That's why off-peak pricing, late-night hotel rates, and empty-leg flights exist. The danger is doing this to customers who would happily have paid full price, which quietly resets the price everyone expects. Never let a marginal-pricing tactic leak into your core market.

Sales vs. Financials

Financial statements are the accounting record of what Sales did: revenue at the top of the income statement, accounts receivable on the balance sheet, gross margin in between. The gap between "we signed the deal" and "we got the money" is where businesses get hurt.

Selling on credit is offering a loan you didn't plan to make. Generous payment terms are a genuine sales weapon — bigger buyers often choose the supplier who'll wait 60 days — but that cash has to come from somewhere, usually your own reserves or a lender. And a customer who never pays isn't a sale at all; it's a loss with paperwork. Sales and finance should agree on credit limits before the champagne, not after.

Sales vs. Tax

Tax is collected by the seller on behalf of the government, which creates a crucial distinction: sales tax, VAT, or GST is not your revenue. You're an unpaid tax collector for a few weeks. Recording it as income flatters your top line and will make you look foolish — or worse — come audit time.

Tax also shapes sales directly. Tax-inclusive pricing has a psychological effect on buyers (a price ending at a round number including tax feels honest). Cross-border selling brings VAT registration thresholds, customs duties, and import paperwork that can make a small export order unprofitable. And when you design sales commissions, calculate them on revenue net of tax, or you'll be paying your team for money that was never yours.

Sales and the outside world

Sales vs. Economy

The economy is the weather, and Sales is the first function to feel it. Order books thin before the accountants notice. Deal cycles lengthen as buyers seek more approvals. Customers trade down, or trade up, or postpone. Sales enquiry rates are among the best leading indicators a small business has.

The strategic move most firms get wrong is cutting sales capacity in a downturn. It's the fastest way to cut cost, and it's often exactly backwards — when competitors retreat, market share is cheapest to buy. Businesses that keep selling through recessions tend to emerge larger. The exception is a business that was already fragile; then cutting is survival, not strategy.

Sales vs. Regulations

Regulations decide what you may say, to whom, and how. Consumer protection law gives cooling-off periods and return rights. Advertising rules make false claims an offence. Sector rules — financial advice, health claims, alcohol, gambling, lending — constrain the sales conversation itself and make mis-selling a legal risk, not just a moral one.

For B2B the rules are quieter but real: contract and unfair-terms law, data protection rules governing your prospect lists and cold outreach, do-not-call registries, and anti-bribery rules that turn a generous gift to a buyer into a criminal matter. The strategic upside is that regulation can be a moat. Where compliance is heavy and complicated, established firms with the processes already built have a real advantage over newcomers — a rare case where red tape protects incumbents.

Worked example: one pricing decision, six factors

Say you run a mid-sized furniture maker and you're considering a 15% price cut to chase volume. Watch the factors collide.

  • Costs and Financials: at a 35% gross margin, a 15% cut leaves 20%. You now need a 75% volume increase to hold gross profit.
  • Manufacturing: can your factory physically produce 75% more? Probably not without overtime, new machinery, or a second shift — all of which raise costs.
  • Suppliers and Raw Materials: more volume means more timber and fabric, at prices that may rise with your order size at exactly the wrong moment.
  • Competitors: they can cut too, and if they do, you've both given away margin for nothing. Price wars rarely produce a winner.
  • Market and Economy: the cut only works if demand is genuinely price-elastic. In many furniture segments it isn't — buyers care about design and delivery time.
  • Company: if you're positioned as a premium maker, the discount contradicts your whole identity.

You might still cut the price — to fill a genuinely idle factory, say. But now you're doing it deliberately, with eyes open, instead of hoping. That's the difference between owning a business and reacting to one.

Summary

  • Sales sits between two loops: the demand side (market, competitors, customers) constrains it, and the supply side (suppliers, materials, manufacturing, labour, distribution, stores) has to deliver what it promises.
  • Demand side: the market sets your ceiling, competitors set your price pressure, the product determines whether selling is hard or easy, and customer service converts one sale into the next.
  • Supply side: suppliers, raw materials, manufacturing, and labour decide what you can actually deliver; distribution and stores decide how the customer gets it; forecast accuracy links them all to Sales.
  • Organisation: the company's identity constrains what sales may promise, staff decide who sells and how well, and management's targets and incentives shape the behaviour you actually get.
  • Money: revenue is the scoreboard, costs and acquisition economics decide whether the score means anything, financials track the gap between a deal and the cash, and tax is money you collect, never money you earn.
  • Outside world: the economy is felt first in the order book, and regulation limits what can be said — while sometimes protecting those already compliant.
  • The strategist's habit: when sales results disappoint, ask which other factor is actually causing it before reaching for a new sales script.
