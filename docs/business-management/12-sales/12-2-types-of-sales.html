12-2 Types of Sales

Part of 12 Sales

"Sales" sounds like one activity. It isn't. A £3.50 coffee handed over a counter and a two-year contract to supply a factory with 40,000 bearings are both sales, and they have almost nothing else in common — not the length of the conversation, not the paperwork, not who does the persuading, not the odds of it happening again next month.

Sorting sales into types isn't box-ticking. Each type demands a different skill from your team, costs a different amount to win, and delivers its money on a different timetable. A business that runs a single sales approach across all of them will be brilliant at one and quietly hopeless at the rest.

Four cuts that do most of the work

You can slice sales in dozens of ways, but four cuts explain nearly everything that matters. Think of them as independent dials on the same machine — most real sales set a position on all four at once.


Change any dial and you change your costs, your cash and the kind of person you need to hire.

Cut 1: Who is doing the buying

This is the cut that carries over straight from the Product chapter's split between consumer and business products — and it should, because the buyer is what makes a product one type or the other.

Consumer sale Business sale Government or public sale
Who buys An individual or household A firm, to operate or resell A public body, funded by taxes
Typical order size Small Large Large, on a contract
Who decides Usually one person Often several — buyer, manager, finance A committee, bound by rules
How long it takes Seconds to days Weeks to months Months, usually by tender
What wins the deal Price, convenience, feeling Price per unit, reliability, support Compliance, price, track record
Where demand comes from Direct — "I want it" Derived — from their customers Budgets and political priorities

That middle column is worth one more sentence, because it's the most common blind spot. Business demand is derived demand: a café buys your bread because its customers are buying sandwiches. If the café empties out, the order stops, and no amount of charm on your part revives it. You're not selling to the café; you're selling to the café's customers at one remove.

The right-hand column carries its own flavour of pain. Public sector sales are gone through a formal tender — a published process where you bid against others on written criteria. Slow, paper-heavy, and heavily shaped by the Regulations chapter, but the contracts are often large and long, and public bodies rarely go bust mid-contract.

Cut 2: How the sale reaches the buyer

You've already met this as the direct-versus-indirect question from the Distribution chapter, and the same trade applies: direct routes keep more margin and more control but cost you a building, a website or a sales team; indirect routes reach further but somebody else takes a cut and owns the relationship.

In sales terms, the practical difference is who owns the conversation:

  • Direct — your shop counter, your website, your van, your rep. You talk to the buyer.
  • Retail — a shop buys from you and sells on. You talk to the shop; the customers belong to them.
  • Wholesale — you sell in bulk to someone who breaks it down for smaller buyers.
  • Online and marketplace — a website or platform closes the sale, often taking a percentage.
  • Agent or broker — someone introduces and negotiates without ever owning the goods.

Each route has its own cost per sale, and they differ by more than you'd guess. A purchase on your own website might cost you 2% in fees and a few minutes of picking. The same purchase through a delivery app might cost 25%. A wholesale contract might cost you an afternoon of negotiation a quarter. There's no universally "better" route — only a route whose cost fits the margin you make on the product.

Cut 3: How the persuasion actually happens

Three broad styles, running from cheapest per sale to most expensive.

Self-service. Nobody persuades anyone. The product persuades itself through packaging, price tag, shelf position, photos, reviews and a website that loads fast. Cheapest possible sale, and it only works when the decision is small, the product is familiar and the buyer already knows what they want. This is the natural match for the convenience products from the Product chapter — milk, bread, chocolate — which is why those products fight over shelf space rather than for salespeople.

Personal selling. A human being talks to the buyer. One-to-one, or one-to-a-group. It costs far more per sale — wages, commission, travel, the time spent on buyers who say no — and it earns its keep when the order is large, the product is complicated, or the buyer needs reassurance. Think of the bike shop owner spending twenty minutes fitting a customer properly to a bike.

Consultative (or relationship) selling. A slower, deeper version of personal selling. Instead of pitching a product, you diagnose the buyer's problem and design a solution around it. Long cycles, several meetings, often several people on the buyer's side. This is the normal shape of high-value business-to-business sales — and it only makes sense when the deal is big enough to pay for the time.

Here's the trap. The style must match the product and the price. Use hard-sell tactics on a £2 impulse purchase and you're burning pounds to make pennies. Use self-service on a £40,000 piece of industrial equipment and you'll lose every serious buyer. And a firm that uses a consultative approach on a commodity the buyer can price-check in ten seconds will simply be out-sold by a rival who got to the point.

One more distinction worth carrying, straight from the Competitors chapter: transactional sales compete on price and speed; consultative sales compete on understanding and trust. Those are the same two battlegrounds — price versus everything that isn't price — just wearing sales clothes.

Cut 4: One-off or repeating

Ask a simple question about any sale: will it happen again on its own?

A one-off sale — a wedding cake, a sofa, a house move — is won, delivered and closed. You must go and find the next customer from scratch.

A recurring sale — a subscription, a weekly bread delivery, a maintenance contract, a retainer — is won once and then repeats. The first sale costs money to win; every repeat after that earns money almost for free.


This is why businesses obsess over retention, and why the Customer Service chapter sits right next to this one: service is a sales channel. A delighted regular is a sale you don't have to go and win. The Revenue chapter picks up the same thread — recurring revenue is more predictable, which makes it worth more to a business than a lumpy pile of one-off sales, even when the totals look identical on paper.

It also splits sales roles in two. Hunters chase new business, and need resilience and a thick skin. Farmers look after accounts, and need patience and warmth. Different people, often different pay structures — and one of them is usually more valuable to you than the other, depending on where your business is in its life.

Two more cuts worth knowing

  • Domestic or cross-border. Selling abroad opens a bigger market but drags in customs duties, currency swings, foreign consumer law and tax registration. The Tax, Regulations and Economy chapters all arrive on the same pallet as the goods.
  • Goods, services or digital. A service can't be stored, so it's sold against capacity — eight seats in Priya's class, and an unsold seat tonight is money gone forever. A digital product has almost no cost per extra sale, which changes the economics of discounting completely.

Worked example: the six sales at Priya's bakery

What she sells Buyer Route Persuasion Repeats?
A loaf over the counter Consumer Direct shop Self-service Weekly habit
A birthday cake, ordered by phone Consumer Direct, made to order Personal selling Rarely
Weekly bread to local cafés Business Direct wholesale Consultative — reliability and price per unit Every week, contracted
Bread via a delivery app Consumer Indirect platform Self-service plus reviews Unpredictable
Saturday bread-making class Consumer Direct, booked online Self-service, capacity-limited Occasional, word of mouth
A school's termly bread order Public sector Tendered contract Written bid, then a relationship Every term

Six types of sale from one set of ovens, and look what each demands. The counter needs no salesperson at all — it needs stock, layout and a fair price. The cake needs someone who can ask good questions and quote confidently. The café contract needs a relationship, a delivery you never miss, and thirty days of patience while you wait to be paid. The council needs compliance paperwork and three months of nerves.

Now the economics. Each sale has a different cost per sale and a different margin per sale. The £4 counter sale costs pennies to win and pays the same day. The café contract may take six hours of negotiation and a sample drop-off — call it £150 of your time — to win £400 a month for two years. That's a superb return, but you only see it if you count the whole sale rather than the first invoice. A business that judges every route by the same "average" is flying blind, because the average of six very different things describes none of them.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Match the style to the stake. Self-service for small, familiar, frequent purchases; personal selling for larger, complicated ones; consultative selling for big, risky ones. Getting the style wrong wastes the most expensive resource you have — attention.
  • Know your cost per sale, per route. Not your average price, not your average margin — what it actually costs, in fees, wages and hours, to get one sale through each door.
  • Treat recurring sales as assets, not events. A subscription or contract pays back its acquisition cost many times over. That's why the Customer Service chapter is really a sales chapter in disguise.
  • Build for the mix, not the average. Different types need different people, pay and training — the Labour and Costs chapters, arriving in your sales plan.
  • Watch the cash timing. The type of sale decides when the money lands, not just how much. Counter cash today, wholesale in thirty days, public sector in sixty. That's the Financials chapter, and it's why a busy month can still leave you short.
  • Stay on the right side of the line. Consumer rights, cooling-off periods, honest claims and rules about who you may sell to are all real. Mis-selling isn't a clever tactic; it's a Regulations problem with your name on it.
  • Run the loop. Set targets by sale type, organise the routes and the people who run them, lead the team through the rejections, and then control it — cost per sale, conversion rate, repeat rate — checked monthly, not annually.

Summary

  • "Sales" covers many different activities. Four cuts do most of the work: who buys, how the sale reaches them, how the persuasion happens, and whether it repeats.
  • By buyer: consumer sales are fast and personal; business sales are larger, slower, committee-decided and driven by derived demand; public sector sales go through tenders and rules.
  • By route: direct, retail, wholesale, online and agents — each with its own cost per sale and its own degree of control over the customer relationship.
  • By persuasion: self-service (cheapest per sale, suits small familiar purchases), personal selling (costlier, suits larger and riskier ones) and consultative selling (slowest and deepest, suits high-value solutions).
  • By rhythm: one-off sales must be won again from scratch; recurring sales pay back their acquisition cost over and over, which makes customer service a genuine sales channel.
  • Transactional sales compete on price and speed; consultative sales compete on understanding and trust — the same two battlegrounds your competitors are fighting on.
  • The strategist's move: choose the type deliberately, price its true cost per sale, match the people and the cash flow to it, and measure conversion and repeat rates rather than just revenue.
