09-1 What Labour Means

Part of 09 Labour

When an owner says "our biggest cost is labour," they usually mean the payroll — the row of names on the wage sheet. Fair enough, but that answer hides the interesting part. Labour isn't the names. It's the effort those names supply: the baking at four in the morning, the driving, the lifting, the thinking, the patient explanation to a confused customer at the counter.

So here's the definition worth carrying through the rest of this chapter:

Labour is the human effort — physical and mental — that a business uses to produce and sell what it offers.

Three parts of that sentence are doing real work, and each one changes how you manage it.

Effort, not people

Start with the thing most people muddle. A person and their labour are not the same thing.

Priya's head baker, Dario, is a person. The eight hours he spends shaping dough on Tuesday is labour. When he's asleep, on holiday, or off sick, he's still a person — he just isn't supplying any labour. His labour is a flow that arrives by the hour and stops when he stops.

Follow that idea and some odd cases sort themselves out:

  • Priya before her first hire. No employees at all — but she was supplying an enormous amount of labour, baking, ordering, bookkeeping and locking up. No staff, plenty of labour.
  • Her brother-in-law, fixing the oven for free on a Sunday. Unpaid, but unquestionably labour.
  • A volunteer at a charity shop. Labour.
  • A bread-slicing machine. Not labour. Useful, capital, but not labour.

The last one matters because replacing labour with machines is a genuine strategic choice — and it only makes sense if you know precisely what you're replacing. You're replacing hours of effort, not a human being. (The Manufacturing chapter picks up exactly this thread.)

Where labour sits among the inputs

Economists have long sorted the deep inputs every business needs into a handful of categories. Labour is one of them.


Land, labour, capital and enterprise. You've met the first one wearing a different coat in the Raw Materials chapter, the third in Financials, and the fourth is the owner's own gamble. Labour is the input with a heartbeat — and that's not a sentimental point, it's a practical one, because a heartbeat behaves nothing like a sack of flour.

Five ways labour refuses to behave like an input

This is the heart of the section. Every other factor of production is obedient. Labour isn't, and the differences are precisely what make managing people a job rather than a calculation.

You can't own it — you rent it. You buy flour outright. You don't buy Dario; you buy slices of his day. He keeps the deed to himself, which means he can also take it elsewhere. This is why the Management chapter gives leading a job of its own: you can't command a person the way you set a machine's dial. You have to give them a reason.

You can't store it. Unused flour keeps. An unused Tuesday afternoon doesn't. If the bakery is quiet at 2pm, Priya can't bank those idle hours for December. Labour is perishable, in the same way fresh milk is perishable — which is why the week's rota is a planning document, not paperwork.

It's inseparable from the person. You don't hire a skill. You hire a whole human being, with their good days, bad days, ambitions, family, and the fact that their mother is in hospital this week. A supplier relationship can stay transactional. A labour relationship can't.

It's heterogeneous. One hour isn't one hour. Dario's hour might produce forty loaves; an untrained temp's hour might produce twenty-five, or the same forty with twice the waste. Costs chapters love neat per-unit numbers; labour refuses to give you one unless you measure carefully.

It can choose, learn, and leave. Labour is the only input that can refuse, argue, suggest a better way, and genuinely improve with practice. It's also the only input that can walk out on a Friday and take what it knows to the shop down the road. Hold that thought whenever the Competitors chapter comes up — you're competing for talent against the same firms you compete for customers against, sometimes on the same street.

There's a lovely consequence hidden in that list. Labour is the only input that can make all the other inputs better. Machines only improve because someone thought about it. Suppliers only improve because someone negotiated. Raw materials only get used well because someone counted. Every gain in the business eventually traces back to a person's effort.

The price of labour

Labour is bought and sold, which means there's a labour market — the same buyers-and-sellers machinery you met in the Market chapter, with one swap. Employers are the buyers, workers are the sellers, and the thing traded is human effort.

The going price is the wage or salary, set the same way any other price is: by supply and demand. Scarce skills command more; a town with fifty applicants for three jobs pushes the other way. Employers pay in several shapes — hourly wages, annual salaries, piece rates, commission, tips, and profit shares.

One caution before we move on. The wage isn't the whole cost of labour. Add payroll taxes, training, uniform and equipment, the dip in productivity while someone learns, and the cost of recruiting their replacement. That fully loaded figure is the honest number, and the Costs, Tax and Financials chapters all take a bite out of it later.

Three words that get confused

Labour, staff and management get used interchangeably in casual speech. They're different things, and keeping them apart keeps the next two chapters clear.

  • Labour is the effort itself.
  • Staff are the people who supply it — the subject of the Staff chapter.
  • Management is what points it at a goal — the four jobs from the opening of this book, applied to people.

Notice Priya could have plenty of labour and no staff. She can never have staff without labour.

Worked example: a week of Priya's labour

Here's one ordinary week at the bakery, written the way a strategist would write it:

Who Hours What the effort consists of Paid?
Priya (owner) 50 Baking, ordering, rota, accounts, supplier calls No — draws profit instead
Dario (head baker) 38 Baking, quality, training Mel Yes
Mel (baker) 24 Baking, counter cover Yes
Two Saturday staff 16 Counter, cleaning Yes
Priya's brother-in-law 4 Servicing the oven No
Total 132 hours

Two numbers live in that table, and they answer different questions.

Headcount says three employees plus an owner. That's the number that shows up in a staffing plan, and it's what most owners mean when they say "we're a small team."

Labour hours say 132. That's the number that actually produced the week's bread. It's the resource you schedule, the resource that runs out at Christmas, and the resource you measure output against.

Priya's week turns over roughly £2,400 in sales. That's about £18 of revenue per labour hour — a figure she can track, compare month to month, and improve. She can't track "three employees" in any useful way at all.

And look hard at that top row. Fifty of the 132 hours — nearly 40% of the week's effort — are unpaid and invisible in the accounts. That's the most common blind spot in small business. The owner's own labour isn't on the payroll, so it feels free. It isn't. It's the scarcest, least replaceable input the business has, and a business that only shows a profit because the owner is quietly donating fifty hours a week is not really profitable. It's subsidised.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Count hours, not just heads. Headcount tells you about cost; labour hours tell you about capacity. You need both numbers, and they answer different questions.
  • Remember it's perishable. An unplanned hour is gone. If your week has idle troughs and frantic peaks, that's a rota problem and a planning problem, not a lack of effort.
  • Treat retention as strategy, not sentiment. This is the one input that can leave and take its knowledge to a rival. Pay, training and how people are treated all show up in the Revenue line eventually — through quality, speed and whether customers come back.
  • Invest in it, because it's the only self-improving input. As the productivity loop in the next subsection will show, a better-trained hour isn't just a happier hour. It's a cheaper one.
  • Price it properly. Wages are a market price shaped by scarcity, local rivals, tax and regulation — not something you can simply decide.
  • Never call the owner's labour free. Budget it, even if only as a line in your own head. If the business can't cover the market rate for what you do, it can't yet cover what it costs.
  • Ask what you're actually buying. When you hire, you're buying effort over time — with a personality, a learning curve, and an off switch you don't control. That's the whole reason the Management chapter's leading job exists.

Summary

  • Labour is the human effort — physical and mental — that a business uses to produce and sell what it offers. It's an activity, not a person and not a payroll.
  • It is one of the classic factors of production, alongside land, capital and enterprise.
  • Labour behaves unlike every other input: you can't own it (only rent it), can't store it, it's inseparable from the person, it's heterogeneous hour to hour, and it can choose, learn and leave.
  • It's the only input that can make every other input better, because improvements ultimately come from somebody's thinking.
  • Its price is the wage, set in a labour market by supply and demand — and the true cost is the wage plus tax, training, equipment and the productivity dip.
  • Keep three words apart: labour is the effort, staff are the people who supply it, management directs it.
  • Measure in hours as well as heads, remember hours are perishable, and never treat the owner's own labour as free.
