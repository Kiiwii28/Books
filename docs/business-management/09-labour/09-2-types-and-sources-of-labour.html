09-2 Types and Sources of Labour

Part of 09 Labour

You've met labour as the human effort — physical and mental — that a business uses to make and sell things. That definition is the what. This subsection answers the two questions that follow immediately, and that every manager has to answer in practice: what kind of labour are we talking about, and where does it actually come from?

Get those two straight and a lot of downstream decisions — your cost structure, your flexibility, your training budget, your exposure to the labour market — stop being mysteries and start being choices.

Types of labour: four useful lenses

"Labour" covers a hospital consultant and a Saturday dish-washer, so the word alone tells you nothing. Four lenses sort it into something you can manage.

1. By skill: how easily could you replace them?

Band What it means Examples What it costs you
Entry-level No prior training needed, learned on the job Packing, cleaning, kitchen porter Low wage, high turnover
Semi-skilled Some short training or a licence Forklift driver, barista, basic assembly Moderate wage
Skilled A trade or formal qualification Baker, electrician, chef, bookkeeper High wage, slow to replace
Professional / specialist Long formal training plus judgement Engineer, accountant, senior developer Highest wage, genuinely scarce

Two consequences follow, and they're the same two ideas you've met before in different clothes.

First, skill is a labour-market scarcity problem. Remember the Market chapter: price is set where supply meets demand. The "price" here is the wage, and the supply is the number of people who can do the job. A town with twelve bakeries and four qualified bakers is a market where the bakers name their terms. A town with three bakeries and fifty applicants is one where you do.

Second, skill can be manufactured. Hiring a ready-made specialist is the buy option from the Suppliers chapter. Training someone up is the make option — slower, cheaper per hour once it lands, and it builds loyalty. A good strategist is always quietly asking which of the two is cheaper over three years, not three weeks.

2. By employment type: how tightly are they attached to you?

This is the lens with the biggest strategic consequences, because it decides how your costs behave.

Type Your commitment Cost behaviour Typical use The catch
Permanent full-time Ongoing, with notice and rights Fixed Core, steady work You pay in a quiet month
Permanent part-time Ongoing, fewer hours Fixed (smaller) Covering daily peaks More complex rotas
Fixed-term Until a set date Fixed but time-limited Projects, leave cover Ends abruptly
Casual / zero-hours No guaranteed hours Variable Unpredictable spikes Insecurity, weak skill-building
Agency temp The agency employs them Variable Sickness cover, seasonal rush Pricier per hour, less commitment
Freelance / contractor A project or contract Variable Specialists, one-off jobs The tax status must be genuine
Apprentice A fixed training period Fixed, often subsidised Building skilled staff Time before they're productive
Family / volunteer Informal Usually fixed Small firms, charities Boundaries blur

Read that as a ladder of commitment, and notice that each rung down buys flexibility and costs you something else.


Move left and you get loyalty, consistency, accumulated skill and a cheaper hourly rate. Move right and you get the ability to shrink your wage bill the moment demand does. This is the Costs chapter's fixed-versus-variable split wearing a uniform: a permanent employee is a cost you carry whether you sell anything or not, while an agency temp arrives only when the work does.

Which is right depends entirely on how well you can forecast. Steady demand rewards permanent staff. Spiky demand rewards the right-hand rungs — at the price of people who don't know your product, your standards or your customers, which is a Product and Customer Service problem as much as a cost one.

One warning while you're here: employment status isn't a free choice. Whether someone is genuinely self-employed or is really an employee is decided by law, not by the label on the contract. Firms that call everyone a "contractor" to dodge payroll taxes and employment rights tend to meet the Tax and Regulations chapters rather abruptly.

3. By role: do they touch the product?

Direct labour works on the thing you sell — bakers, machinists, drivers, sales staff. Indirect labour keeps the place running — supervisors, cleaners, bookkeepers, maintenance, HR. It's exactly the same split you met with raw materials: direct labour usually scales with output and lands in cost of goods sold, while indirect labour behaves more like overhead and barely moves when sales dip. Get that distinction wrong in your costing and you'll confidently misjudge which products make money.

4. By when and where the work happens

Seasonal and cyclical labour: harvest pickers, Christmas retail staff, summer tourism, month-end accountants. Shift-based labour: night bakers, 24-hour call centres. Located labour: on-site, remote or hybrid, or offshore entirely. Each choice changes your recruitment pool dramatically — advertise a remote role and you compete nationally; demand on-site presence and you compete with every employer within commuting distance.

Where labour comes from

Before the list, the frame. There is a labour market, and it behaves like any other market from the Market chapter: buyers (employers) and sellers (workers), with the wage as the going price. You are not the only buyer, and your best candidate is somebody else's employee too. Hold that thought while we go through the sources.

External sources

  • The local labour pool. For most small businesses this is the main one: the people who live within a reasonable journey of your door. Its size depends on population, housing, transport links, and how many other employers are fishing in the same pond.
  • The education pipeline. Schools, colleges, universities, apprenticeship schemes and training providers. Slow-burn, but they produce people shaped to your needs if you build a relationship — and government often subsidises apprenticeships, which is training part-financed by the tax system.
  • Other employers. You can hire trained people away from a rival, and sometimes you should. You'll pay the market rate, you may inherit their habits, and you should know that the traffic runs both ways — the Competitors chapter's warning about rivals applies to the people as much as the customers.
  • Referrals and word of mouth. The cheapest source, and usually the best cultural fit. The risk is a workforce that all looks and thinks the same, which is a real cost you'll only notice when you need a new idea.
  • Agencies, labour providers and gig platforms. Instant, flexible, and priced accordingly. Ideal for covering a sick day, a seasonal rush or a role you can't fill. Poor as a permanent answer.
  • Migration. People moving for work, from another region or another country. This is a major source for agriculture, food processing, care, hospitality and technology — and it arrives tangled in visas, right-to-work checks and sponsorship duties, which is the Regulations chapter showing up in your recruitment process.
  • The pools people forget. Parents returning after time out, older workers wanting fewer hours, students, people with disabilities, veterans, ex-offenders. Reaching them is often a matter of adjusting a shift pattern, a premises or a training plan rather than spending more money — and these groups tend to reward the employers who bother.

Internal sources

  • Promotion. You already know them, they already know you, and promoting sends a signal to everyone else that effort is rewarded.
  • Retraining and redeployment. When a product line declines or a process is automated, the answer is sometimes to move the person rather than replace them. That's the Product life cycle problem meeting a Labour solution.
  • Growing your own. Apprentices and trainees are slower to become productive, but you shape exactly the skills you need and you rarely lose them in the first two years.

The source you don't hire

Wages climbing faster than your prices? You can also need less labour. Automation, better tools and process redesign all act as sources of "labour" without a payroll — and, as the Manufacturing chapter will show, they're often the honest answer when a role can't be filled at a price you can afford. Outsourcing does the same thing by moving the work to somebody else's payroll: the labour still exists, it just isn't yours. Note the Suppliers logic though — you only win if they do it more cheaply than closing the gap yourself.

What decides whether you can actually get the labour you want

Supply of labour to your business isn't fixed. It responds to:

  • Pay and conditions, relative to what similar employers nearby are offering.
  • Location and travel — a bus route can matter more than a pay rise.
  • Your reputation as an employer. Word travels faster than any job advert.
  • Competition from other employers chasing the same skill, which is why one large employer opening nearby can move your entire wage bill.
  • Legal rules — minimum wage, working hours, health and safety, right to work. Straight from the Regulations and Tax chapters.
  • Demographics and the economy. An ageing town, a shrinking region, or a recession that suddenly fills your inbox with applications.

Worked example: Priya's workforce

Priya's bakery, from earlier in the book, needs about 100 loaves a day, plus a market stall on Saturdays and a Christmas order surge. Here's her actual labour mix.

Role Type Direct or indirect Where they came from
Priya Owner-manager Both —
Dario, head baker Permanent full-time Direct Hired from the café next door
Mel, baker Permanent full-time Direct Priya's apprentice two years ago
Two Saturday staff Permanent part-time Direct Local college students, poster in the window
Sickness cover Agency temp Direct Agency, as needed
Bookkeeper Freelance Indirect Referred by her accountant
Van driver, three mornings Contractor Indirect A retired driver wanting part-time work

Now watch what the flexible layer does to her cost structure:

Month Permanent wage bill Flexible layer Total
January (quiet) £4,400 £0 £4,400
Ordinary month £4,400 £400 £4,800
December (peak) £4,400 £1,400 £5,800

The £4,400 is the price of consistency — Dario and Mel know the recipes, the customers know them, and the bread tastes the same every week. The flexible layer is the price of matching demand: in December it swells, in January it vanishes. Priya is running the Costs chapter's fixed-versus-variable idea with real people, and she's sourced her labour from four different directions — the local pool, her own training pipeline, an agency, and a referral — because no single source could supply all of it.

That's what "sources of labour" means in practice. Not one recruitment channel, but a portfolio, deliberately chosen.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Never say "we need more staff." Say what kind, on what contract, from what source. Those three answers determine your cost, your flexibility and your risk.
  • Match contract type to demand certainty. Permanent where demand is steady, flexible where it isn't. Flexibility is insurance, and insurance always has a premium.
  • Treat training as a source, not a cost. Converting entry-level people into skilled ones is often cheaper than bidding for scarce talent — it just takes patience and planning.
  • Cast a wider net than the obvious. Overlooked pools often need an adjusted shift pattern, not a bigger budget.
  • Know your labour market like you know your customers' market. Local pay rates, rival employers, demographic trends and visa rules all move the wage you'll pay for the same skill.
  • Remember the loop. Forecasting your labour needs is planning, setting up rotas and contracts is organizing, keeping good people is leading, and checking turnover, output per hour and wage cost per unit is controlling.

Summary

  • Labour sorts by skill (entry-level to specialist), and scarce skills carry more bargaining power and higher wages.
  • It also sorts by employment type — permanent, part-time, fixed-term, casual, agency, freelance, apprentice — which runs along a ladder from commitment and consistency to flexibility and variable cost.
  • Direct labour touches the product and usually varies with output; indirect labour supports the business and behaves more like overhead.
  • Labour also varies by when it's needed (seasonal, cyclical, shift-based) and where it's done (on-site, remote, offshore).
  • External sources include the local pool, the education pipeline, other employers, referrals, agencies, migration, and the pools people overlook.
  • Internal sources — promotion, retraining, apprenticeships — are often cheaper and build loyalty.
  • Automation and outsourcing are sources of labour without a payroll: the work still gets done, just differently.
  • What you can actually get depends on pay, conditions, location, your reputation, rival employers, regulation and demographics — all of which move.
  • The strategist's habit: choose the type and the source deliberately, then watch turnover, productivity and wage cost per unit like any other control measure.
