01-2 The Core Jobs Every Manager Does

Part of 01 Foundations of Business Management

Every manager, from the shift supervisor at a fast-food counter to the CEO of a global airline, is doing some blend of the same four jobs. The titles change, the scale changes, the amount of paperwork changes — but the underlying work doesn't. Those four jobs are planning, organizing, leading, and controlling. Together they're often called the management cycle, and once you can spot them in the wild, you'll start seeing them everywhere.

The four jobs at a glance

Think of them not as a to-do list you finish, but as a loop you keep running.


Each job answers a different question:

Job The question it answers Everyday example
Planning Where are we going? "We'll sell 2,000 loaves this month."
Organizing Who does what, with what? Assigning two bakers to mornings, buying a second oven
Leading Why should anyone care? Coaching a tired baker through a rough week
Controlling Are we actually on track? Checking Friday's sales against the target

Planning: deciding what "good" looks like

Planning is the job of turning a vague wish into a target someone could actually hit. A vague wish sounds like "we should sell more bread." A plan sounds like "we'll sell 2,000 loaves in March, mostly through the new weekend market stall."

Good plans do three things. They set a goal (what success looks like), they set a path (the steps to get there), and they set a measure (how you'll know if it's working). Without the measure, nobody can tell success from luck.

Planning happens at every size. A manager might plan tomorrow's staffing, next quarter's budget, or the company's direction for the next five years. The horizon changes; the thinking is the same.

Organizing: putting people and things in the right places

Organizing is arranging resources so the plan can actually happen. That means people (who does which task), materials (what gets bought and when), money (what the budget allows), and structure (who reports to whom, and who decides what).

A useful test: if two people each think the other one is ordering the flour, that's an organizing failure, not a personality clash. Organizing is where a manager creates clarity — clear roles, clear responsibilities, clear lines of authority. Boring to talk about, but it's the difference between a team that flows and a team that collides.

Leading: getting humans to actually want to do it

You can have a perfect plan and a perfect org chart and still get nothing done, because plans don't bake bread — people do. Leading is the human job: motivating, communicating, coaching, setting the tone, and resolving the small frictions that slow everyone down.

Leading looks like explaining why a change is happening rather than just announcing it. It looks like noticing that someone is struggling before they quit. It looks like standing behind a decision when it gets criticized. This is the job managers most often underrate, because it leaves no paper trail — and the job that employees notice most when it's missing.

Controlling: checking, comparing, correcting

Controlling has nothing to do with being controlling in the personality sense. In management, it means measuring performance and correcting course. You compare what's actually happening against what you planned, and then you act on the gap.

There are three possible outcomes of a check:

  • Ahead of plan — why? Can we do more of it?
  • On plan — keep going, keep watching.
  • Behind plan — what needs to change: the plan, the resources, or the effort?

Notice that last section of the loop: controlling sends you back to planning. That's the whole point of the cycle. Management isn't a straight line from idea to result; it's a spiral that keeps adjusting.

A worked example: Priya's bakery

Priya runs a small bakery with eight staff. She's never read a management textbook, but watch her work a week:

  1. Planning. She decides the goal for next month: 2,000 loaves sold, and a new weekend market stall opened. She picks the dates.
  2. Organizing. She moves two bakers to the early shift, hires a part-timer for Saturdays, and books the stall. She tells everyone who owns what.
  3. Leading. One baker is grumpy about the shift change. Priya sits down with him, hears him out, and swaps his Saturday so he can keep coaching his kid's football team. He's back on board.
  4. Controlling. Friday's numbers come in at 1,600 loaves for the month so far — behind pace. She digs in: the market stall sold less than expected because it opened too late. She adjusts the opening time and re-forecasts the month.

That's a full lap of the cycle in one week. Next week, she starts again.

They're not a checklist

New managers often assume the four jobs happen in order: plan in January, organize in February, lead in March, check in April. Real life doesn't cooperate. You'll plan on Monday morning, discover a staffing gap at lunch, coach someone at 3 p.m., and revise the plan by 5 p.m.

What also shifts is the mix. Where you sit in the hierarchy changes how much of each job you do:

Level Planning Organizing Leading Controlling
First-line (team lead) Short-term, daily Small, task-level Heavy — most of the day Constant, immediate
Middle (department head) Months ahead Whole teams Regular, less constant Weekly or monthly
Top (CEO, director) Years ahead Whole company Symbolic and cultural Big-picture metrics

A team lead spends most of her time leading and controlling, because the plan and the structure already exist. A CEO spends most of his time planning and organizing, because he's setting the frame everyone else works inside.

Summary

  • Managers everywhere do the same four jobs: planning, organizing, leading, controlling.
  • Planning sets the goal, the path, and the measure of success.
  • Organizing arranges people, materials, money, and authority so the plan can happen.
  • Leading is the human work of motivating, communicating, and coaching.
  • Controlling measures actual results against the plan and corrects course — which loops straight back into planning.
  • The four jobs overlap and repeat continuously; they're a cycle, not a checklist.
  • The mix of the four shifts with your level: front-line managers lean on leading and controlling, senior managers lean on planning and organizing.
