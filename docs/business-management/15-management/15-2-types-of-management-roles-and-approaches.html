15-2 Types of Management Roles and Approaches

Part of 15 Management

A small bakery with six staff and a car plant with six thousand both employ "managers", and at first glance the words have nothing in common. But look closer and two separate questions are hiding inside the job title. What role does this person occupy — where do they sit in the structure, and what are they accountable for? And what approach do they take — how do they go about getting things done through other people?

Those two questions are independent, and muddling them causes real damage. You can be a front-line shift supervisor who delegates brilliantly, or a chief executive who micromanages every invoice. You can be a middle manager in a rigid bureaucracy or a first-line team lead running a loose, trusting crew.

You already know the four jobs every manager does — planning, organizing, leading, controlling. This subsection is about the shapes that work takes: the roles it's performed in, and the styles it's performed with.

Roles: where a manager sits

By level

The most familiar cut is height in the hierarchy. The four jobs don't change as you climb, but their mix does.

Level Typical titles Spends most time on Horizon Judged on
First-line Team leader, supervisor, shift manager Leading and controlling — today's work Today to this week Output, quality, absenteeism
Middle Department head, plant manager, regional manager Turning plans into teams and budgets This quarter to next year Team performance, cost against budget
Top CEO, managing director, director Planning and organizing the whole business Years Survival, growth, profit

Two things shift as you climb. First, distance from the work. A team leader can see the bread coming out of the oven; a CEO sees a monthly report. That filter is why the controlling job — checking reality against the plan — gets harder, not easier, the higher you go.

Second, you increasingly manage managers rather than work. A first-line lead is close enough to do the job themselves. A top manager mostly manages other people's judgement — which is a completely different skill, and the one most often missing when a talented specialist gets promoted.

Levels also cost money. Every layer added is salary, employer taxes, office space and meetings — classic fixed costs from the Costs chapter, paid whether or not sales arrive. Which is why span of control matters: how many people report to one manager. Narrow spans mean many layers and heavy overhead; wide spans mean a flatter, cheaper structure but thinner support for each person. There's no magic number. Routine work tolerates a wide span; work needing coaching and judgement does not.

Here's that arithmetic in the open. Suppose a growing firm hires an operations manager on £45,000, plus 20% in employer costs — call it £54,000 a year. If the business keeps 20p of contribution from each pound of sales, that manager must generate £270,000 of extra sales — roughly a 10% lift on a £2.7m business — just to pay for themselves, before they've earned anyone a penny. That's not an argument against hiring managers. It's the reminder that management, like everything else, has to earn its keep.

Line and staff roles

A second cut is by what the role is accountable for.

Line managers sit directly above the work that makes and sells the product — production, sales, service delivery, distribution. They own results, and they're the ones whose necks are on the line when the numbers miss.

Staff managers advise and support: finance, HR, legal, IT, quality. They don't make the product, but they set the standards and keep the systems running.

The friction between the two is one of the oldest stories in management. Line managers want speed; staff managers want compliance. Line managers are measured on output; staff are measured on standards being met. This is exactly the core and support parts split from the Parts of a Company section, and it's where the seams live — the handoffs where a company drops the baton. Note too that staff roles usually carry functional authority: a finance director can refuse a payment, and a safety officer can stop a production line. Advice with teeth is a strange and occasionally combustible thing.

Generalist, specialist, and project roles

Small businesses need generalists — people who can do a bit of everything, because there isn't enough volume for a specialist. (This is the same make-or-buy logic as the Suppliers chapter: outsource the accounting until doing it in-house is cheaper than buying it in.) Bigger firms buy specialists, and pay for the depth.

Then there's the project or matrix role — a manager appointed to coordinate across functions: a new product launch, a new site, a system migration. In a matrix structure, people report to two managers at once, a functional one and a project one. It's the organisation admitting that its own seams need a human bridge, and it works — at the cost of people genuinely not knowing who decides.

Approaches: how a manager goes about it

The spectrum from telling to trusting

Every management approach sits somewhere on one line: how much of the deciding the manager keeps, and how much they hand over.


At the left sits the autocratic style: decide, announce, move on. Fast, clear, and sometimes exactly right. At the right sits laissez-faire: set the frame and let the team decide. Slow to build trust in, cheap to run, and utterly dependent on having good people.

Note the word "laissez-faire" doesn't mean absent. It means the manager has deliberately handed the decision to the people closest to the problem — which is a decision in itself.

The main approaches

Approach What the manager does Works when Breaks when
Autocratic / directive Decides alone, instructs A crisis, safety-critical work, inexperienced staff Skilled people doing creative work
Paternalistic Decides, but looks after the team like family Small, long-serving, loyal teams Staff want a say, or poor performance hides behind warmth
Democratic / participative Consults before deciding Experienced staff, complex problems, buy-in needed There's genuine urgency or confidentiality
Laissez-faire / delegative Sets goals, hands over the decision Expert, self-motivated professionals Goals are vague or people are new
Bureaucratic Rules, procedures, defined process Regulated industries, safety, consistency at scale Fast-moving markets and very small firms
Transactional Targets, rewards, penalties Routine, measurable work such as sales Work needing initiative and judgement
Transformational Vision, inspiration, developing people Growth, change, turnaround Routine delivery often goes unwatched
Servant / coaching Removes obstacles, develops the team Skilled professionals, service businesses Direction is needed fast

Two slivers of history are worth thirty seconds, because these ideas aren't fashions. Scientific management — measure the task, standardise it — gave mass production its economics and its human costs. Fayol's functions are the direct ancestor of the plan–organize–lead–control cycle you met earlier, so the four jobs are over a century old. Later thinkers (Mayo and the Hawthorne studies, then Drucker's management by objectives) added the human side: attention changes behaviour, and agreeing a goal while letting people find the way beats dictating every step.

One idea from that tradition is worth carrying: Theory X and Theory Y. Managers who assume people avoid work build rules, supervision and penalties — and tend to get exactly the passive workforce they expected. Managers who assume people can take pride in work give responsibility and get engagement. The assumption becomes the outcome. This connects straight to the Labour chapter: you can't own effort, only rent it, and labour is the one input that can choose, learn and leave.

No best approach — it depends

Beginners want a winner. There isn't one. The style that works is contingent on the situation:

  • The person. An experienced baker who knows the dough better than you do should be left alone. A first-week hire needs telling, not delegating. Labour is heterogenous — hours aren't interchangeable.
  • The task. Standardised, high-volume work rewards process and consistency (think the mass production end of the Manufacturing chapter). Design, sales and customer relationships reward judgement and delegation.
  • The pressure. A burst pipe needs someone to decide now. You can return to consulting on Monday — and you should say so out loud.
  • The stage of the business. A startup launching a product needs fast decisions on incomplete information. A mature operation selling a settled product needs consistency, process and cost control — the Product life cycle chapter, applied to how you run the team.
  • The strategy. A cost-led, high-volume competitor lives on tight process control; a differentiated one lives on relationships and judgement. Your market position, from the Competitors chapter, quietly sets your management style.

Putting it together: Priya's bakery

Alone. Priya is owner, worker and manager of no one. She still does all four jobs — planning the bake, organizing the flour order, controlling the till — but there's nobody to lead. This is why solo founders exhaust themselves: a company's worth of decisions, run through one head.

Eight staff, one head baker. Priya is top and middle by default. Note what she does with decisions rather than with people: bread quality gets delegated — Dario knows more about dough than she does — while prices and credit stay strictly autocratic, because they decide whether the business survives. That's not inconsistency; it's allocating decisions, and it's the single most useful habit a growing manager can build.

Forty staff, three sites. Now there's a general manager, a production manager and a retail manager, and Priya's job becomes planning, organizing and choosing people — plus checking the numbers. The classic failure at this point isn't a lack of skill; it's dipping back into the oven. Her staff learned the four jobs. Managing is not doing, and a founder who won't stop doing undermines every manager they hired.

Two cautions

Style is a choice, not a personality. The brisk, loud manager and the quiet, methodical one can both be effective — the test from earlier in this book hasn't changed: are the goals clear, are the resources lined up, is someone checking the results? Pick your approach per decision, not per person.

Don't copy a big company's structure. Layers, matrices and committees cost money and speed, and a small firm adopting them gets the cost without the scale. And beware the classic promotion trap: make your best baker a manager without changing what you measure them on, and you lose a great baker and gain a mediocre manager.

Summary

  • A management job answers two independent questions: what role (where you sit) and what approach (how you operate).
  • Roles sort by level — first-line, middle, top. The four jobs don't change; the mix does, along with your distance from the work and the horizon you plan over.
  • Line roles own results; staff roles set standards and advise. Their natural friction is the seam problem from the Parts of a Company section.
  • Roles also split into generalist (a small firm's necessity) and specialist (a big firm's purchase), plus project and matrix roles created to bridge the seams.
  • Management approaches sit on a spectrum from telling to delegating — autocratic, paternalistic, democratic, laissez-faire, bureaucratic, transactional, transformational, servant.
  • Managers get the workforce their assumptions deserve. Assume people avoid work and you build rules; assume they can take pride in it and you get engagement — the same lesson the Labour chapter makes about effort you can rent but never own.
  • There is no best approach. It depends on the person, the task, the urgency, the business's stage and its competitive strategy.
  • The strategist's habit: allocate decisions deliberately, keep the layer count and span of control honest against the £54,000-a-layer arithmetic, and treat management as a cost that must earn its keep like any other.
