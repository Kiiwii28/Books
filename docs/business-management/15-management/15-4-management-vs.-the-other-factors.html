15-4 Management vs. The Other Factors

Part of 15 Management

Every chapter in this book ends with the same kind of section: pick a factor, then walk it around the room and introduce it to all the others. Suppliers versus labour. Stores versus distribution. Tax versus the economy. This section does the same thing for management — but with a twist you need to notice, because it changes how you read everything else.

Management is the only factor that decides anything

Look at that list of twenty factors. Suppliers deliver things. Raw materials sit there. Markets set prices whether you like it or not. Tax takes its cut on a schedule. The economy does whatever it does. Rules get made by people you'll never meet.

Not one of them has an opinion. Not one of them chooses.

Management is the odd one out because it's the only factor that is also an actor. It's the layer where information turns into decisions, decisions turn into instructions, and instructions turn into results. That's why "management vs. the other factors" isn't really a contest between equals — it's closer to the relationship between a hand and a tool. The tool matters enormously. But the hand is what swings it.

This matters for you as a strategist because it's easy to get the causality backwards. When a business fails, the post-mortem often blames the market, the economy, the competition, or the cost of materials. Sometimes that's fair. But far more often, those factors were visible beforehand, and someone — sitting in the management layer — either saw them coming and didn't act, or acted too late, or acted in the wrong direction. The factors set the conditions. Management sets the response.

Think of management as a multiplier, not a competitor

Here's a useful mental model. Imagine you could score every factor in your business from 0 to 10: your product, your market, your staff, your costs, your location. Now imagine management as a multiplier applied to the total.

Great management multiplies. Mediocre management leaves the score roughly where it was. Poor management divides. And absent management — nobody actually deciding — multiplies by zero, no matter how good the underlying factors are.

Picture two coffee shops on the same street. Same foot traffic, same landlord, same bean supplier, same espresso machine, same rent, roughly the same opening hours. One owner arrives at 6am, watches which pastries sell out by 10am and which get binned at close, retrains the new barista on the milk texture, and renegotiates the milk contract after noticing the invoices crept up. The other owner checks the bank balance once a month and wonders why the takings are flat.

Six months later, one is hiring and one is advertising the lease. Nothing in the market, the tax code, or the supply chain changed. Only the multiplier did. That is what management looks like when it's isolated from everything else — and it's why the Foundations chapter argued that good management makes or breaks a business.

One caveat, though, and it's important. Multipliers don't beat zero. If your product is illegal, or your market genuinely doesn't exist, brilliant management just takes you to a well-organised failure. Good managers know when the real constraint sits somewhere else in the list — a supplier who can't deliver, a regulation that shuts the door, a cost structure that can't work at any volume. The skill isn't "manage harder." It's diagnosing which factor is actually binding this month.

With that in place, let's walk the room.

Management and the people factors: staff and labour

Staff and labour are capacity. Management is direction. A team with no management isn't a team — it's a group of people making independent, sometimes contradictory, decisions. A manager with no team is just a plan in a notebook.

The relationship is also circular, which is what makes it tricky. Management decides who gets hired, what they're trained on, what they're measured against, and whether they stay — so management creates the quality of the staff factor. But staff quality then constrains what management can attempt. You cannot execute a premium service strategy with a team you've underpaid and undertrained for three years. The Staff and Labour chapters both make this point from their own side; from management's side, the lesson is that your people plan and your business plan are the same document.

And here's the factor that appears twice. "Management" is on the list as a factor — meaning management itself has to be managed. Someone must set the manager's targets, review their decisions, and hold them accountable. In a small business, that someone is often the owner, which means the owner has to manage themselves — the hardest version of the job. In a larger company, it's the board, and the machinery is governance, succession planning, and performance review. A business with excellent staff, excellent product, and an unmanaged management layer is a car with a great engine and no driver.

Management and the company itself

The Company chapter describes the container: legal structure, ownership, divisions, culture, the formal shape of the thing. Management is what makes the container do work.

Every structural question is a management question. Do we incorporate as a limited company or stay a sole trader? Do we open a second location, or franchise? Do we split into product divisions or keep one team? These decisions shape what's possible later — they're the ones that are expensive to reverse, which is exactly why they belong to the management layer rather than to any single department.

Culture is the clearest example. Nobody writes a policy that says "we shout at each other in meetings." It emerges from what managers tolerate, reward, and ignore, day after day. Culture is management's exhaust fumes. You can smell it everywhere in the Company chapter, but it's produced in the Management chapter.

Management and the market side: market, competitors, sales, customer service

The Market chapter is blunt about this: supply and demand set prices, and you don't get a vote. Management can't move the market. What it can do is choose which market to be in, and that's an enormous lever. Deciding to serve small businesses instead of consumers, or to sell in Germany instead of France, is a management decision that changes your market, your competitors, your costs, your regulations, and your tax bill all at once.

Against competitors, management is the strategist. The Competitors chapter teaches you to size up your rivals — their prices, their capacity, their weaknesses. But sizing them up is useless without someone deciding what to do about it: match, undercut, differentiate, or walk away. That decision is management's alone.

Sales and customer service are where management's intentions become real or fall apart. Sales targets, pricing authority, discount limits, commission structures — all management-designed. Give a sales team unlimited discount authority and no oversight, and you'll get revenue that costs more than it earns. That's not a sales failure; it's a management failure showing up in the Sales chapter's numbers.

Customer service is the same story with a softer edge. Management sets the standard: how fast you answer, what you'll refund without asking, how much discretion a front-line person has. A company with a beautiful product and grudging service has a management team that decided service wasn't worth the cost. They may have decided it unconsciously — which is worse.

Management and the make side: product, suppliers, raw materials, manufacturing, distribution, stores

This whole chain — from raw material to shelf to doorstep — is a series of choices that only management is positioned to make, because each choice constrains the next.

  • Product: management decides what to build, for whom, at what price, and when to kill it as the life cycle turns.
  • Suppliers: management chooses them, negotiates with them, and decides whether to single-source for lower cost or dual-source for resilience. The Suppliers chapter's whole "choosing and managing" section is a management process.
  • Raw materials: whether to buy commodity inputs or premium ones, whether to hedge, whether to stockpile — management calls, with cash-flow consequences that land in Financials.
  • Manufacturing: make or buy, automate or hire, batch or flow. These are the decisions with the longest payback periods in the business, so they need the longest view — which only management has.
  • Distribution: channel design is one of the highest-leverage decisions available, and it's almost pure management. Sell through dealers and you give away margin but gain reach. Sell direct and you keep margin but take on warehouses, returns, and support.
  • Stores: location, format, opening hours, staffing levels, what goes by the till. The Store chapter's factors are all set by management before a customer ever walks in.

Management and the money factors: financials, revenue, costs, tax

The Financials chapter calls the statements a scoreboard, and that's exactly right. Financials tell you what happened. Management decides what happens next. A business that produces immaculate accounts and makes no decisions from them has an expensive filing habit, not a finance function.

Revenue deserves a special note: management rarely creates revenue directly. It creates the conditions. Revenue is generated by sales, by stores, by products, by customers. Management's job is to point those things in the right direction and then get out of the way — or intervene when they drift.

Costs are management's most direct lever. Almost every cost in a business is somebody's decision, and those decisions roll up to management: what you buy, who you hire, what you rent, how much you spend on marketing. Cutting costs badly is one of the classic management errors — trimming the training budget and wondering why service degrades six months later. Trimming well means cutting the activities that don't pay for themselves and protecting the ones that do.

Tax is where management's relationship with the rules gets interesting. Management doesn't set tax rates. It does decide legal structure, timing of income and expenses, where to operate, and how aggressively to claim relief. Note the word legal. Managing tax is planning within the rules; stepping outside them turns a financial decision into a legal one, and eventually a Regulations decision — the kind with a courtroom attached.

Management and the world: economy and regulations

These two are the factors management has the least control over and therefore the most need to read. You can't stop a recession. You can decide to enter it with cash reserves, low fixed costs, and a product people buy in hard times. That decision gets made before the recession, which is the whole point.

Regulations work the same way. Management doesn't write them. But management decides whether compliance is a grudging cost or a competitive advantage — a moat, because rules raise the bar for everyone, and the competitor who can't clear it is out of the running. Product safety, data protection, licensing, employment law: each one is a management planning task with cost, revenue, and risk implications that ripple through every other chapter.

Worked example: one management decision, seven factors changed

Suppose you run a small bicycle maker. You sell through forty independent dealers. One Tuesday, management decides to start selling direct to customers online.

Factor Before After
Product Three models, one spec each Same models, plus bundles and spare parts
Distribution Pallets to 40 dealers Parcels to 40,000 front doors
Stores None (dealers' showrooms) One warehouse, plus a returns desk
Sales Dealer reps and trade shows Website, ads, email list
Customer service The dealer's problem Now yours, seven days a week
Costs Simple, wholesale margin given away Warehousing, shipping, returns, ad spend
Financials Predictable cash from bulk orders Cash tied up in stock; marketing paid upfront

Not one of those changes was forced by the market, a supplier, the tax office, or a regulator. The market hadn't moved. The suppliers hadn't moved. One decision, made in the management layer, rearranged seven other factors — and probably the staff plan, the labour requirement, and the tax position too.

That's the asymmetry. Management acts on the others. The others mostly react back. A shrinking market, a poached key employee, a supplier who goes bust, a sudden tariff — all of these arrive at the management layer as a demand to adapt. So it's a loop, not a dictatorship. Management steers; the factors push back; good management notices the push early.

A quick diagnostic you can actually use

For each factor, ask a single question: who decides?

  • Who decides our prices? If the answer is "nobody, we match the competitor," that's a management gap.
  • Who decides which suppliers we keep? If it's "whoever placed the last order," that's a gap.
  • Who decides when to cut a product? If the answer is "it just faded out," that's a gap.
  • Who decides how much discount a salesperson can give? If it's "they decide," that's a gap.
  • Who decides what we do if the economy turns? If there's no answer, you'll find out later, expensively.

Every "nobody" is a factor drifting without a hand on it. That's usually where the margin is quietly leaking.

Summary

  • Management is the only factor on the list that is also an actor. Every other factor is a resource or a condition; management is the layer that decides.
  • Treat management as a multiplier on all the other factors — but remember it can't beat a zero. Diagnose which factor is genuinely binding before you manage harder.
  • Against people (staff, labour), management supplies direction to their capacity — and management itself has to be managed, by an owner, a board, or a governance process.
  • Against the company, management is what turns a legal container into a working organisation, culture included.
  • Against the market side (market, competitors, sales, customer service), management chooses the battlefield, sets the targets, and defines the standard.
  • Against the make side (product, suppliers, raw materials, manufacturing, distribution, stores), management makes the chained decisions where each choice constrains the next.
  • Against the money side (financials, revenue, costs, tax), management reads the scoreboard, creates the conditions for revenue, and owns almost every cost decision.
  • Against the world (economy, regulations), management can't control — only prepare, position, and turn compliance into advantage.
  • Trace any earlier "vs. the other factors" section back far enough and it lands here: somebody, somewhere, decided. Being that somebody, deliberately and early, is the difference between running a business and being run by one.
