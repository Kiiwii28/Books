15-3 Management as a Factor Across the Whole Business

Part of 15 Management

Management doesn't show up anywhere on a balance sheet. There's no "management" line in your costs, no supplier who sells it by the pallet, no stocktake that counts it, no machine you can buy that does it for you. That's exactly why it gets ignored — and exactly why it decides what every other factor in this book is worth.

Here's the claim of this subsection: every other factor you've studied is used, and management is the using. Materials, suppliers, labour, machines, stock, channels, cash — each one has a potential, and management determines how much of that potential you actually capture. Same bakery, same oven, same staff, same street. Different management, different business.

You can't buy it, so nobody budgets for it

Every other factor in this book arrives with an invoice. Flour has a price. So do hours, machines, shelf space and vans. You can buy more of any of them.

Management is the odd one out. You can hire managers — that's a cost, and it belongs in the Labour chapter like any other wage. But you cannot buy the decisions being good. There's no supplier who'll sell you a crate of sound judgement.

That has an awkward consequence. The single factor that multiplies everything else is the one factor that never appears in a budget review, because nothing was ever purchased. So it becomes the thing everyone assumes is happening — right up until the moment it clearly isn't.

The reframe worth carrying: when you look at any other factor, don't ask what do we have? Ask how much of it are we actually using well? A trained baker scheduled onto the shift where she's least needed is wasted potential. A machine idle for half the day is capital going nowhere. Materials in a back room nobody reorders in time are cash quietly spoiling. The resource is only half the story; the management is the other half.

Management is the connective tissue

Look back at the Parts of a Company section. Companies rarely fail inside a department. They fail at the seams — sales promising a date operations can't hit, finance discovering in March what everyone else knew in January, stock running out while the buying list sits unread.

There is no department that owns a seam. A seam is the space between two boxes on the org chart, and the only thing that can run across all of them is management. That's the sense in which management is a factor across the whole business rather than a function inside it.


Here's what that looks like factor by factor — the decisions management actually makes, and what the absence of management looks like in the same place:

Factor What management decides there What weak management looks like
Raw materials and suppliers Which inputs, how much buffer, who to depend on Stockouts on Monday, dead stock by December
Labour and staff How many, on what contracts, trained how Churn, overtime, burnout, knowledge walking out
Manufacturing Batch size, capacity, make-or-buy Expensive machines on the wrong product
Stores and distribution How much stock, where, at what speed and cost Cash frozen on shelves, deliveries that miss
Sales and service Targets, incentives, service standard Discounting to hit a number nobody checked
Financials Budgets, cash, credit terms, reporting A profitable year that ends in a cash crisis
Market and competitors Which fights to enter, how to win them Competing everywhere, winning nowhere
Economy, tax, regulation Forecasting, planning, compliance Fines, shocks, and "we didn't see it coming"

Read the middle column downwards and notice something. Not one of those decisions is about doing the work. They're all about pointing the resources at a goal — which is the definition of management from the very start of this chapter, showing up eight different ways.

Attention is the scarcest resource of all

You can buy more flour. You can hire more people, lease another van, rent a bigger store. There is exactly one resource in the business you cannot buy more of: your own attention in a given week.

That makes management, at heart, an allocation problem. Where you point your attention is your strategy, whether you meant it that way or not. An owner who spends forty hours a week on the shop floor and none on the cash position has, in practice, adopted the strategy "be magnificent at service and hope the money works out."

The controlling job from earlier in this chapter is really a statement about attention. The few numbers you check on a rhythm are an announcement, to yourself and your team, of what you've decided matters. If you review sales daily and cash quarterly, you've told everyone which one is optional.

A practical rule: spend attention where a small change produces a large result. Usually that's the biggest cost line, the tightest constraint, or the thing most likely to break — and only rarely is it the thing that's most pleasant to do.

One decision, ten consequences

Management decisions are never single decisions. They arrive as bundles, and the bundle is what a good manager sees before committing.

Priya decides to supply three local cafés with wholesale bread. Set aside the arithmetic for a moment and just watch where it goes:

Where it lands What changes
Suppliers More flour, so a renegotiated price and probably a contract
Labour An earlier start, an extra shift, training on a different standard
Manufacturing Bigger batches — and a hard look at whether the oven can cope
Stores A raw materials buffer, plus packaging she didn't need before
Distribution A 6am van route and a delivery window that must not slip
Sales A new type of sale — negotiated, recurring, with its own cost per sale
Customer service The café is now a customer, and if the bread is late, their customers suffer
Financials Thirty-day payment terms — she's lending the cafés a month of cash
Competitors She's now up against industrial bakers on price and reliability
Market A different market, where crust and atmosphere don't sell and unit price does

Ten factors, one decision. And notice the shape of the risk: each line is manageable on its own, but a manager who sees only the first one — more sales, good — has committed to nine consequences she hasn't thought about. That is precisely the difference between a management decision and an enthusiastic one.

This is also why the ripple runs the other way. Her financials constrain how much credit she can extend. Her oven capacity caps how much she can promise. Her supplier terms decide whether the margin works at all. A decision that ignores its own constraints isn't a plan; it's a wish with a delivery date.

The one factor you can't outsource

You can hand manufacturing to a contract factory, distribution to a courier, the books to an accountant, and IT to somebody who actually enjoys it. Outsourcing is a legitimate strategy, and the Suppliers chapter explained exactly when it pays.

What you cannot outsource is deciding what the business is for, and what "good" means. You can hire managers — that's the types of management roles you just met. But accountability doesn't transfer with the job title. Somebody still has to decide, and somebody still carries the consequences.

That's why management is the strategic factor and operations is the operational one. Operations executes a plan; management chooses which plan is worth executing.

Management debt compounds

Here's a framing worth borrowing. Every decision you don't make is still made — by default. Prices drift. Rotas drift. Quality drifts. The waste figure nobody counted drifts upward, quietly, every week.

Call that management debt. Like any debt, it accumulates, and it compounds. Deferred maintenance becomes a broken oven at the worst possible moment. An unaddressed staffing problem becomes a resignation letter. A customer complaint nobody logged becomes a review that costs you a year of custom.

The unpleasant part is the invisibility. A bank loan at least tells you what you owe. Management debt sits there silently until it's called in — usually in the same week as something else going wrong, which is exactly when you have least room to deal with it. The good news is that it's the cheapest debt in business to repay early. Most of it is a conversation, a decision, or an afternoon with a clipboard.

Reading the outside world is management too

Management doesn't only run the factors inside the building. It's also how the business reads the ones outside it. The Economy chapter's shifts, the Tax rules, the Regulations, the moves your Competitors make, the drift in your Market — none of those are controllable, and all of them are manageable in the sense that matters: seeing them early and responding deliberately.

Remember the honest caveat from earlier in this chapter. Luck and market conditions are real, and management can't stop a rent rise or a pandemic. What it governs is how fast you see it coming and how well you answer. Two businesses hit by the same bad news have two very different outcomes, and that gap is management.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Judge yourself by yields, not by activity. For each factor, ask what you got out of what you had. Revenue per labour hour, contribution per square metre, stockturn, cost per sale. Activity is the input; yield is the result.
  • Spend attention where it moves the most. Attention is the one input you can't buy more of, and where you point it is your real strategy.
  • Look at the seams, not just the functions. Most of the value and nearly all the chaos live in the handoffs between departments.
  • Expect ripple, and plan the bundle. Before you commit, ask which five other factors this decision touches. Then check the constraints on each.
  • Protect what you can't outsource. You can delegate the doing. You can't delegate deciding what the business is for.
  • Repay management debt early. A small decision today beats a large crisis in March.
  • Run the loop on a rhythm. Plan, organise, lead, control — on every factor, every week, not just when something breaks. The loop from the opening of this book doesn't have an end; that's the point of it.

Summary

  • Management isn't a line item or a department — it's the factor that decides how much value you get out of every other factor.
  • You can buy every other input. You can't buy management, which is why it's the one nobody budgets for and the one that multiplies the rest.
  • It's the connective tissue of the business: departments fail at their seams, and only management runs across all of them.
  • Attention is the scarcest resource in the business. Where you point it is your actual strategy, and the numbers you check on a rhythm announce what you've decided matters.
  • A management decision is never one decision — it's a bundle that lands on suppliers, labour, manufacturing, stores, distribution, sales, service, cash, competitors and markets at once.
  • Management is the one factor you cannot outsource. You can delegate the doing; accountability for what the business is for stays put.
  • Deferred decisions are management debt, and it compounds silently — but it's also the cheapest debt in business to repay early.
  • A healthy business runs the four jobs across all the factors, on a rhythm, rather than waiting for something to break.
