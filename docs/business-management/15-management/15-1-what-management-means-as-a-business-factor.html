15-1 What Management Means as a Business Factor

Part of 15 Management

You've already met management as an activity — setting goals and coordinating people and resources to reach them, and doing it as an endless loop of planning, organising, leading and controlling. That's the right way to understand the job. But there's a second way to look at it that matters just as much for strategy, and it's the angle this chapter takes: management as a factor.

A factor is simply an input a business has to consume in order to produce anything. Flour. Hours. A van. Money in the bank. If you picture the business as a machine that converts inputs into something a customer will pay for, then management is one of the things that goes in — something the business needs, pays for, and can genuinely run short of. And when it runs short, everything else suffers, because management is the input that decides how well all the other inputs are used.

Management among the factors of production

Economists have long bundled the deepest inputs into a short list: land (natural resources), labour (human effort), capital (money, tools, machines) and enterprise (the idea and the risk — what an owner brings). The Labour chapter walked through that list, and you'll recognise it.

Management doesn't slot into that list neatly, and economists honestly disagree about it. Some fold it into labour — a manager is a worker supplying effort like anyone else. Others treat it as the practical arm of enterprise, the day-to-day execution of the owner's gamble. Both views are defensible, and neither is going to change your Monday morning.

For a business strategist there's a more useful way to see it: management is the factor that organises the other factors. Land, labour and capital sitting in a room produce precisely nothing until somebody decides what to make, in what order, to what standard, for whom, and at what price.


Read that diagram and notice two things about the box in the middle.

It has no output of its own. A machine makes parts. A baker makes bread. Management makes conditions — clear goals, sensible rotas, a standard of quality, a decision about what to stop doing. That's why its contribution is always measured indirectly, in everything else. Nobody in the accounts ever sees a line called "management output", which makes management the hardest thing in the business to justify and the easiest thing to under-invest in.

Its whole job is to improve the others. Labour is the only input that can make other inputs better; management is the thing that points that effort somewhere useful. Every gain you've read about in this book — a cheaper supplier, a better yield, a fuller delivery van, a shorter queue — started as somebody's decision.

The factor you can't buy by the kilo

Look at how you buy everything else:

  • Flour, by the tonne.
  • Labour, by the hour.
  • Capital, as a lump sum, at an interest rate.
  • Premises, by the month.

Now try to buy management that way. You hire a manager, certainly. But what exactly have you bought? Not hours in the same sense — a manager who sits quietly for eight hours and makes one excellent decision has done more for the business than one who works twelve and makes none. There is no unit of management. You cannot order forty of them.

And here's the awkward part that catches out every growing firm: hiring a person does not install the management. You can pay a premium for someone with twenty years of experience and still get six months of decisions that don't fit, because they don't know your customers, your cost base, your suppliers, your staff, or the way you actually operate. What you've bought is potential. The management itself still has to be built, and it has to be built in the context of this business.

One more oddity, and it's a happy one. Labour is perishable — an unused Tuesday afternoon is gone forever, as the Labour chapter pointed out. Management is the opposite. A good decision keeps working long after it's made. A rota that finally fits demand, a supplier contract that locks in a price, a rule for what to do when stock runs out — these all keep paying out for months. That's why the good-management loop from the opening chapter compounds.

The factor that doesn't scale

This is the most practically useful idea in the subsection, so it's worth slowing down for.

Almost everything else in a business can be duplicated. Need more output? Buy a second oven, and you roughly double your baking capacity — the arithmetic the Manufacturing chapter worked through. Need more hours? Hire more staff. Need more shelf space? Rent the unit next door. Need more money? Borrow it, or sell a slice of the company.

You cannot buy a second copy of your best decision-maker.

And it's worse than that. Management doesn't just fail to scale — it barely scales at all. Six competent managers are not six times better than one; they're a small team with a coordination problem of its own. Management is the factor where adding more of the input produces diminishing returns fastest.

So management behaves like a capacity ceiling — the same kind of hard limit you met with the oven in Manufacturing and the hours in Labour. A founder can only hold so many open questions in their head, make so many decisions a week, and be in so many places at five in the morning. That ceiling is why businesses stall at suspiciously round sizes: the number of sites the owner can visit in a week, the number of names they can keep straight, the number of jobs they can hold in mind at once.

Growth, then, isn't only a question of demand or capital. It's a question of whether you have enough management to run what you already have. A business that wins five new contracts and has no management capacity to absorb them hasn't grown. It's just got busier and worse.

Management is a fixed cost — with a break-even

Like machines and salaried staff, management is largely a fixed cost: you pay it whether the week is busy or quiet. The fixed-versus-variable split from the Costs chapter applies here as neatly as anywhere in the book.

Which means management has a break-even point, in exactly the way the second oven did. A manager costs a certain amount each month. That cost has to be covered by the extra contribution — the money left from each additional unit of sale after its direct costs — that the freed-up management makes possible. Below a certain volume, professional management costs more than it earns, and the right answer is to keep doing it yourself. Above it, the opposite is true, and staying small becomes the expensive choice.

That reframes the whole question. Management isn't a reward you give yourself once the business is successful. It's an investment with a threshold. Under the threshold it's a cost; over it, it's the thing that unlocks growth. And most firms don't fail to spot this — they simply never do the arithmetic that tells them which side of the line they're on.

Where management comes from

If management is a factor, it needs sources — just as labour did. There are four, and each trades one thing for another.

The owner. The cheapest on paper and the one every business starts with. Its limits are hard: there's only one of them, they're already working fifty-hour weeks, and the Labour chapter's warning applies — owner labour is never actually free.

Promoting from within. You know them; they know the business. Cheapest in cash, best for morale, and the fastest way to get decisions that fit reality. Two costs are worth naming. First, you lose a productive doer — a direct worker becomes an indirect one, which is a real shift in your cost structure, not just a change of job title. Second, promotion doesn't come with a toolkit. Very few people are born knowing how to schedule a week, run an appraisal, or hold a difficult conversation. That training is part of the purchase price.

Hiring from outside. They bring the toolkit and the pattern recognition from other businesses. They don't bring knowledge of yours. Expect a settling-in period during which their decisions are worse than the person they replaced — and expect a step up in fixed cost.

Buying it in. Consultants, fractional finance directors, an accountant who acts as an unofficial advisor, a non-executive who turns up once a quarter. This is management purchased from a suppliers as an outside supplier, and the supplier test applies: what are they worth, and how easily could you walk away? Excellent for a defined problem and a fixed fee; hopeless for the day-to-day, because the day after the engagement ends, they're gone.

Notice that the first three are really a make-or-buy decision for management itself. Do you grow it, or do you shop for it? Most healthy firms do both, deliberately.

Worked example: Brightline Cleaning hits its ceiling

Ana runs Brightline Cleaning. Two vans, fourteen part-time cleaners, twelve small commercial contracts — offices and a couple of medical practices. She has no supervisor. She does everything.

One contract looks like this, per month:

Per contract, per month
Revenue £1,200
Cleaner wages £750
Consumables £50
Contribution £400

Twelve contracts give her £4,800 a month of contribution, which covers the vans, insurance, equipment, and her own drawings. It works. She's profitable. She's also completely full.

Ana's constraint isn't demand — three more businesses have asked her to quote. It isn't vans or cleaners either; both could be added. It's management. She can quote, schedule, cover sickness, train new starters, chase late payers and handle complaints for about twelve contracts and no more. The thirteenth would tip her, and quality would start slipping on the first twelve.

So she prices up a supervisor. Salary plus employer's taxes and pension — the fully loaded figure, not the headline number — comes to about £2,600 a month.

Break-even: £2,600 ÷ £400 = 6.5 contracts.

That's the whole calculation. A supervisor has to unlock at least seven extra contracts just to pay for herself. Below seven, Ana has made herself poorer for the sake of looking more professional. Above it — say a supervisor who frees her to run twenty more contracts — she's adding 20 × £400 = £8,000 of contribution for £2,600 of cost.

And notice the traps on both sides, because they're the same trap in different clothes:

  • Hire too early, with only two contracts in sight, and £2,600 of fixed cost buys you £800 of contribution. You've just converted a profitable small business into a struggling one.
  • Never hire, and the ceiling is permanent. Brightline stays a twelve-contract business forever, while a rival who made the jump takes the contracts Ana had to turn down.

There's a quiet third option too, and it's the one most good small firms take: promote a senior cleaner to supervisor at a smaller cost, accept that they'll need training, and buy in the finance expertise two days a month. Grow some of the management, buy the rest.

This is why management is so often the real answer to "why did that business stop growing?" It wasn't the market. It wasn't the money. It was the fact that the most important input in the business came in exactly one copy, and nobody worked out when to buy a second.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Treat management as an input you budget, not a perk you add later. It has a cost, a capacity, and a break-even — do the arithmetic like any other investment.
  • Find your ceiling before you hit it. Where does your own management capacity run out — twelve contracts, four sites, thirty staff? That number is your real growth limit, and it's more honest than any sales forecast.
  • Ask which side of the break-even you're on. Before hiring, promoting, or buying in a manager, work out how much extra contribution they must unlock to pay for themselves. Then judge whether the demand is actually there.
  • Remember the benefit is always indirect. Management shows up as better yield, fewer mistakes, faster turns and happier customers — never as a neat line of its own. That's precisely why it gets cut first in a squeeze and why that cut is usually a mistake.
  • Use the make-or-buy frame. Grow management from inside, hire it in, or buy a slice of it part-time. The right mixture changes as the business grows, and there's no shame in any of the three.
  • Connect it to everything else. Management wages move with the labour market, the fully loaded cost includes tax, hiring one creates a legal commitment, and the cash goes out monthly whatever the customers do. This factor never sits in isolation.
  • Then run the loop on the factor itself. Plan what management you'll need next year, organise who does what, lead the managers you've got, and control it by checking whether they're paying their way.

Summary

  • Management can be seen as an activity (the jobs you do) or as a factor (an input the business needs, pays for, and can run out of). This subsection is about the second view.
  • Among land, labour, capital and enterprise, management is best understood as the factor that organises the others — economists disagree about where it belongs, but its function is clear.
  • It has no output of its own. Its product is the conditions under which everything else produces, which is why its value is always measured indirectly.
  • You cannot buy it by the kilo. There's no unit of management, hiring a person doesn't install it, and it has to be built in the context of your business.
  • It barely scales — six managers aren't six times better than one — which makes management a capacity ceiling on everything else. That's why firms stall at the size one person can personally hold together.
  • It's largely a fixed cost with a break-even: a manager must unlock enough extra contribution to cover the fully loaded cost, or they make the business poorer.
  • Management comes from four sources — the owner, internal promotion, external hire, and buying it in — and choosing between them is a make-or-buy decision in its own right.
