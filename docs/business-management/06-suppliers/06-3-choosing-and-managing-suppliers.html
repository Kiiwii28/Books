06-3 Choosing and Managing Suppliers

Part of 06 Suppliers

Picking a supplier is one of those decisions that looks small and turns out to be enormous. Get it right, and your shelves stay stocked, your products hold up, and your costs behave. Get it wrong, and you'll spend the next two years apologising to customers for something a stranger did three countries away. This is the practical half of dealing with suppliers: how to choose one, and how to keep the relationship healthy once you have.

Think of it as marriage with better paperwork.

Choosing: it's not just about the lowest price

The rookie mistake is to run a beauty contest on price alone. Price is what you pay today; total cost of ownership is what you actually pay over time — the price, plus shipping, duties, storage, defects, rework, delays, and the customers you lose when a delivery doesn't arrive.

That's why the cheapest quote is so often the most expensive supplier. A supplier who is 8% cheaper but late one delivery in five will cost you more in expedited freight and angry emails than you ever saved.

Here are the criteria that genuinely matter:

Criterion The question you're really asking
Quality Can they hit the spec every single time, or just on a good day?
Price and terms What's the true landed cost, and when do I have to pay?
Reliability How often do they deliver on time, in full?
Capacity Can they handle my growth, or a sudden spike?
Flexibility Will they take a small rush order without a drama?
Financial health Will they still exist in eighteen months?
Compliance and ethics Do they meet labour, safety, and environmental rules?
Location and lead time How long between ordering and receiving?
Communication When something goes wrong, do they tell me first?

Notice how many of these are borrowed from other parts of the business. "Price and terms" is a Costs and Financials question — a supplier offering 90-day payment terms is quietly lending you working capital. "Compliance and ethics" is a Regulations issue, and a supplier caught using illegal labour becomes your reputational problem, not theirs. "Capacity and lead time" connect straight to Distribution and to how much stock you must hold. And "quality" flows into Product, which flows into Customer Service. Nothing here happens in isolation.

A process, not a hunch

Good supplier selection follows roughly the same path every time, whether you're buying industrial bolts or basil.


A few notes on the steps that people rush:

Define the need properly. If you can't describe what "good" looks like in measurable terms — 5,000 units a month, delivered by the 3rd, meeting this spec, at this tolerance — you can't evaluate anyone. Vague requirements are how you end up with a supplier who is technically compliant and practically useless.

Trial before you commit. A sample order is the cheapest due diligence available. It reveals their quality, their packaging, their paperwork, and how they behave when you're a small customer rather than a hypothetical big one.

Score, don't vibe. Give each criterion a weight and mark candidates against it. This forces you to be honest about what you actually value, and it gives you a defensible answer when someone asks why you didn't pick the cheapest bid.

One supplier or several?

A tempting shortcut is single sourcing — one supplier for everything. It's simpler to manage, and you get volume discounts and a closer relationship.

The risk is obvious once you say it out loud: if they fail, you fail. A fire, a strike, a bankruptcy, or a ship stuck in a canal, and your business stops.

Multi-sourcing — spreading the work across two or three suppliers — costs you some scale discount but buys resilience and a permanent benchmark. If you can't compare one supplier's prices to another's, you have no idea whether you're being treated well. A common middle path is the 70/30 split: one main supplier, one backup who stays warm and ready.

There's also sole sourcing, where only one supplier in the world can make what you need. That's not a choice, it's a constraint — and it means your job becomes managing risk rather than managing price: longer contracts, buffer stock, and a genuine relationship.

Managing: the part most businesses skip

Choosing a supplier is a one-off project. Managing one is a permanent habit. And like most habits, it decays the moment you stop paying attention.


Set expectations in writing. A simple agreement covering price, lead times, quality standards, minimum order quantities, payment terms, and what happens when things go wrong. This isn't about distrust — it's about making sure nobody is guessing.

Measure with a small number of numbers. On-time-in-full delivery, defect rate, and responsiveness will catch most problems. Track them in a simple supplier scorecard and share it. Suppliers improve fastest when a customer is quietly counting.

Review on a rhythm. A short quarterly conversation — here's what went well, here's what didn't, here's what we need next quarter — prevents small irritations from calcifying into a broken relationship.

Plan your exit before you need it. What would you do if this supplier vanished tomorrow? Could you switch in a week, a month, a year? Knowing the answer is what lets you sleep.

A worked example

Imagine a small bakery chain deciding on a flour supplier.

  • Supplier A quotes the lowest price but delivers on a "roughly Thursday" basis and has missed two orders this year.
  • Supplier B charges 6% more, delivers every Tuesday before 7am, and offers 45-day payment terms.
  • Supplier C is mid-priced and can supply organic and specialist blends that Supplier B can't.

The naive answer is A. The better answer is B as the main supplier, C for the specialist lines, and A kept as an emergency backup for shortfalls. The 6% premium buys reliable opening hours, fewer wasted staff mornings, and 45 days of free cash flow — which, as the Financials chapter will tell you, is worth real money to a business with thin margins and rent due every month.

Where this connects to everything else

Supplier management is a hinge between several chapters, and a good strategist notices the swing:

  • Raw Materials — the quality of what you buy sets the ceiling on the quality of what you make.
  • Costs — supplier prices are usually the largest single cost line, so this is where margin is won or lost.
  • Product — consistency from suppliers is what lets you promise consistency to customers.
  • Distribution — lead times and delivery reliability determine how much stock you must hold and how fast you can respond.
  • Regulations and Tax — duties, tariffs, and rules travel with goods across borders.
  • Economy — currency swings and inflation can turn a comfortable contract uncomfortable overnight, which is why long contracts sometimes include price-adjustment clauses.
  • Competitors — a better supplier relationship can be a genuine advantage, especially if it locks up capacity others can't get.


That loop is the whole business in one picture — and the supplier sits at the top of it. If the flow into the loop is unreliable, everything downstream, right up to the cash in your account, inherits that unreliability.

Summary

Choosing and managing suppliers comes down to a handful of durable ideas:

  • Choose on total cost of ownership, not sticker price — reliability, quality, and payment terms are all part of the bill.
  • Run a structured process: define the need, shortlist, sample, score, trial, then contract.
  • Decide deliberately between single, multi-, and sole sourcing, balancing simplicity against resilience.
  • Manage actively with clear agreements, a few tracked metrics, regular reviews, and a plan for if they disappear.
  • Remember that a supplier is not an isolated transaction — it's the first link in the chain that ends in your customer's hands and your bank balance.

Pick well, manage continuously, and your supply base becomes a quiet advantage. Pick carelessly, and it becomes the story you'll be explaining to customers for years.
