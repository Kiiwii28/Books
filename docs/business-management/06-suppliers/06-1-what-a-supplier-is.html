06-1 What a Supplier Is

Part of 06 Suppliers

Ask most business owners who their supplier is and they'll name the firm that delivers their stock. That's right, as far as it goes — but the word covers far more ground, and getting the full picture straight now makes the next two subsections ("Types of Suppliers" and "Choosing and Managing Suppliers") much easier to use.

The definition

A supplier is any organisation that provides something your business needs in order to operate.

Notice how deliberately broad that is. Not "the firm that sells us stock" — anything your business needs. That pulls in a lot of people you probably don't call suppliers:

  • the mill that sends you flour, or the wholesaler that sends you boxes,
  • the electricity company keeping the ovens on,
  • the accountant who files your returns,
  • the bank that lends you money to buy the second van,
  • the software platform that takes your card payments,
  • the agency that sends you a temp when two staff are off sick.

All of them supply something you can't operate without. All of them take money out of your business in return. If you only think of the first one, you'll miss where a surprising amount of your cost, risk and leverage actually lives.

Everything you sell came from somewhere

Here's the mental picture worth carrying. Your business sits in the middle of a chain. Things flow in from suppliers, you add value, and things flow out to customers — with money flowing the other way in both directions.


Two things follow from that picture, and both matter.

First, the supplier is the mirror image of the customer. Your customers are the people who pay you for what you make; your suppliers are the people you pay for what you need. Same market, opposite side of the counter. That's exactly the point from the Market chapter about buyers, sellers and the space between them — and it means almost everything you know about looking after customers has a supply-side twin. You want your customers to come back, to trust you, and to give you a heads-up when something's wrong. Your suppliers want exactly the same from you.

Second, nothing appears out of nowhere. The loaf on the counter is flour, water, salt, yeast, gas, packaging, a baker's time, a van, a card machine and a shop. Every one of those arrived via a supplier relationship, or several. Trace any product backwards and you'll find a chain of businesses, each one somebody else's supplier.

You are somebody's supplier too

Easy to forget: every business is on both sides of this at once. A bakery sells bread to a café — the bakery is the café's supplier. The same bakery buys flour from a mill — the mill is the bakery's supplier. The plumber who fixes the oven is a supplier; the office that hires the plumber is a customer of a different supplier.

And sometimes the same firm is both. A café that buys Priya's bread and also sells her lunch is her supplier and her customer at the same time. Big firms live in these loops constantly, and they're worth protecting — being a reliable customer to your own customers is often worth more than any discount you could argue for. (The Competitors chapter makes the darker version of this point too: a supplier who could start making your product, or a big customer who could start making it themselves, is a rival in waiting.)

Why the word "supplier" is doing more work than it seems

Three consequences fall out of the definition, and each connects to a different part of the business.

Suppliers are where most of your costs start. In a product business, the money you hand to suppliers is often the single biggest cost block — bigger than rent, bigger than wages in some trades. That's why the Costs chapter spends so long on direct versus fixed costs: a flour bill rises with every loaf you bake, while the accountant's monthly fee doesn't care how much you sold. Small improvements in what you pay suppliers land disproportionately on your profit, which is a theme we'll come back to. And where payment timing sits — paying a supplier in 30 days versus 60 — is a cash question belonging to the Financials chapter.

Suppliers are where a lot of your risk sits. A supplier who fails, strikes, doubles their price, or disappears into administration mid-order is not a mild inconvenience — it can stop your business dead. You can't manage what you can't see, and the risk often arrives from a firm you've never spoken to. Remember Priya's flour mill buys wheat from farmers she'll never meet. If the harvest is bad, the price rise arrives at her counter anyway. That's a Raw Materials problem wearing a Suppliers costume.

Suppliers are sometimes where your capability sits. Not everything you're good at has to be inside your four walls. Plenty of successful firms are essentially a small, sharp hub surrounded by suppliers who do the heavy lifting — the manufacturing, the warehousing, the accounts. Which raises the question the last subsection of this chapter will return to: should you make it yourself, or buy it in?

Worked example: Priya's day, traced backwards

Take one ordinary Tuesday at Priya's bakery and follow the money outwards.

She opens at 7am. The ovens are already hot — gas, from a utility she has never spoken to and cannot negotiate with. The flour came from a mill forty minutes away; the yeast and butter from a cash-and-carry she visits herself; the cardboard boxes from a printer two towns over. The till takes card payments through a platform that skims a percentage of every sale before the money ever reaches her account. Her accountant will file her year-end figures in three months. A courier drops off a box of labels at 10am.

Now notice something. Priya thinks of the mill as "her supplier" and thinks of the gas company, the card platform and the accountant as just facts of life. But they're all suppliers — they all provide something she needs, and they all take a cut. And the one she'd find hardest to replace isn't the mill she negotiates with every quarter. It's the payment platform, sitting invisibly on every single sale.

That's the gap between how people use the word and what it actually covers, and closing it is the first real step towards managing suppliers rather than just paying them.

The one question worth asking about any supplier

If you take a single idea away from this subsection, make it this one — because it sets up everything that follows:

How easily could I walk away?

Not "do I like them". Not "are they cheap". Could I replace them, and what would it cost me to try? If the answer is "there are six others who could do this tomorrow", that supplier competes for your business and you hold the cards. If the answer is "there is exactly one firm in the country who makes this", then they set the price, the lead time and the terms — and a piece of your margin belongs to them until you find another way.

This is the Market chapter's supply-and-demand logic, viewed from underneath. A supplier with no rivals is a monopoly you have to live with, and that's just one of the shapes we'll sort suppliers into next.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Recognition comes first. Write down everything your business actually needs to operate, and who provides it. Most owners find two or three entries on that list they'd never thought of as suppliers at all.
  • Every supplier is a cost, a risk and sometimes a capability. Treating them as purely a price to be squeezed misses two-thirds of the picture.
  • You're on both sides of the counter. The habits that make a good customer — paying on time, communicating, not vanishing when something goes wrong — are the habits that make a good buyer of supplies.
  • Know where you can walk away and where you can't. That single judgement, applied supplier by supplier, is the foundation for choosing, managing and occasionally replacing the firms your business depends on.

Summary

  • A supplier is any organisation that provides something your business needs to operate — not just the firm that delivers your stock.
  • The list stretches well beyond goods: utilities, services, banks, software platforms, staffing agencies and advisers all supply something you can't run without.
  • Your business sits in the middle of a chain — inputs flow in from suppliers, value gets added, products flow out to customers, and money flows the other way in both directions.
  • The supplier relationship is the mirror image of the customer relationship, and every business is on both sides of it at once. Firms are sometimes supplier and customer — or supplier and future rival.
  • Suppliers carry three things for you: a large share of your costs, a large share of your risk, and sometimes a chunk of your capability.
  • The question that matters most about any supplier is simple: how easily could I walk away?
