06-4 Suppliers vs. The Other Factors

Part of 06 Suppliers

Suppliers are the only factor in this entire book that lives outside your company's walls but inside your cost structure. That single sentence explains almost every relationship in this section.

Think about what you learned in the Company chapter: a company is a legal and organisational boundary — a "we" with a bank account, staff, and a set of things it chooses to do itself. Everything on the other side of that boundary has to be bought. And nearly everything you buy arrives through a supplier. Pull the supplier away and the other factors don't politely stand still: your Manufacturing line stops, your Product's quality story collapses, your Sales team starts quoting lead times it can't hit, your Financials show inventory you can't sell, and your Customer Service team spends its week apologising for someone else's failure.

So "Suppliers vs. the Other Factors" isn't a list of twenty tidy comparisons. It's a question of position. Where does the supplier sit relative to each of the others — upstream, downstream, or wrapped around them?


The dotted lines matter as much as the solid ones. Suppliers sit at the point where the outside world — market prices, competition, cash — leaks into your operating chain. Let's walk the whole map, cluster by cluster.

Suppliers vs. the Strategic Frame: Company, Competitors, Market, Product

Company

The relationship here is the make-or-buy decision, and it's one of the most consequential choices a manager ever makes. Every supplier relationship is a deliberate statement: we don't need to own this capability.

Take Bean & Brook, a small coffee roastery. It doesn't grow coffee, doesn't own farms, doesn't run container ships. It buys green beans from importers and does the one thing it has decided is its core competency: sourcing, roasting, and brand. Northern Italy's packaging-machine makers, Apple's contract assemblers, and your local bakery buying flour from a mill are all making the same trade.

The upside is enormous — you rent capability without buying it. The downside is that anything you outsource, you no longer control. If your coffee importer switches farms without telling you, your "single-origin Ethiopian" claim becomes a lie, and you find out from a customer, not a spreadsheet. The strategic rule of thumb: outsource what is not your differentiation; own what is. A company that outsources its point of difference has quietly become a reseller.

Competitors

Look back at the Competitors chapter — you learned to size up rivals on price, product, and brand. Here's the missing lens: your competitors are often bidding for the same suppliers you are.

In a shortage, supplier capacity is a zero-sum prize. When semiconductors dried up in 2021–22, carmakers didn't lose sales because demand vanished. They lost sales because a handful of fabs allocated scarce chips to whoever had signed long-term agreements — and the carmakers who had treated chips as a cheap commodity to be bought at the last minute were at the back of the queue. The bill ran into billions.

Three practical consequences:

  • Exclusivity is a competitive weapon. Locking a key supplier into a supply agreement starves rivals of the same input.
  • Supplier strength is competitive intelligence. Your supplier sells to your competitor too — they know the market's volumes, lead times, and pricing pressure better than anyone.
  • Suppliers can become competitors. A supplier who forward-integrates (the importer starts selling roasted beans under its own label) is now on both sides of your value chain.

Market

The Market chapter taught you that supply and demand set the price. Suppliers are where that theory becomes a bill you actually pay.

Your supplier operates in their market — the green coffee market, the steel market, the freight market — and that market's price becomes your cost of goods. When the coffee "C price" spikes because of a drought in Brazil, Bean & Brook's margin compresses long before its own customers notice anything. This is why the supplier relationship is a transmission belt for the market: commodity cycles, currency swings, and freight rates arrive at your door through it, usually with a lag of one contract cycle.

The strategist's move is to know which market your supplier is exposed to, and how much of that exposure you're willing to absorb.

Product

Product and supplier are handcuffed together, and the Product chapter's lesson on quality applies with brutal directness: your product's quality ceiling is set by your inputs.

  • Design is constrained by supplier capability. A bike maker that designs a frame around a carbon layup no supplier can produce has designed a fantasy.
  • Features depend on suppliers. Electric-vehicle range is largely a battery supplier's spec sheet. Phone camera quality is a sensor supplier's roadmap.
  • Co-development is a competitive advantage. The best suppliers aren't order-takers; they're engineering partners who tell you what's possible before you commit to a design. Getting them in early shortens new-product development and prevents expensive redesigns.
  • The life-cycle link is real. In the Product Life Cycle, growth phases demand volume, and volume demands supplier capacity. A product that takes off with a single-source supplier often stalls exactly when it should be scaling.

Notice the pattern across all four of these: the supplier determines what you can be, not just what you can spend.

Suppliers vs. the Value Chain: Raw Materials, Manufacturing, Distribution, Stores

Raw Materials

This is the closest, most-confused pairing in the book, so let's draw the line cleanly.

Raw materials are things. Suppliers are entities. You buy raw materials from a supplier — but not everything a supplier sells is a raw material. Suppliers sell components, sub-assemblies, finished goods, services, software licences, and labour. An IT contractor is a supplier with no raw material in sight.

The dependency runs deeper than vocabulary. Your supplier choice determines which raw materials you can even access, at what grade, and at what price. Suppliers also stack in tiers — the tier 1 supplier you invoice sits on top of tier 2 suppliers and tier 3 raw-material producers you've never met. When a fire hits a single chemical plant three tiers down, your line stops anyway. That's why "know your supply chain" means knowing past your immediate counterparties.

Manufacturing

Manufacturing is where the classic make-vs-buy question gets its sharpest edge. Every factory you don't build is a supplier doing it for you.

  • Contract manufacturing lets you scale without capital. A clothing brand can go from 500 to 50,000 units without owning a sewing machine.
  • But you inherit their limits. Their defect rate becomes your warranty cost. Their capacity becomes your growth ceiling. Their quality system becomes your brand risk.
  • Vertical integration is the reverse bet — more control, better margin potential, far more capital and rigidity. It's the same trade-off you saw in the Company chapter, expressed in machinery.

A useful test: If this supplier doubled its price tomorrow, would I be trapped or merely annoyed? Trapped means you've outsourced too deep.

Distribution

Distribution is the mirror image of supply: inbound logistics versus outbound logistics. Suppliers feed the front door, distribution moves things out the back. Some firms do both — Amazon is a supplier's customer and a distributor simultaneously.

The supplier relationship defines where your distribution responsibility begins. Under DDP terms a supplier delivers to your warehouse; under FOB you own the goods from the moment they're loaded onto a ship, and the freight, insurance, and customs work becomes yours. Suppliers who drop-ship directly to your customers blur the two factors into one — convenient, until a customer's experience depends on a partner's packaging.

Stores

Physical stores are the visible end of a supply chain that starts with a supplier — and the place where supplier failure becomes most embarrassing.

  • Lead times set reorder points. If your supplier needs eight weeks, your store must carry eight weeks of cover, which is eight weeks of cash sitting on a shelf.
  • Stockouts are usually supply-chain stories. The empty shelf in the Customer Service chapter's complaints is frequently a supplier's late shipment wearing a store's uniform.
  • Vendor-managed inventory and consignment reverse the burden: the supplier watches your shelf and replenishes it. You get less cash tied up; they get more visibility.
  • Direct-to-consumer suppliers compete with your stores. If your supplier starts selling the same goods online, your shelf space is now a showroom for their brand.

Suppliers vs. the People Factor: Labour, Staff, Management, Sales, Customer Service

Labour

Labour touches suppliers twice.

First, labour is itself a purchased input. Staffing agencies, freelancers, and contractors are suppliers in the ordinary sense — you're renting hours instead of hiring them. That's the fixed-versus-variable cost trade-off you met in Costs: outsourcing turns a salary you pay in a bad month into a cost you only pay when you need it, at a higher unit rate.

Second, your supplier's labour is your risk. A strike at a port, a shortage of welders at a fabricator, or a labour-law violation in a tier 2 factory becomes your delay and, increasingly, your legal liability. Your supplier's workforce is invisible on your org chart and entirely real on your P&L.

Staff

Staff are your internal people; suppliers are your external capacity. The bridge between them is the buyer.

A skilled procurement person is worth multiples of their salary. They negotiate terms, spot concentration risk, build relationships that get you priority in a shortage, and know when a "cheaper" supplier is actually a more expensive one. This is why supplier management sits inside the Staff and Management chapters too: relationships don't manage themselves. In small businesses, buying is often the owner's job by default — and it's one of the highest-leverage hours in the week.

There's also a knowledge angle. Good suppliers train your staff, share technical expertise, and act as an extension of your team. Bad ones teach your staff to work around problems.

Management

Suppliers are a management discipline, not a purchasing transaction.

The classic framing — the Kraljic matrix — sorts purchases into four boxes: strategic (high value, high risk — partner closely), bottleneck (low value, high risk — secure supply above all), leverage (high value, low risk — squeeze on price), and non-critical (low value, low risk — automate it). A single roastery might treat specialty green beans as strategic, replacement gaskets as bottleneck, packaging as leverage, and cleaning supplies as non-critical. Same company, four entirely different relationships.

Management also decides risk appetite: single-source for efficiency or dual-source for resilience? That's not a procurement question — it's a strategy question, and it belongs in the boardroom.

Sales

Sales promises what suppliers can deliver. The two are locked together by lead time.

  • Quote a two-week delivery and your supplier takes five weeks, and Sales becomes the department that lies to customers.
  • Sell a large order before confirming supplier capacity, and you've sold something you may not be able to make.
  • Exclusivity and territory arrangements are jointly negotiated with suppliers and shape what Sales can offer.

A healthy business gives Sales a live view of supplier lead times and stock. A fragile one lets Sales discover the constraint at the same moment the customer does.

Customer Service

Customer Service is where supplier failures are felt, and it's the factor that pays for them in reputation rather than cash.

  • Warranty and returns costs are often supplier defects in disguise. Track them to the source and you have a negotiation lever, not just a complaint queue.
  • Service recovery depends on supplier responsiveness. A customer waiting on a replacement part is waiting on your supplier's dispatch desk.
  • SLAs cascade. The commitments you make to customers must be mirrored by commitments your supplier makes to you. If your supplier has no obligation to respond in 48 hours, you cannot honestly promise one.

The suppliers who make Customer Service easy are usually the suppliers who were chosen with Customer Service in mind — which is a decision made long before any complaint arrives.

Suppliers vs. the Numbers: Financials, Revenue, Costs

Financials

Suppliers show up on your balance sheet in three places, and each is a cash decision.

  • Payables are the mirror image of the Revenue chapter's receivables. Stretching payment terms from 30 to 60 days is effectively an interest-free loan from your supplier — one of the cheapest sources of working capital a small firm has.
  • Inventory is cash you've converted into goods. Every extra week of safety stock is money that isn't funding growth.
  • Concentration risk is a footnote investors read carefully. A business where one supplier provides 70% of inputs is a business with a single point of failure baked into its valuation.

Revenue

You cannot sell what you cannot get, so supplier capacity is a revenue ceiling — and revenue growth plans that ignore it are fiction.

If demand doubles and your supplier can only deliver 30% more, your revenue grows 30% and your disappointed customers go to competitors. The strategic move is to align supplier capacity planning with sales forecasting before the growth push, not after. And watch for supplier-side revenue levers too: volume rebates and annual bonuses reduce your effective purchase price, which shows up as margin rather than revenue — but it's real money either way.

Costs

This is the most direct relationship in the whole book. Cost of goods sold is mostly supplier price.

But unit price is a trap. The useful number is total cost of ownership: price, plus freight, duties, quality failures, rework, inventory carrying cost, and the management time spent firefighting. A supplier 10% cheaper who delivers 5% defective can easily be more expensive than the incumbent. This is exactly the Cost chapter's hidden-cost lesson, applied upstream.

The profitability insight is simply this: every pound saved on a purchased input drops straight to the bottom line, while every pound of extra sales contributes only its margin. In low-margin businesses, procurement is often the fastest route to profit that exists.

Suppliers vs. the Rules of the Game: Economy, Tax, Regulations

Economy

Suppliers are the shock absorber between the macroeconomy and your P&L.

  • Inflation arrives as a supplier price increase, usually with a justification letter attached.
  • Interest rates change the cost of the inventory you're funding.
  • Currency moves change the price of anything imported, even if the supplier's own price hasn't budged.
  • Recessions do the opposite work: demand falls, suppliers' spare capacity rises, and suddenly your negotiating position improves.

The strategist's question is where your supplier is exposed — one country, one commodity, one currency, one shipping lane — because that's where the economy will hit you.

Tax

Tax quietly rewrites supplier economics.

  • Import duties make a nearby supplier competitive against a cheaper distant one.
  • VAT/GST on purchases is usually recoverable, but it's a cash-flow event between payment and reclaim.
  • Transfer pricing rules govern what you may pay an overseas supplier you also own — and getting it wrong is expensive.
  • Withholding tax on cross-border payments can push an offshore supplier to charge more to compensate.

Tax is also a reason suppliers cluster geographically: free ports, export-processing zones, and duty-drawback schemes all exist to make certain supply routes cheaper than others.

Regulations

Here the relationship turns from commercial to legal, and the stakes are the highest they get.

You increasingly inherit liability for your suppliers' behaviour. Labour-law breaches, environmental damage, sanctioned trading partners, conflict minerals, forced labour in tier 2 factories — regulators now expect you to know and to act. Supplier codes of conduct, audits, and traceability systems are the cost of doing business in regulated markets.

Regulation cuts both ways, though. A supplier holding a patented process or a licence can be a moat. A regulation that raises the bar for entry can protect your supplier relationships from new rivals. And when rules change, your supplier is often the first to tell you what's coming.

Summary

The supplier factor is the seam where the outside world enters your business, and that's why it touches all nineteen others.

  • Strategically, suppliers define what your company chooses not to own, shape your competitive position, transmit market prices, and cap what your product can ever be.
  • Operationally, they feed raw materials into manufacturing, hand off to distribution and stores, and are staffed, managed, sold through, and serviced by people.
  • Financially, they are your cost of goods sold, your payables, your inventory, and your revenue ceiling.
  • Externally, they are the channel through which the economy, tax rules, and regulation arrive at your door.

If you take one habit from this section, take this: treat every significant supplier as a strategic relationship, not a transaction. Ask what breaks if they fail, what happens if they raise prices, who else is bidding for their capacity, and whether what you outsource is something you should own. Suppliers look like a cost line. Managed well, they are one of the most reliable levers on profitability a business has.
