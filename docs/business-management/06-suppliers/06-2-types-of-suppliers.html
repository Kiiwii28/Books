06-2 Types of Suppliers

Part of 06 Suppliers

A supplier is any business that provides goods or services yours needs in order to operate. That definition is deliberately broad — and that's exactly the problem. Calling a multinational chipmaker, the bloke who mows your yard, and a wholesaler of finished cushions all "suppliers" tells you almost nothing about how each one should be handled, what risk they carry, or how much of your money they swallow.

So the useful next step is to sort suppliers into types. Not because labels are tidy, but because the type of a supplier predicts almost everything that matters: how easily you can replace them, how their failures land on you, where your costs sit, and what kind of relationship makes sense.

There's no single official taxonomy — different businesses slice the same pie in different ways. Here are the five cuts that are most useful to a strategist.

Cut 1: Direct versus indirect suppliers

Direct suppliers provide things that physically become your product or are sold with it. Flour for a bakery. Timber for a furniture maker. Packaging that goes out the door with the goods.

Indirect suppliers keep the business running but never touch the product: the accountant, the IT provider, the cleaning company, the firm that services the delivery van, the energy utility. These are often called MRO suppliers (maintenance, repair and operations), and managers have a bad habit of ignoring them until the day the only machine that matters breaks down.

The distinction matters because direct suppliers shape quality and cost of goods sold, while indirect suppliers shape overhead — two very different lines on your cost sheet.

Cut 2: By what they supply

  • Raw material suppliers sell stuff that is processed or transformed: timber, steel, flour, cotton, crude oil. Their prices swing with global markets, which is why the Economy chapter matters here.
  • Component suppliers sell parts you assemble rather than transform: hinges, bolts, circuit boards, zips, screens.
  • Finished goods suppliers sell you something you resell unchanged — a wholesaler, importer or brand distributor. Retailers live almost entirely on these, which links straight to the Stores chapter.
  • Service suppliers sell work rather than things: haulage, marketing agencies, IT support, security, and staffing agencies that provide Labour by the day.
  • Capital goods suppliers sell big one-off items — machines, vehicles, shop fittings. These aren't daily costs; they're investment decisions that sit in your Financials.

Cut 3: By depth in the chain — the "tiers"

Every supplier has suppliers of its own. That chain is usually described in tiers.


Your Tier 1 supplier is the one you pay directly. Tier 2 supplies your Tier 1. Tier 3 supplies the Tier 2, and so on. You may never meet them — but they can still ruin your week: a fire at a Tier 2 sawmill becomes a shortage at your Tier 1, which becomes an empty workshop and an angry customer. Visibility gets thinner the further down you go, and that invisibility is one of the quiet risks of modern supply chains.

Cut 4: By the market they sell into

This cut tells you who holds the power in the relationship.

  • Sole-source (monopoly) suppliers. Only one realistic option exists — a patented component, a rare material, a specialist machinist. You have very little leverage, and switching is close to impossible.
  • Oligopoly suppliers. A handful of big players dominate. Think of the few global makers of certain industrial chemicals or aircraft engines. Prices move together and negotiation is tough.
  • Competitive suppliers. Many interchangeable firms offer much the same thing — stationery, basic haulage, generic packaging. Here you can shop around, switch easily, and squeeze on price.

Cut 5: By relationship and geography

  • Spot suppliers. You buy when you need to, at whatever price is going that day. Flexible, but no loyalty and no guarantees.
  • Contracted suppliers. Agreed prices, delivery terms and volumes over a set period. Predictable — which is exactly what your budgeting needs.
  • Strategic partners. Long-term relationships where you share forecasts and plans, sometimes exclusively. More effort, but they'll often prioritise you when supply is tight.
  • Local, national, or international. Local means short lead times, easy visits and sometimes lower transport cost; international means lower unit prices but longer lead times, currency swings, import duties and a longer list of Regulations to comply with.

Putting it together: a worked example

Rowan and Ash is a small furniture workshop. A quick inventory of its suppliers shows every type in action:

Supplier What they provide Type
Kelmoor Timber Oak boards Tier 1, direct, raw material, contracted, local
FastFix Ltd Hinges and screws Direct, component, spot, competitive market
Nordic Cushions Finished cushions Direct, finished goods, wholesaler
Granite Freight Deliveries to customers Indirect, service
Pemberton Tools Saw blades, machine servicing Indirect, MRO
Hollybank Finance Bookkeeping Indirect, service
The oak grower Kelmoor buys from Standing timber Tier 2 — not Rowan and Ash's supplier, but their risk

That table isn't busywork. It instantly shows Rowan and Ash that FastFix is a low-risk convenience, while Kelmoor Timber is a single-source bottleneck whose own supplier could shut the workshop down.

Why this matters to a strategist

Classifying suppliers is the first act of strategy, because each type hints at a different decision:

  • Risk. Sole-source and deep-tier suppliers are where a single failure can halt your Product. Competitive spot suppliers barely matter.
  • Cost. Direct suppliers drive cost of goods sold; indirect suppliers drive overhead. Both end up in your Costs and Financials.
  • Cash. Payment terms and lead times are the hidden difference between a service supplier paid monthly and a raw material supplier demanding cash up front — a working-capital question dressed up as a purchasing one.
  • Quality and reliability. Components and finished goods flow into your Product, and Distribution depends on service suppliers turning up on time.
  • Compliance and reputation. International and deep-tier suppliers drag labour, environmental and tax obligations into your risk register, even when they aren't legally yours.
  • Negotiation posture. Knowing whether you're facing a monopoly or a crowded market tells you whether to push on price or invest in the relationship.

None of this is about picking the cheapest option. It's about knowing what kind of supplier you're sitting across the table from — which is precisely what choosing and managing them depends on.

Summary

  • "Supplier" covers a huge range, so sorting them by type reveals where risk, cost and leverage actually sit.
  • Direct suppliers shape your product and cost of goods sold; indirect suppliers keep the lights on and add overhead.
  • Suppliers also split by what they sell (raw materials, components, finished goods, services, capital goods).
  • Tiers describe depth in the chain: Tier 1 is who you pay, Tier 2 and beyond are invisible but can still stop your business.
  • The market structure — sole-source, oligopoly or competitive — decides who has the upper hand.
  • Relationship and geography range from one-off spot buys to strategic partnerships, and from local firms to international ones with duties, currencies and regulations attached.
  • Every one of these types feeds into other chapters — Costs, Financials, Product, Distribution, Labour, Tax, Regulations and the wider Economy all lean on how well you understand the suppliers behind them.
