Stores as a Factor Across the Whole Business

A store is where your stock waits. Say it that way and it sounds like the dullest thing in this book — a room full of shelves doing nothing while the real business happens elsewhere.

But waiting isn't free, and it isn't neutral. Every item sitting in your store is a decision you've already made: to spend cash now, on the bet that a customer will want this thing later. Multiply that by every item on every shelf and you have one of the biggest and least examined levers in the whole business. Get the store right and it quietly funds your growth. Get it wrong and it eats your margin while looking perfectly tidy.

The store is the junction, not a cupboard

Look at where a store sits. Suppliers deliver in bulk, on their schedule. Customers buy in single units, whenever they feel like it. Something has to reconcile those two facts, and that something is the store.


You've met this idea twice already. The Market chapter explained that buyers and sellers are separated by gaps — space, time, quantity, matching — and that a market is the arrangement that bridges them. The Distribution chapter said the same thing from the seller's side. The store is where the time gap and the quantity gap get closed physically: goods wait here so customers don't have to.

That reframing does real work. It means the store isn't a storage problem — it's a buffer, and a buffer is a deliberate trade. Big buffer, few disappointments, lots of cash tied up. Small buffer, lean and cheap, but you'll run dry at the worst possible moment. There is no free position; there's only the one you chose on purpose.

Every shelf is money wearing a disguise

Here's the mental shift that separates a good strategist from a busy one.

Stock isn't "the stuff we sell". Stock is cash you have already spent and cannot spend again until somebody buys it. Your money went out to the supplier weeks or months ago; your customer's money comes in whenever they wander past. That gap is working capital — the subject of the Financials chapter — and the store is where it lives.

Run the loop and you'll see the problem: buy → hold → sell → wait to be paid. Every day the goods sit waiting, you're effectively lending money to your own shelves, interest-free. That's money that could have paid a wage, bought an oven, or sat in the bank. It's a real cost, even though no invoice ever arrives for it.

What holding stock actually costs

Beginners count the price on the purchase order and stop there. The purchase price is the least of it. Carrying stock costs money in at least six ways:

  • Space. Rent, heating, racking, the extra room you rented because the back one was full.
  • Insurance. Cover for fire, flood and theft rises with the value on the premises.
  • Shrinkage. Theft, breakage, spillage, damage in handling. It's invisible unless somebody is counting.
  • Obsolescence. Last season's colours, last year's model, a product sliding down its life cycle. Stock doesn't just sit — it ages.
  • Handling labour. Picking, stacking, counting, moving, re-counting. Every one of those hours is a wage.
  • The capital itself. The cash you can't use elsewhere, plus any interest you're paying to borrow it.

Add those together and the rule of thumb most businesses use is 20–25% of the value of the stock, per year. Which means £20,000 of stock on the shelves costs you roughly £4,500 a year just to be there — before anyone buys a thing.

Worked example: the bike shop's shelves

Think of the independent bike shop from the Competitors chapter. It holds around £24,000 of stock at cost — bikes, helmets, tubes, clothing, tools — and its cost of goods sold runs about £96,000 a year.

That's a stockturn of 4: the shelves empty and refill four times a year, or roughly every three months. And at 25% carrying cost, that stock costs about £6,000 a year in space, insurance, shrinkage and tied-up cash.

Now the owner does something unglamorous: she walks the shelves and finds £8,000 of it is dead. Sizes nobody wears, a discontinued colourway, three frames that have sat since the shop opened. She clears it at a discount and stops reordering it.

Before After
Average stock at cost £24,000 £16,000
Cost of goods sold £96,000 £96,000
Stockturn 4.0 6.0
Carrying cost (25%) £6,000 £4,000

She has saved £2,000 a year. Now hold that number against her margin. If the shop nets 5p on every pound of sales, she'd have to sell an extra £40,000 of bikes and helmets to earn the same £2,000 in profit.

Forty thousand pounds of extra selling — extra customers, extra staff hours, extra wear — to match what one afternoon with a clipboard achieved. Saving money in the store is almost always cheaper than earning it back at the till. That's the single most useful thing to know about stores as a profit lever.

One caveat, and it's the important one: shrinking the store only counts if you can still serve customers. Which brings us to the other half of the trade.

The other side of the coin: what running out costs

A customer walks in wanting a £600 bike. You don't have it. She walks to the shop down the road, buys it there, and comes back to you for a £4 inner tube twice a year.

The lost sale is the small part. The real cost is the relationship — the servicing, the accessories, the upgrades, and the friend she tells about the shop that didn't have it. Convenience goods depend on availability; so does almost everything else once a customer is standing in front of you with money out.

So the genuine trade looks like this:

Hold more Hold less
Cash Tied up in stock Freed for other uses
Availability High — few disappointments Risk of stockouts
Costs Storage, insurance, shrinkage Lower
Risk Obsolescence, forced markdowns Lost sales, damaged reputation
Makes sense when Demand is uncertain, lead times are long Stock is costly, perishable, or dates fast

Notice how many chapters are already in that table. Demand uncertainty is the Market chapter. Shelf life is the Product chapter. Lead times are Suppliers and Distribution. The cash line is Financials.

How the store reaches into every other part of the business

  • Product and its life cycle. A product in decline sitting in your store is a write-off waiting to happen. Watch the curve and you'll discount early, at a small loss, instead of late, at a big one.
  • Manufacturing. Batch production needs somewhere for the batch to sit, so the store is what makes batch manufacturing possible. But a deep buffer also hides problems — unreliable suppliers, poor quality, bad forecasting. When cash gets tight and inventory shrinks, the faults underneath suddenly become visible. Comfortable stock levels are a painkiller, not a cure.
  • Sales and revenue. You can only sell what someone can actually get hold of. Availability is part of the augmented product — the layer where most competition really happens.
  • Distribution. The store is the other end of the same pipe. Change your channels and you change what stock you hold, where, and for how long.
  • Labour. Picking, stacking, counting, securing and stocktaking are hours of indirect labour that never touch the product but always appear on the payroll.
  • Costs and financials. Carrying cost is a real cost, and it behaves like overhead — it barely twitches when sales dip, which is exactly when it hurts most.
  • Economy. Inflation makes stock a hedge; high interest rates make it expensive; a downturn strands it. A store is a position on the economy, whether you meant it or not.
  • Tax and regulations. Premises, insurance, food hygiene, fire safety and waste rules all land on the building where stock lives.
  • Suppliers and competitors. Your store is partly insurance against a supplier who lets you down — and the reason you can answer a rival's price cut without losing the sale.

What a good strategist does with it

  • Measure it with two or three numbers, not vibes. Stockturn, days of cover, shrinkage and availability. Track them weekly; store problems grow silently.
  • Split the range. A small share of your items will carry most of your sales and value. Look after those precisely; treat the long tail lightly.
  • Match stock to lead time and reliability. Long lead times and flaky suppliers buy you nothing by being lean. This is the how easily could I walk away? test from the Suppliers chapter, paid for in shelf space.
  • Count the true cost. Space, insurance, shrinkage, obsolescence, labour and tied-up cash — then compare it honestly against the cost of disappointing a customer.
  • Run the management loop on it. Plan how much to hold and where, organize the layout, systems and supplier terms, lead the people who keep it accurate, then control it by checking the numbers against the plan. The store is the easiest part of a business to run blind, and one of the most expensive when you do.
  • Treat it as a decision, not a cost of doing business. Every shelf is a choice about where your cash goes. Make it deliberately.

Summary

  • A store is where stock waits between being made and being bought. It closes the time and quantity gaps from the Market and Distribution chapters — it's a buffer, not a cupboard.
  • Stock is cash you've already spent, so the store is where working capital lives and where the business either frees money or strangles itself.
  • Holding stock costs roughly 20–25% of its value a year through space, insurance, shrinkage, obsolescence, handling and tied-up cash.
  • Cutting dead stock is often far cheaper than earning the same profit through extra sales — the £2,000 saved versus £40,000 sold example.
  • The other side of the trade is availability: running out costs you the sale, the customer and the reputation.
  • Stores touch Product, Manufacturing, Sales, Distribution, Labour, Costs, Financials, the Economy, Tax, Regulations, Suppliers and Competitors all at once.
  • Manage it with a few honest numbers — stockturn, days of cover, shrinkage, availability — reviewed on a rhythm, and treat every shelf as a decision about where your cash goes.
