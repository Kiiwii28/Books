11-2 Types of Stores

Part of 11 Stores

A warehouse the size of six football pitches. A stockroom smaller than a cupboard. A corner shop. A web page you have never physically stood in. All four are stores, and all four do the same basic job: they hold goods between the moment those goods are made and the moment somebody uses them.

That's where the family resemblance ends, though — and the differences aren't cosmetic. The type of store you have decides how much cash is sitting still doing nothing, how much rent you pay for the privilege, who you have to hire, and how much of the customer's money you actually keep. So let's sort the family out.

The two jobs a store can do

Every store does one or both of two jobs, and knowing which one dominates tells you most of what you need to know about the place.

Holding. The store keeps goods safe, counted and in good condition until they're wanted. This is the gap-closing machinery from the Market chapter — the time gap between when something is produced and when it is consumed, and the quantity gap between a pallet of flour and one loaf.

Offering. The store puts goods where buyers already are, so they can see, compare, choose and carry away. This closes the space and information gaps: you don't travel to the mill, and you don't have to guess which loaf is freshest when it's under your nose.


A vast warehouse is weighted almost entirely to holding. A showroom is almost entirely offering — the goods may never be in the building. The shop at the front of a bakery does both at once: the shelf is the offering, the back room is the holding.

Types by what they hold

Sort the storage side by how far the goods have travelled down your value chain, and a tidy ladder appears.

Type of store Holds The tricky part
Raw materials store Inputs from suppliers Price risk, spoilage, and cash you have already spent
Work-in-progress store Part-finished goods Awkward to value, easy to lose, and it blocks the line
Finished goods store Completed, sellable product Ties up the most cash — it's materials, labour and overhead, sitting still
Spare parts and consumables store What keeps machines running Boring, ignored, and the reason a line stops for a week
Bonded or regulated store Goods under customs or safety rules Paperwork, inspections, and you can't move them without permission

Look at the second column downwards and notice what's happening. The further goods have travelled, the more money is frozen inside them. A back room full of flour holds inputs at £1.20 a loaf's worth. A finished goods store holds loaves that have already absorbed wages, gas and rent. All of it is money you've spent that hasn't become revenue yet — which is the Financials chapter's working-capital problem, viewed from the storeroom floor.

The same finished goods can also be held at different depths in your network: one big central warehouse, or a handful of regional ones, or a store room behind every shop. Few and large is cheaper per unit but slower and further away; many and small is faster but dearer. That's the same speed–cost–reach triangle from the Distribution chapter — pick two.

Types by how they sell

Now the offering side. Here we sort by format — the shape of the offer a customer walks into. Note that this is a different question from the Distribution Channels chapter, which asked how goods travel to a buyer. This asks what kind of place the buyer arrives at.

Format What it is Wins on The economics
Convenience store Small, nearby, long hours, narrow range Location and speed Higher prices, tiny baskets, entirely dependent on footfall
Supermarket / hypermarket Big range, self-service, low prices Range and price Razor-thin margins, huge volume, brutal on suppliers
Specialty store Deep range in one category Expertise and choice Better margins, but needs genuine differentiation
Discount store Deliberately limited range, low prices Price Ruthless cost control and no frills whatsoever
Department store Many categories under one roof Assortment and experience Expensive to run; often relies on concessions
Cash and carry / wholesale Sells to businesses, not the public Bulk price per unit Volume-driven, usually membership-based
Market stall / pop-up Temporary, cheap to open Novelty and low fixed cost Flexible, unpredictable, weather-dependent
Showroom Displays goods, takes orders Advice and experience Almost no stock cost, but you need a fulfilment system behind it

Notice that format follows product type, exactly as the Product and Distribution chapters predicted. Convenience goods need intensive coverage — shops everywhere — which is why the same fizzy drink is in every petrol station and corner shop. Specialty goods need few, well-chosen outlets, because rarity is part of what the buyer is paying for. Put an artisan loaf in a discount supermarket and you haven't widened your market; you've muddled what your product is for.

Types by who owns them

Ownership decides who chooses the range, who sets the price, and where the profit lands.

  • Independent — the owner decides everything, keeps everything, and carries every bit of the risk.
  • Chain or multiple — central buying power and a consistent experience, but the local manager often controls very little of the offer.
  • Franchise — the brand and the system come from the franchisor, the capital and the graft from the franchisee.
  • Cooperative — owned by its members, usually on a one-member-one-vote basis, with surpluses shared by trade rather than by shares.
  • Concession or shop-in-shop — you rent space inside somebody else's store, selling without paying for the whole building.

That first versus second entry is really a question about organizing, from the management cycle: where in the hierarchy does the decision about price and range actually get made?

Types by channel: physical, online, or both

  • Physical store. Rent, business rates, staff, fittings — mostly fixed costs, paid whether or not a single customer walks in. You buy footfall, and you pay for it monthly.
  • Online store. Very little fixed cost, but a variable cost on every order: platform commission, payment fees, packaging, delivery, and returns. You buy reach and pay per sale.
  • Marketplace listing. You're a guest in somebody else's store. They own the traffic, you rent access, and you obey their rules.
  • Hybrid. Click and collect, order in store and deliver home, dark stores (a warehouse behaving like a shop that nobody enters), micro-fulfilment tucked into the back of a branch.

That fixed-versus-variable contrast is the Costs chapter in a single line, and it decides how badly each type suffers in a quiet month. The physical store still pays its rent; the online store just sells less.

Worked example: Priya's four stores

Store Type What it holds Who pays, and when
Back room Raw materials store Flour, butter, yeast, boxes Priya, on delivery — the cash has already gone
Counter and display Retail store — convenience format, independent A day's worth of bread The customer, on the spot
Saturday market stall Pop-up Whatever is left over The customer, on the spot; a small stall fee either way
Web pre-order page Online store Nothing — orders only The customer, up front, before the bread exists

Same bakery, four stores, four completely different cash profiles. The back room is money spent with nothing coming back yet. The counter is money in the same hour. The stall risks a small fixed fee against uncertain weather. The web shop takes payment before production, which is the strongest cash position of the four — and the one that costs her delivery time.

Just as importantly, she doesn't need all four to be equally profitable. They do different jobs: the counter pays the rent, the back room keeps production running, the stall finds new customers, and the web store smooths out demand. That's the same portfolio logic you met with the product life cycle — different pieces playing different roles, so that no single wobble sinks the whole thing.

What this means for you as a strategist

  • Match the type to the product and the buyer's habits. A convenience product in a single boutique fails on availability; a specialty product sold cheaply throws away the very thing that earned the premium.
  • Price the holding, not just the selling. Every store full of goods is cash doing nothing — and possibly spoiling, going out of date, or being stolen. Shrinkage and obsolescence are invisible until somebody counts.
  • Compare fixed and variable costs honestly. A shop and a web page can look similarly profitable on a good month and wildly different in a bad one.
  • Decide what each store is actually for. Availability, margin, discovery, experience, or reach? A store with no job is a cost with a sign above the door.
  • Staff to the flow. A shop's labour bill should follow its footfall, not its square footage — the Labour chapter's perishable-hours idea, applied to the rota.
  • Run the loop. Plan the range and stock levels, organise the layout and the deliveries, lead the people behind the counter, and then control it with three numbers: stock turn, sales per unit of space (or per order), and shrinkage. The management cycle works in a stockroom exactly as it works in a boardroom.

Summary

  • Every store does one or both of two jobs: holding goods until they're wanted, and offering them where buyers can choose. Different types weight those two jobs differently.
  • Sorted by what they hold, stores run from raw materials, through work in progress, to finished goods — plus spare parts and regulated stock. The further goods have travelled, the more cash is frozen inside them.
  • Sorted by format, stores range from convenience and discount through supermarkets, specialty shops and department stores to stalls, pop-ups and showrooms. Format follows product type, just as the Product and Distribution chapters predicted.
  • Sorted by ownership — independent, chain, franchise, cooperative, concession — the question is who decides the range and price, and where the profit goes.
  • Sorted by channel — physical, online, marketplace or hybrid — the real difference is whether your costs are mostly fixed or mostly variable, which decides how hard a quiet month hits.
  • The strategist's habit: choose the type deliberately, count what holding costs you, and measure each store on stock turn, sales per unit of space and shrinkage.
