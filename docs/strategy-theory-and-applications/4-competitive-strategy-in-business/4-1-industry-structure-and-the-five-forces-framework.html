4-1 Industry Structure and the Five Forces Framework

Part of 4 Competitive Strategy in Business

Industry structure is the discipline of asking why some industries are consistently more profitable than others — not why a particular company did well last year, but why the average firm in, say, pharmaceuticals earns returns that the average firm in airlines rarely approaches. Michael Porter's five forces framework, first published in Harvard Business Review in 1979 and substantially revised in 2008, is the most durable answer to that question and remains the default starting point for competitive diagnosis. This subsection sets out the framework, shows how to use it rather than merely recite it, and keeps in view what it can and cannot tell you. It picks up the diagnosis element from the formulation process and prepares the ground for the next two subsections on positioning and on sustaining advantage.

The Core Idea: Structure Sets the Average

Porter's argument, building on the older structure–conduct–performance tradition in industrial economics, is that the profitability of an industry is not primarily a matter of how well run its firms are, but of its structure — the configuration of forces that determine how much value the industry captures and how that value is divided.

The crucial corollary is that profitability is not created inside the industry alone; it is the residue left after the industry's customers, suppliers, and potential competitors have taken their share, and after substitutes have capped what can be charged. A firm in a structurally poor industry can be excellently managed and still earn mediocre returns; a firm in a structurally rich industry can be mediocre and earn good ones. That is why "we will simply execute better" is not a strategy, and why the framework belongs at the start of the analysis rather than as a checklist appended to it.

Porter is explicit that the framework applies at the level of the industry — a group of firms producing products that are close substitutes for each other — not at the level of a firm, a segment, or a market defined by a particular customer group. Defining that boundary correctly is the first and most consequential act of judgement in the analysis, and it is discussed below.

The Five Forces


1. Rivalry among existing competitors. The intensity with which firms in the industry compete for position — in price, features, service, or advertising. Rivalry is best understood as an outcome of the other four forces rather than an independent one: where entry is easy, buyers hold power, and substitutes are close, rivalry tends to be destructive whatever the intentions of the incumbents. Its intensity is driven by the number and balance of competitors, the growth rate of the market, the height of exit barriers, the ratio of fixed to variable costs (which invites price competition whenever capacity is idle), the lumpiness of capacity additions, the degree of product differentiation and switching costs, and the diversity of competitors' goals and origins.

2. Threat of new entrants. The ease with which new capacity can appear. The relevant question is less whether entry will happen than whether the threat disciplines incumbents' pricing today. Entry barriers include economies of scale, capital requirements, switching costs for customers, access to distribution channels, incumbency advantages independent of size (patents, proprietary technology, location, accumulated brand), unequal access to inputs, restrictive government policy, and the credibility of expected retaliation. Some barriers are structural, others are built deliberately — and, as later subsections will argue, a firm can invest in raising them.

3. Threat of substitutes. Products or services from outside the industry that perform the same function: video conferencing for business travel, e-mail for postal mail, heat pumps for gas boilers. Substitutes matter through their price–performance trajectory, not merely their current price; a substitute that is improving quickly can render an entire cost position obsolete. Because substitutes usually originate outside the industry's boundary, this is the force most often missed by analysts who define their industry by the products they already make.

4. Bargaining power of suppliers. The ability of input providers to capture value: concentrated suppliers relative to the industry, high switching costs, differentiated or scarce inputs, absence of substitute inputs, and the credible threat of forward integration. Labour is a supplier too, which is why tight specialised labour markets belong in this force rather than in a footnote.

5. Bargaining power of buyers. The mirror image: concentrated buyers, large volumes relative to a supplier's capacity, standardised products with low switching costs, transparent pricing, and the credible threat of backward integration or self-supply. Buyer power is amplified by price sensitivity, which depends on the buyer's own margins, the share of the input in its cost structure, and the incentives of the person making the purchase — a procurement function rewarded on unit-price savings will behave differently from an operations manager rewarded on uptime.

Two extensions are worth knowing. The first is complementors — providers of products that increase the value of the industry's offering — which Brandenburger and Nalebuff added as a sixth force. The second is the treatment of government and regulation as a direct force rather than an influence filtered through entry and supplier power; for healthcare, defence, energy, and education, doing so is usually more accurate. In platform and multi-sided businesses, each side of the market has its own buyer/supplier dynamics and the analysis must be run more than once.

Applying It: From Structure to Implication

A five forces analysis that ends with five bullet points has failed. The output worth having is a ranked judgement about which forces currently determine the industry's profit pool, how they are changing, and what that implies for where to position. A workable sequence runs as follows.


Define the boundary by the question. Product and geographic scope are analytical choices, not facts. "The coffee industry" is too broad to say anything; "wholesale specialty coffee roasting within a four-hour delivery radius" is answerable. The test is whether a price change by one firm in the defined set materially affects demand for another: if yes, they are rivals. Narrow definitions will make a firm look structurally advantaged and fatally overstate its insulation; broad ones will bury the segment where the economics actually differ.

Put evidence and numbers against each force. "Buyers have moderate power" is not an analysis. Better: three buyers represent 60% of revenue; the input accounts for 4% of the buyer's cost base, so it is not a priority in their purchasing; switching requires re-training two staff and re-validating a process, which takes about six weeks. Where quantification is impossible, rank the forces ordinally and be explicit about the reasoning.

Rank, do not enumerate. Usually two forces dominate. Identifying them is what makes the analysis strategic, because it tells you where to concentrate investment — a firm facing one dominant force should build its whole strategy around mitigating or exploiting it, not distribute effort evenly.

Track direction of change. Structure is a film, not a photograph. The most valuable part of the analysis is often the arrow: a force that is currently moderate but rising — a supplier consolidating, a substitute's cost curve falling, a buyer group merging — is more consequential than a force that is severe but stable. Porter's 2008 revision places particular weight on this, and it is the natural bridge back to the formulation process, where re-formulation triggers are agreed in advance.

Locate the profit pool. Industry averages hide a great deal. Within most industries some segments, stages of the value chain, or customer groups earn returns far above the average, and the forces differ across them. The analysis should end by identifying where in the structure value is actually captured.

A Worked Example

Return to Northwind, the specialty coffee roaster, whose strategy is to sell beans bundled with training and menu design to independent cafés with two to ten sites. Run the five forces against its industry.

Rivalry (moderate, and differentiated). Regional roasting is fragmented — many small roasters, several national brands — but the national brands compete on price in grocery and food service, not on local support. Product differentiation through service is achievable; capacity is not easily withdrawn, since a roastery is a sunk asset.

Threat of new entrants (high at the commodity end, low at the service end). A small roastery can be equipped for well under £100,000, there is no regulation, and online channels make distribution accessible. The barrier is not capital but the training capability and dense local logistics that Northwind's bundle depends on — which is precisely why that bundle is the strategy.

Threat of substitutes (moderate, and rising). National roasters' commodity beans, pods, and other beverages all substitute at the café level. The important substitute is subtle: cafés installing their own small roaster — backward integration that converts a customer into a non-customer.

Supplier power (low for green coffee, high for micro-lots). Commodity arabica trades in a global market with many importers, so supplier power is weak; named single-origin producers with scarce micro-lots hold real power, and equipment suppliers hold moderate power.

Buyer power (high and rising). Individual cafés are small, but they are sharply price-sensitive because their own margins are thin, and switching costs are low unless a service relationship is embedded. Multi-site groups with fifteen or more sites have genuine negotiating leverage. Rising rents are squeezing café margins further, which increases their price sensitivity — the force is moving against the industry.

Three implications follow directly. First, the segment where buyer power is weakest and differentiation is possible is small independent cafés, which is exactly why Northwind's scope stops at ten sites rather than extending into national chains. Second, embedding training and menu design raises the customer's switching cost, which is a deliberate structural move: it attacks the buyer power force rather than merely accepting it. Third, the rising price sensitivity of cafés means the bundle must demonstrably improve café profitability — a claim which becomes, in the formulation process, a testable one.

This is the framework doing its job: not producing the answer, but showing where the value can be captured and which forces the strategy has to change rather than endure.

What the Framework Does Not Tell You

Five forces analysis is widely taught and widely misused, usually by being treated as a verdict rather than a diagnostic. Four limits matter.

It explains averages, not outcomes. Empirical variance-decomposition studies (Schmalensee, Rumelt, McGahan and Porter) attribute somewhere in the region of 10–20% of the variation in business-unit profitability to industry membership, with firm-specific effects comparable or larger. Industry structure sets the average return available; where a particular firm lands within the distribution is a matter of position and capability. "The industry is unattractive, therefore we should exit" is a non sequitur, and it discards the observation that some firms earn excellent returns in every industry.

It is static and poorly suited to fast-changing markets. The framework describes the structure of an industry at a moment; it struggles with markets being created or destroyed by technology, where boundaries shift faster than the analysis can be completed. In such settings it remains useful as a list of questions but should be paired with scenario work and with explicit attention to how the forces are trending.

It assumes a bounded industry. Convergence, platform business models, and ecosystem competition all blur the boundary on which the whole analysis rests. When the boundary is genuinely ambiguous, run the analysis two or three times under different definitions and see which produces the decision-relevant insight.

It is a diagnosis, not a strategy. Structure constrains but does not determine conduct: firms can reshape forces through investment, build switching costs, lock up supply, or create network effects that raise entry barriers. The next subsection takes up exactly that question — how a firm chooses a position within the structure — and the one after that examines how advantage built on such a position is defended over time.

A final caution concerns how the analysis is conducted rather than what it contains. Judgements about which forces dominate are made by people whose attention is selective and whose recent experience is vivid; a dramatic new entrant will be over-weighted while a slow shift in buyer concentration goes unnoticed. The procedural remedies described earlier apply with full force here: evidence and numbers against each force, a deliberate search for the force that is changing rather than the force that is loudest, and a written view of the direction of travel that can later be shown to be wrong.
