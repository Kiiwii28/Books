4-3 Building and Sustaining Competitive Advantage

Part of 4 Competitive Strategy in Business

Positioning tells you where to stand; it does not tell you why anyone should let you stand there. The two preceding subsections worked from the outside in — industry structure defined how much profit was available, and generic strategies defined the positions from which a firm could claim some of it. This subsection works from the inside out, and asks the question those frameworks leave open: once a firm has found a defensible position, what makes the advantage behind it durable, and why does the same advantage evaporate for one firm and compound for another?

Two views, one question

The field has argued for decades about whether advantage is best explained from the market or from the firm.

The outside-in view, associated with Porter, holds that advantage comes from a defensible market position: attractive structure, a clear generic strategy, and a set of activities that fit together. The inside-out view, associated with the resource-based tradition — Wernerfelt, Barney, Peteraf, Grant — holds that advantage comes from what the firm owns and knows: resources that are valuable, rare, and hard to copy. Each view has been caricatured as complete, and neither is. They are two halves of the same argument:

  • The market view explains which advantages are worth having — a low-cost position in a commodity industry is worth little, however efficiently achieved.
  • The resource view explains why some advantages persist — a position that any adequately financed rival can occupy in eighteen months is not an advantage at all, only a head start.

A workable synthesis is that strategy selects a position where the firm's distinctive resources can be deployed, and then uses the profits from that position to deepen the resources. The position is the market-facing expression; the resources are the reason it holds.


The loop matters more than any single turn. A firm that captures value but never reinvests it is consuming its own advantage, however strong the position looks this year.

What makes a resource valuable

Barney's VRIO test remains the most practical screen, because it is a sequence rather than a verdict: a resource has to clear each question to matter strategically.

Test Question If the answer is no
Valuable Does it enable the firm to exploit an opportunity or neutralise a threat? It is a liability, not an advantage
Rare Do few competitors hold it? It yields parity, not advantage — necessary but not sufficient
Inimitable Would imitation be costly or slow? It yields only a temporary advantage
Organised Is the firm structured to exploit it — incentives, processes, coordination? The firm captures less than the resource is worth

Two clarifications keep the test honest. First, resources are not valuable in the abstract; a fleet of regional depots is valuable for a dense-delivery strategy and worthless for a digital one. Second, the fourth question is where most firms actually fail. It is common to find two competitors with near-identical capabilities in which one earns materially better returns purely because its structure lets it deploy them faster — the organisational element is doing all the work, and it is the hardest to copy because it is invisible in the market.

Isolating mechanisms: why advantages persist

The decisive concept in this subsection is the isolating mechanism — anything that delays or prevents imitation from eroding an advantage. Dierickx and Cool's 1989 contribution is the most useful taxonomy available, because it explains the persistence of advantages that are not owned in any legal sense and cannot be bought on any market.

Asset stock accumulation. Some assets are not obtainable by purchase but only by accumulation — brand trust, a dealer network, a safety record, tacit operating knowledge. You cannot buy thirty years of operating discipline; a rival with funds can build the same capability, but only by spending the same years. This is the source of the notion of time compression diseconomies: doubling the investment does not halve the time, because accumulation is rate-limited by learning and experience rather than by money.

Asset mass efficiencies. The more of an asset you already have, the cheaper the next unit is to obtain. Dense delivery routes make the next customer cheaper to serve; a large installed base makes the next software release cheaper to test. This creates a self-reinforcing spiral that punishes the smaller competitor precisely when it is trying to catch up.

Interconnectedness of asset stocks. Advantage built from many interacting assets is harder to replicate than any one of them. This is Porter's fit viewed dynamically: a rival can copy the loyalty scheme or the pricing model, but the value comes from the combination.

Causal ambiguity. When even the incumbent cannot fully articulate why it is successful, imitation stalls. This is a two-edged property: it defends an advantage effectively, and it makes the advantage harder to repair or transfer internally — as the chapter on mental models noted, firms are frequently unaware of their own recipes.

Social complexity and path dependence. Trust between teams, a relationship with a supplier built over twenty years, a reputation staked on a specific promise — these arise from particular histories and do not transfer.

Switching costs and network effects. Where customers face real cost in moving (retraining, re-integration, lost accumulated data) or where the product becomes more valuable as more customers adopt it, the advantage protects itself through the customer's own calculation rather than through any action by the firm.

Economies of scale and learning. Classic, and still the most robust in manufacturing: unit cost falls with accumulated volume along a learning curve, so the market leader can price below a rival's cost floor. The fragility is that the curve confers advantage only while the underlying technology is stable; a process change that resets accumulated volume resets the curve.

Mechanism What it buys Typical fragility
Accumulated tacit know-how Time delay on imitation Staff turnover; failure to codify and pass on
Fit across many activities Imitation requires whole-system change Bureaucratic drift; visible incoherence under cost pressure
Causal ambiguity Imitators can see outcomes, not causes Success can hide a decaying cause for years
Switching costs Customers stay without being asked Rises invite substitution by a whole new category
Network effects Demand-side scale economies Multi-homing; a standards shift
Brand and reputation Price premium and lower cost of trust One well-publicised breach

The renewal problem

Everything above explains persistence; none of it explains survival. The resource-based tradition has a well-known blind spot: it is a theory of what exists, and advantage requires a theory of what to build next. Teece's answer is dynamic capabilities — the capacity to sense opportunities and threats, seize them by committing resources, and transform the firm's asset base as circumstances change. This is not an extra resource but a meta-capability, and it is the one that fails most often, because the very processes that made the existing capability reliable also make it resistant to change.

The erosion forces are predictable even when the timing is not:

  • Imitation slowly closes the gap on measurable dimensions, particularly where the advantage is visible and no isolating mechanism guards it.
  • Substitution removes the need for the capability entirely — the capability is intact and irrelevant.
  • Preference shift moves value to a different set of purchase criteria, leaving an efficient operator of the old criteria well positioned for a market that no longer exists.
  • Internal decay does the work from the inside: cost discipline applied to the very activities that made the position distinctive, so that the advantage is quietly arbitraged away by its own owner.

The last of these is the most common and the least examined. A firm under margin pressure cuts training, then field support, then the quality control that allowed it to price at a premium — and each cut is individually defensible on the numbers. A single activity viewed in isolation always looks like a candidate for reduction; only a view of the whole system reveals that it was the strategy.


Ambidexterity — running the existing business efficiently while funding the search for the next one — is the organisational form of this loop. It is expensive, it is uncomfortable, and the earlier chapter's point about organisational slack applies directly: an exploration budget is the cheapest insurance a firm buys, and it is always the first line cut under stress.

Reading the early warnings

Advantage decays slowly and then suddenly, so the useful instrument is a small set of indicators reviewed on a fixed rhythm:

  • Margin persistence. Is the premium over the marginal competitor stable, widening, or quietly compressing despite decent revenue?
  • Share stability within cohorts. Total share can look healthy while the firm loses the new customers who set the next decade's reference price.
  • Customer switching and behaviour. Retention that is explained only by contract term, not by preference, is a rented position.
  • Competitor capability, not competitor moves. Watch what rivals can now do, not only what they announce. The relevant question is whether a rival has assembled the combination, not whether it has copied the component.
  • Internal capability health. Training throughput, backlog quality, engineering attrition, mentor-to-junior ratios — the things that produce the advantage rather than the results it produces.

The discipline echoes the earlier chapters: put a name against each indicator, define in advance what would count as evidence of erosion, and treat a persistent reading as a trigger for re-examining the diagnosis rather than as something to be explained away. Escalation of commitment to a decaying advantage is the single most expensive form of the bias, because the sunk costs are large, public, and identity-bearing.

A worked example

Return to Northwind, the specialty roaster whose strategy is to win by making each café more profitable per square metre, selling beans bundled with training and menu design.

What the advantage actually is. Not the coffee: the beans are excellent and roughly comparable to three regional rivals'. The advantage is the combination — dense local routes that make next-day service economic, a field-training capability that no national roaster can deliver at the account sizes Northwind serves, and a menu-design practice that sits on top of both.

Mechanism by mechanism. Route density gives asset mass efficiency: each account added to a route lowers the cost to serve every other account on it. The apprenticeship pipeline gives time compression diseconomies for anyone trying to copy: a competitor can hire two trainers, but cannot hire the four years of accumulated recipe knowledge those trainers carry. Café menus and staff habits built around Northwind's beans create switching costs that are real — not contractual, but embedded in how the customer's own business runs. And there is genuine causal ambiguity: a visiting competitor sees the bundle, the pricing, and the training days, and still cannot see which part produces the margin.

Where it is fragile. All three mechanisms can be attacked, and the firm should say how. A national roaster could open a regional training academy and accept the cost as a marketing expense — undercutting the time-compression barrier with subsidy, which is exactly how most accumulated advantages are beaten. A digital training platform could substitute for the field visit and remove the route-density advantage along with it. And internal decay is a live risk: under margin pressure, the field trainers are the most expensive line on the cost sheet and the easiest to reduce.

What renewal looks like. Investing ahead of erosion rather than defending the current position: codifying the training curriculum so it can be delivered remotely before a platform rival does it, adding a supply-side data service that deepens switching costs, and protecting the training headcount as a ring-fenced commitment even in a poor year — the equivalent of a kill criterion in reverse, a pre-commitment to the activity that constitutes the strategy.

Common failure modes

Failure What it looks like Antidote
Confusing a head start with an advantage Early lead, no isolating mechanism Ask what stops a funded rival in eighteen months
Capability in the wrong market Excellent at something customers no longer value Re-examine the diagnosis, not the execution
Imitating a rival's visible features Copying what can be seen, ignoring the fit beneath Ask what the rival's own combination is
Defending a position already substituted Reinvesting in a declining capability Separate a slow result from a wrong theory
Advantage arbitraged by its owner Cutting the activities that justify the premium Cost discipline applied at system level, not activity level
Causal ambiguity turned inward Nobody can explain why it works, so nobody can fix it Codify tacit knowledge even at the cost of some ambiguity
Never renewing A decade of excellent exploitation Fund exploration deliberately; review the erosion indicators

The practical conclusion is narrower than the literature sometimes implies. Advantage is rarely won by a single decisive insight and rarely lost in a single decisive mistake. It is built by accumulating assets that cannot be bought, protected by mechanisms that make imitation slow rather than impossible, and retained only by firms that are willing to make their current advantage obsolete themselves. That final willingness is not an analytical question at all — it is a matter of execution and leadership, which is where the remaining chapters turn.
