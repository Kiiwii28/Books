3-1 Personal Vision, Goals, and Strategic Priorities

Part of 3 Applying Strategy in Life

Organisational strategy is written by one group of people and lived by another. In personal strategy the two are the same person, which is both the opportunity and the difficulty: there is no board to demand a diagnosis, no competitor to punish a vague vision, and no annual cycle that forces the question. The vocabulary still transfers almost unchanged. This subsection covers the first three moves — a vision that specifies a future state, goals that make it measurable, and strategic priorities that make it finite — using the same elements introduced earlier: purpose, diagnosis, guiding policy, scope and trade-offs.

What a personal vision is for

A vision describes the future state you are trying to bring about, over a horizon long enough that reaching it requires building something you do not currently have. Its function is to make the rest of the choices comparable: without it, every opportunity looks equally attractive and every request on your time is judged on its own merits rather than against a standard.

Most personal vision statements fail the same way corporate ones do — they are lists of adjectives rather than descriptions of a state. "Be healthy, present, and successful" cannot be recognised if you arrive there, cannot be falsified if you do not, and excludes nothing. A usable version has three properties:

  • Concrete. It describes circumstances you could point at: the kind of work, the shape of your days, the relationships that are intact, the number on the balance sheet.
  • Sufficiently distant. Five to ten years is typical. A one-year horizon is a plan; a fifty-year one is a mood.
  • Demanding enough to force new capability. Here the logic of strategic intent applies directly: if the vision is merely an extrapolation of your current trajectory, it is a forecast wearing a vision's clothes. The gap between the vision and what your present habits would deliver is the thing that motivates learning, investment, and uncomfortable choices.

Keep the vision stable and contestable. Stability matters because you will revise dozens of plans under it; contestability matters because a vision that no reasonable friend could disagree with is not doing any work.

The diagnosis in between

Vision and goals are separated by a step that personal planning usually skips: an honest diagnosis of where you actually are. The organisational version of this element was the hardest to write and the most consequential; the personal version is harder still, because the person doing the analysis is the person with the most to protect.

A workable personal diagnosis answers four questions with evidence rather than narrative:

  1. Where do the hours and the money actually go? Not the intended allocation — the observed one, from a calendar and a bank statement.
  2. What is the binding constraint? Usually one thing dominates: available hours given caregiving or a demanding job, a specific skill or credential gap, a financial obligation, a health limit, or a relationship that needs repair. A vision that ignores the binding constraint produces a plan that quietly stalls.
  3. What is the real asset base? Skills, reputation, network, energy, savings, obligations. These are the resources the strategy must leverage, not the ones you wish you had.
  4. Which belief about yourself is doing the framing? "People like me don't run departments" is a mental model in exactly the sense discussed earlier — it determines what you notice as an opportunity and what you treat as out of reach, and it tends to accumulate apparent evidence.

Aspiration levels deserve particular care here. The cognitive literature is clear that people set targets relative to precedent and peers, then stop searching once an option clears the bar. A goal inherited from last year's plan, or copied from a more advantageously situated colleague, is an anchor rather than a requirement. Derive the goal from the vision and the constraint, and be able to say why that number rather than a comfortable one.

From vision to goals

Goals convert a future state into dated, measurable outcomes. Four rules keep them useful.

Keep the set small. Three to five goals across the domains that matter — work, health, relationships, learning, finances — is generous. Beyond that, the ranking collapses and every week becomes a negotiation with no arbiter.

Separate outcomes from lead measures. "Be promoted to lead a team of eight" is an outcome you influence but do not control. "Lead one cross-functional initiative to completion this year" is a lead measure you do control, and it is what the weekly plan should track. Outcome goals set direction; lead measures generate the evidence.

Use learning goals where the path is genuinely uncertain. If you cannot yet see how the outcome is achievable — a career change, a move abroad, a new venture — the first goal should be to run a bounded experiment rather than to hit a number. "Do three informational interviews and one paid side project this quarter" produces information; "launch a business by June" produces a plan built on untested assumptions.

Date it and make it cost something. A goal without a deadline is a preference, and a goal that requires no sacrifice will be met by whatever you were already doing. The sacrifice test transfers directly: if nothing attractive is being given up, the goal is not yet strategic.

Strategic priorities: the ranking that binds

A priority is not an area of importance. It is a position in a ranking that decides allocation — the answer to "when these two demands collide on a Tuesday evening, which one wins?" Most personal priority lists are not rankings at all; they are inventories, and there is no inventory whose top five items do not all look essential.

Two properties distinguish a real priority set. First, it is ordered: naming a primary matters more than naming a top three, because the tie-break rule is what does the work. Second, it is finite in scope, in the sense developed earlier: the most diagnostic word in a personal strategy, as in a corporate one, is only. Which domains are you deliberately not investing in this year — the sport you are not training for, the qualification you are not pursuing, the volunteer commitment you will not renew?

The ranking is only operational once a collision rule exists. Decide in advance what happens when the top priority has a crunch week: which floors hold, and how far the lower-ranked commitments may flex before they are actually abandoned. Rules agreed in calm conditions are far better than negotiations conducted at 11pm, for the same reason that re-formulation triggers are better agreed with the strategy than during a crisis.


Priorities also need fit. In a firm, activities should reinforce each other so the whole is harder to imitate; in a life, the equivalent is that one investment serves two goals at once — exercise that doubles as time with a friend, a work initiative that builds the skill you wanted to learn anyway. Where activities pull against each other (a demanding promotion and a fixed family floor), the tension is real and should be written down rather than discovered weekly.

The final test is the calendar. A priority that appears in no recurring block of time, and in no line of a budget, has not been chosen; it has been admired. The next subsection takes up precisely this allocation problem.

A worked example

Amara Diallo, 39, is a senior product manager at a logistics software firm. She has two children, a two-hour daily commute, and shares care of an ageing parent with a sibling. Her first attempt at a personal strategy reads:

"My vision is to be a successful leader, a present parent, healthy, and always learning."

Every word is agreeable and none is a choice. Rewritten against the elements:

  • Purpose: to make complicated operational systems genuinely usable by the people who work with them.
  • Vision (five years): running a product organisation of roughly forty people, known for operational software that frontline staff will actually use; children old enough to remember her being at bedtime; still training consistently, not episodically.
  • Diagnosis: she has no direct reports; her credibility with customers is high but she is seen internally as a fixer rather than a builder; the binding constraint is not skill but scope and visibility, and the evenings are consumed by a volunteer board role that no longer builds anything she needs.
  • Goals (12–18 months): lead one cross-functional initiative spanning three teams, from launch to a measured outcome; secure a role with at least eight reports, internally or externally; maintain three training sessions a week at 80% adherence; be home for bedtime four nights in five.
  • Ranked priorities: (1) the builder initiative; (2) the health floor; (3) bedtime four nights in five. Collision rule: in crunch weeks, training drops to twice weekly — never to zero — and the bedtime floor holds, with work resuming after 8.30pm.
  • Trade-offs: resign from the volunteer board; decline conference speaking that does not produce builder evidence; no external job search for twelve months; accept slower salary growth in exchange for the scope shift.

The second version can be wrong — the diagnosis might be mistaken about what is blocking her — and that is a feature. It also decides what she does on Monday, which is the only reliable evidence that a personal strategy exists.

Common failure modes

Failure What it looks like Remedy
Adjective vision "Healthy, happy, successful" Describe a recognisable state and a horizon
Diagnosis-free aspiration Ambitious vision, unchanged weekly behaviour Name the binding constraint before setting goals
Goal sprawl A dozen goals, all nominally equal Cap at three to five, each tied to a priority
Inherited aspiration Targets copied from peers or last year's plan Derive the number from the vision and the constraint
Priorities that never bind Nothing is ever declined Write a collision rule; audit the calendar
Route mistaken for destination A detailed ten-year plan, rewritten monthly Keep the vision stable, revise the route freely

One structural caution closes the section. An organisation's strategy is checked by boards, rivals, and customers; a personal strategy is checked by no one, and the person who wrote it is the person most motivated to interpret the evidence kindly. That asymmetry makes procedural devices — a written diagnosis, a small ranked set, a collision rule fixed in advance, and a review date that is genuinely inconvenient to ignore — more important, not less, than they are in a firm. Allocating time and money against those priorities is the subject of the next subsection; keeping the vision and the ranking honest over years is the subject of the one after.
