1-1 Defining Strategy and Its Core Elements

Part of 1 Basics of Strategy and Formulation

Strategy is one of the most used and least precise words in management. It appears in job titles, quarterly reviews, and political campaigns, often meaning little more than "something important we intend to do." This subsection gives a working definition, separates strategy from the concepts it is routinely confused with, and sets out the core elements a statement must contain before it deserves the name. Later sections build on this foundation: the next one distinguishes levels of strategy and strategic intent, and the one after that turns the elements below into a formulation process.

What Strategy Is

The word descends from the Greek stratēgos, the general who commanded an army, and it retains that martial flavour: strategy is the art of winning under conditions of competition, uncertainty, and limited resources. The classic definition comes from Alfred Chandler, who described strategy as "the determination of the long-run goals and objectives of an enterprise, and the adoption of courses of action and the allocation of resources necessary for carrying out these goals." Kenneth Andrews added that strategy is a pattern of decisions — a consistency that observers can detect across many separate choices, not a single document.

Michael Porter sharpened the idea in a way that remains the most useful test: strategy is not about operational effectiveness, which is about doing the same things better than rivals, but about being different — the deliberate choice of a unique and valuable position, rooted in a different set of activities, defended by trade-offs. His formulation forces attention on what a firm declines to do. A firm that tries to be everything to everyone has no position to defend, and its many activities, however well executed, will not add up to an advantage.

Henry Mintzberg's "five Ps" are a useful compact summary of how the term is used in practice: strategy as plan (a consciously intended course of action), as ploy (a manoeuvre to outwit a competitor), as pattern (consistency in behaviour, whether intended or not), as position (a place in the environment or market), and as perspective (a shared way of seeing the world). His distinction between intended and emergent strategy is a reminder that realised strategy is often what survives contact with events rather than what was written down.

Strategy Is Not a Goal, a Plan, or a Forecast

Much confusion dissolves once the adjacent concepts are separated. The table below gives the working distinctions used throughout this book.

Concept Question it answers Example
Purpose / vision Why do we exist? "Make specialty coffee accessible to independent cafés"
Goal What result, by when? "Reach 400 wholesale accounts by 2028"
Strategy How will we win, and where will we not compete? "Win by improving café profitability through a training-plus-beans bundle, only in the home region"
Tactic What specific move, now? "Offer a free menu redesign to any account over 40 kg/month"
Plan / budget Who does what, with which resources? Twelve-month hiring and capital schedule
Forecast What will probably happen? "Input costs up 8% next year"

A goal is a destination; a forecast is a weather report; a plan is a timetable. Strategy is the reasoning that connects the destination to the weather report and tells you which road to take — and, just as importantly, which roads to abandon.

The Core Elements

Definitions vary, but when a strategic statement actually guides behaviour, it almost always contains the same six ingredients.

1. Objectives. A long-run goal or set of goals that specifies what success looks like and over what horizon. Without an objective there is nothing to be strategic about; the difficulty is making the objective demanding enough to force choices and concrete enough to be judged.

2. A diagnosis. An honest, parsimonious account of the situation: what is really going on, what the critical obstacle or opportunity is, and why. Richard Rumelt argues that the diagnosis is the element most often missing and most consequential, because a correct diagnosis of the challenge does half the work of answering it. A diagnosis is a judgement under uncertainty, not a summary of everything known.

3. A guiding policy. The chosen overall logic for dealing with the diagnosis — the idea about how the organisation will succeed. Crucially, this includes a claim about the source of advantage: why this approach should produce value that rivals cannot easily match. "Be the best" is not a guiding policy; "be the fastest to serve a dense local market" is.

4. Scope. Where the organisation will play — which customers, products, geographies, channels, and stages of the value chain — and, explicitly, where it will not. Scope is the element that makes a strategy finite and therefore actionable. The most diagnostic word in any strategy document is only.

5. Coherent actions and resource commitments. A set of moves that reinforce one another and are backed by money, people, and management attention. Porter's notion of fit is the test here: activities should be mutually amplifying, so that the whole is harder to imitate than any single part. A list of unrelated initiatives is a portfolio, not a strategy.

6. Trade-offs. The deliberate surrender of otherwise attractive options. Trade-offs are what make a position defensible, because a competitor copying the strategy must also accept the sacrifices it entails. A strategy without trade-offs is a statement of intent that any well-funded rival can match, and it usually collapses into a race to be all things to all customers.

A Worked Example

Consider a fictional specialty coffee roaster, Northwind, which sells wholesale beans to independent cafés. Its board paper opens with this:

"Our strategy is to be the region's leading, highest-quality coffee roaster, growing revenue 20% a year through excellent customer service, innovation, and our passionate team."

Every phrase is pleasant and none is strategic. There is no diagnosis, no scope boundary, no claim about why Northwind specifically should win, no trade-off, and no coherent bundle of actions; a competitor could adopt the identical sentence without changing anything it does. It also, tellingly, cannot be falsified — there is no way to lose.

Now the same firm written against the six elements:

  • Objectives: 25% return on capital within three years, with 300 profitable wholesale accounts.
  • Diagnosis: Independent cafés are being squeezed by rising rents and staff costs and cannot afford dedicated barista training, while national roasters sell beans as a commodity and offer no local support.
  • Guiding policy: Win by making each café more profitable per square metre — selling beans bundled with training and menu design rather than beans alone. Advantage comes from dense local logistics and in-house training capability that national roasters cannot serve economically.
  • Scope: Independent cafés with two to ten sites within a four-hour drive. No own-brand retail shops, no national chains, no grocery channel.
  • Coherent actions: Hire two field trainers; run a small-batch roasting schedule with 48-hour turnaround; price as a beans-plus-service retainer; exit the low-margin grocery contract to free roasting capacity.
  • Trade-offs: Forgo grocery volume, and forgo the fast-growing home-subscription segment that would dilute roasting focus.

The second version is contestable — a rival might disagree with the diagnosis — and that is a sign of health. It also changes what people do on Monday morning, which is the only reliable evidence that a strategy exists at all.

Three Practical Tests

Before a statement is called a strategy, three questions are worth asking.

  1. The sacrifice test: what are we giving up? If nothing attractive is being surrendered, this is a wish list.
  2. The imitation test: could a competent rival copy this by reading our public materials, and would copying cost them anything? If not, there is no advantage claim.
  3. The Monday test: does it change any specific decision about hiring, capital, pricing, or accounts this quarter? If not, it is commentary.

These tests connect forward to the rest of the book: the imitation test is developed in the competitive strategy chapters, the Monday test in the chapters on execution and alignment, and the reasons strategists so often fail the sacrifice test are examined in the chapters on the cognitive psychology of strategic choice.
