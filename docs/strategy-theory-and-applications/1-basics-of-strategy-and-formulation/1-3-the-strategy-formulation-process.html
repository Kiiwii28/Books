1-3 The Strategy Formulation Process

Part of 1 Basics of Strategy and Formulation

Strategy formulation is the disciplined work of turning an ambiguous competitive situation into a set of commitments specific enough to act on. The previous subsection set out what a strategy must contain — diagnosis, guiding policy, scope, coherent actions, trade-offs — and the one before it distinguished strategy from goals, plans, and forecasts. This subsection is about the process that produces those elements: who does the work, in what order, with what outputs, and where it habitually goes wrong.

Two warnings frame everything that follows. First, the process is not a template to be filled in; a filled-in template is exactly what the six elements are meant to help you avoid. Second, the process is not linear. It is presented below as a sequence because sequences are easier to describe than loops, but in practice the stages overlap, feed back on one another, and are cut short or repeated as circumstances demand. The purpose of the sequence is not to be followed literally but to make sure no essential judgement gets skipped under time pressure.

Why Have a Process at All?

A named process buys three things. It imposes discipline — a defence against the natural tendency to start with the answer one already prefers and gather support afterwards. It creates shared language — when a leadership team has agreed what a "diagnosis" or a "strategic option" is, disagreements become substantive rather than semantic. And it allocates attention — it forces time on the questions that are expensive to think about and easy to postpone, particularly the question of what to stop doing.

The cost is ritual. Any process repeated annually eventually becomes a document-production exercise, with units rewarded for the polish of their submissions rather than the quality of their thinking. The stages below are worth keeping only if each produces a decision, not a slide.

The Stages


1. Frame the decision. Before analysis, specify what is actually being decided, by whom, by when, and at what level. "Should we enter the German market in the next eighteen months?" is a frame; "develop a growth strategy" is not. Framing sets the scope of the analytical work and the standard of evidence required, and it is the cheapest place to prevent an expensive exercise from answering the wrong question.

2. Diagnose. Establish what is really going on, internally and externally, and reduce it to a handful of critical issues. This is where the familiar analytical toolkits earn their place — industry structure, capability analysis, scenario planning, customer and competitor evidence — but their function is to support a judgement, not to be displayed. The output is one or two paragraphs that a rival could disagree with, not an exhaustive inventory.

3. Set the aspiration. Define what success looks like, over what horizon, and measured how. Aspiration and diagnosis are mutually constraining: an ambition that the diagnosis shows to be unreachable is fantasy, and a diagnosis with no ambition attached produces no pressure to change anything.

4. Generate options. Produce three to five genuinely different ways of winning, not three variants of the same idea. The most common failure here is what might be called false plurality — one real option surrounded by two deliberately weak ones, included so the chosen course can be said to have been tested. Good option generation is deliberately divergent, even uncomfortable: what would we do if our largest customer disappeared? What would a well-funded entrant do to us?

5. Evaluate and choose. Judge the options against explicit criteria — likely value, fit with capabilities, risk, reversibility, feasibility of financing — and then commit. The output is a chosen guiding policy together with the trade-offs it entails. This is the stage that most often fails politically, because genuine choice means some people's proposals lose. The characteristic pathology is averaging: a strategy assembled from the least objectionable parts of every option, which satisfies each sponsor and commits the organisation to nothing.

6. Codify and resource. Turn the choice into a short strategy statement in the six-element form, then attach money, people, and management attention to it. Codification is not bureaucracy; writing forces precision, and a document that cannot be summarised in a page has probably not been decided. The resource step is what separates formulation from commentary, and it is the bridge to operational alignment.

7. Test and adapt. Specify in advance what evidence would show the strategy is failing, and what events would justify revisiting it. Formulation is not a once-a-year event but a standing commitment to revise beliefs when the world speaks.

The underlying rhythm: diverge, then converge

Running through the sequence is a two-stroke rhythm. Stages 2 and 4 diverge — widening the set of facts considered and options entertained. Stages 3, 5, and 6 converge — reducing many possibilities to one committed course. Groups that diverge without converging produce analysis with no decisions; groups that converge prematurely produce confident strategies built on unexamined assumptions. Naming which stroke the meeting is currently in is often the single most useful intervention a facilitator can make.

Who Formulates?

Formulation is not the sole property of the executive suite, though it is ultimately the executive's accountability. Three modes are common, and most organisations use a blend.

  • Top-down: senior leadership sets the diagnosis and guiding policy, and units align. Fast, coherent, and prone to missing local evidence.
  • Bottom-up: units propose strategies within a corporate frame of financial and portfolio constraints. Rich in evidence, prone to aggregation — a pile of unit plans is not a corporate strategy.
  • Middle-out: corporate sets the ambition and the trade-offs it will enforce; units and functions develop options against them. Slower, but usually produces better diagnosis and higher commitment.


Whoever does the drafting, the choice itself must be traceable to a named decision-maker. Strategies that belong to a committee tend to lack trade-offs, because committees are constructed to avoid them.

Cadence: Calendar, Trigger, or Both

Most organisations run an annual cycle tied to the budget, sometimes with a mid-year review. The annual rhythm has real virtues — it synchronises resource allocation, and it guarantees that thinking happens at least once a year — but it has a characteristic defect: it tends to treat a continuous question as a periodic one. A competitor's move in March does not wait for September.

The practical answer is a hybrid. Keep the calendar cycle for the resource-intensive parts (portfolio, capital, major commitments) and add trigger-based re-formulation for events that invalidate the diagnosis: a structural change in the industry, a technological shift, a shock to the core customer, or performance persistently outside the expected range in either direction. Deciding the triggers when the strategy is agreed is much easier than deciding them during a crisis, when the temptation is always to explain the evidence away.

Common Failure Modes

Failure What it looks like Antidote
Boiling the ocean Months of analysis, no decision Frame the decision; cap analysis time
Diagnosis-free planning Numbers without a theory of why Require a contestable diagnosis paragraph
False plurality One option, two straw men Assign an option to its strongest critic
Averaging Strategy that everyone half-supports Force explicit trade-offs; name the loser
Template compliance Polished documents, unchanged behaviour Judge by decisions changed, not decks delivered
Strategy by calendar Blind to events between cycles Pre-agreed re-formulation triggers

A Running Example

Return to the specialty coffee roaster whose finished strategy statement appeared in the previous subsection. Formulated as a process, it looks like this. Northwind's board frames the decision narrowly — "how do we grow profitably without entering grocery?" — rather than the customary "what is our five-year vision?" Diagnosis follows: cafés are squeezed by rents and staff costs and cannot afford training, while national roasters compete on price and provide no local support. The aspiration is 25% return on capital within three years. Four options are developed, including the two that will lose: private-label grocery supply, and a home-subscription service. Both are genuinely attractive, which is why discarding them counts. In evaluation, the board concludes that grocery volume would consume the small-batch roasting capacity the training bundle depends on, and that subscriptions would fragment a delivery fleet built for dense local routes. The choice — beans bundled with training and menu design, within a four-hour drive — is written in six-element form, and the low-margin grocery contract is cancelled, freeing capacity. The tests are agreed at the same meeting: if average revenue per account does not rise within four quarters, or if field trainers cannot be hired at the modelled cost, the diagnosis is wrong and the board reconvenes.

The example matters because the process did not generate the answer; it forced the answer to be explicit, contestable, and paid for. That is all a formulation process can do, and it is a great deal.

Two cautions close the section. The first is cognitive: every stage above is a judgement made by people with limited attention, prior commitments, and a documented fondness for confirming what they already believe. How those limits distort diagnosis, option generation, and evaluation is the subject of the next chapter. The second is temporal: a strategy is a hypothesis about the future, and the process that produces it must be connected to a process that learns from its reception — the concern of the later chapters on execution and strategic learning.
