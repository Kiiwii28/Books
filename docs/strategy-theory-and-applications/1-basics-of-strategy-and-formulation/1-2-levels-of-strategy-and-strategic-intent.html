1-2 Levels of Strategy and Strategic Intent

Part of 1 Basics of Strategy and Formulation

Strategy is not a single document produced once at the top of an organisation. It is a layered system: different groups of managers answer different questions, over different time horizons, and the answers must fit together. Standing above that hierarchy is something less tangible but more powerful — strategic intent, the ambition that tells everyone which direction counts as winning. This subsection sets out the levels, shows how they connect, and then examines strategic intent as the animating force that gives the levels a common purpose.

The Three Levels

Most organisations, whether a multinational, a hospital, or a two-partner consultancy, can be described with three levels of strategy. They are distinguished not by seniority but by the question each level answers.

Corporate level: where should we play at all?

Corporate strategy is the responsibility of the top management team and the board. It concerns the composition and scope of the organisation as a whole: which businesses, markets, and geographies to be in; how capital and talent are allocated between them; whether to acquire, divest, integrate vertically, or form alliances. Its "customer" is ultimately the provider of capital, and its horizon is typically five to ten years or longer.

The test of good corporate strategy is not the elegance of the portfolio chart but parenting advantage: does the centre add more value to its businesses than they would enjoy as stand-alone entities, or under a different parent? A corporate centre that merely aggregates financial results and sets budgets is a cost, not a strategy.

Business level: how do we win here?

Below the corporate level sit strategic business units (SBUs) — units with a distinct set of customers, competitors, and economics. Business strategy answers the competitive question: how will we win in this market against these rivals? It is where the choice of value proposition, positioning, and generic strategy (cost leadership, differentiation, or focus) is made, and where trade-offs become concrete. The horizon is usually one to five years.

In a single-business firm the corporate and business levels collapse into one, which is why founders of small companies often find the distinction abstract until they open a second product line or a second country.

Functional and operational level: how do we support the winning?

Functional strategy translates the business strategy into the policies and capabilities of each function — marketing, operations, R&D, HR, finance, IT. It answers: what must this function do exceptionally well for the business strategy to succeed, and what resources does that require? Horizons are short for operational plans and longer for capability building such as a manufacturing platform or a data infrastructure.


| Level | Central question | Horizon | Typical decisions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corporate | Which businesses, and why under one roof? | 5–10+ years | Acquisitions, divestments, capital allocation, geographic entry |
| Business | How do we win against these competitors? | 1–5 years | Value proposition, positioning, pricing logic, segment focus |
| Functional | What must each function do to support that? | Months to 3 years | Capacity, skills, systems, supplier and channel policies |

Two refinements

Some writers add a fourth, network or ecosystem level — how the organisation positions itself among partners, platforms, and standards-setters, which matters greatly for technology and platform businesses. Others split off operational strategy as the day-to-day scheduling and deployment layer beneath functional strategy. Both are useful extensions rather than contradictions of the three-level scheme.

Making the Levels Coherent

The hierarchy is a constraint system, not a chain of command. Each level narrows the options of the one below it, and each lower level feeds back information about feasibility and cost. Coherence fails in three recognisable ways:

  • The corporate level adds no value. Units are left as a loose portfolio, and "group strategy" is a capital allocation spreadsheet.
  • Levels are copy-pasted. The business unit's strategy document repeats the corporate one with the word "division" inserted, and the functions repeat the business one. Nothing is being decided anywhere.
  • Functions drift. Marketing pursues premium branding while operations is incentivised for low-cost volume — each functional strategy defensible on its own, together incoherent.

The remedy in each case is the same discipline introduced earlier: at every level, ask what is being declined, and check that the answers are mutually consistent.

Strategic Intent

Levels describe the architecture of strategy. Strategic intent supplies its ambition. The term was popularised by Gary Hamel and C. K. Prahalad in their 1989 Harvard Business Review article, drawing on firms that had vaulted past far better-resourced rivals: Komatsu's declared aim to "encircle Caterpillar," Canon's ambition to "beat Xerox," Honda's early intent to become a global automotive manufacturer.

Their argument was that conventional strategic planning starts from fit — matching ambitions to existing resources — and therefore ratifies the status quo. Strategic intent instead starts from stretch: an aspiration so far beyond current resources that the organisation is forced to invent new ways of competing. The resulting gap between ambition and capability is not a planning error to be corrected but the engine of the whole exercise.


Hamel and Prahalad's five levers of resource leverage explain how the gap is closed: concentrating resources on a few decisive goals; accumulating them through learning and experience; complementing them by blending resource types to create disproportionate value; conserving them by co-opting partners' resources and reusing assets; and recovering investment quickly by shortening the time between spending and payback.

A practical way to state intent is the BHAG — a big, hairy, audacious goal, in Collins and Porras's phrase — anchored by a deadline and a measurable outcome, plus a consistent marching rhythm that keeps progress steady rather than episodic.

Intent versus purpose, vision, and mission

These terms are often used interchangeably and need not be, though organisations vary widely in practice. A workable division of labour:

  • Purpose (mission): why the organisation exists and whom it serves.
  • Vision: a description of the future state if it succeeds.
  • Strategic intent: the winning ambition — what the organisation intends to be best in the world at, expressed with enough stretch to force capability building.
  • Values: constraints on the means used to get there.
  • Goals and objectives: the measurable milestones along the way.

Purpose and values are largely stable and non-competitive; strategic intent is inherently comparative and demanding.

The risks of intent

Strategic intent is powerful and easy to abuse. Stated as pure aspiration, it becomes hubris and demoralises the organisation when milestones are missed. Three safeguards keep it honest: an explicit diagnosis of why the gap can be closed (intent must be paired with a credible logic of resource leverage, not just an adjective like "world-class"); intermediate milestones that make progress visible and falsifiable; and a willingness to change means while keeping the intent stable. When a firm abandons its ambition every time a plan misses, intent was never real; when it refuses to revise any plan that serves the intent, stretch has hardened into dogma.

A Worked Example

Return to Northwind, the specialty coffee roaster, now expanded so that all three levels are visible.

  • Corporate strategy. Own two businesses — wholesale roasting to independent cafés, and a small equipment-servicing arm — and nothing else. Divest the grocery contract to release roasting capacity; no retail shops, no home subscription. The parenting logic: shared roasting capacity, shared local logistics, and a common customer relationship that neither business could build alone.
  • Business strategy (wholesale). Win by making each café more profitable per square metre, selling beans bundled with training and menu design rather than beans alone. Serve cafés with two to ten sites within a four-hour drive.
  • Functional strategy. Operations runs a small-batch schedule with 48-hour turnaround and holds a deliberately narrow SKU range; HR hires two field trainers and builds an apprenticeship pipeline; finance prices the bundle as a service retainer rather than at cost-plus per kilogram. Each functional choice is what makes the business strategy physically possible.

Now add strategic intent: to be the roaster that no independent café in the region can outgrow. Northwind today can serve perhaps forty cafés with its training capacity; the intent demands several hundred. That gap is what justifies the apprenticeship pipeline, the narrow SKU range, and eventually a second roastery — choices that a fit-based plan would have deferred indefinitely. The intent is stable; the plans serving it are revised annually.

Why the Two Ideas Belong Together

Levels without intent produce coherent but timid organisations: every unit aligned, nothing attempted. Intent without levels produces slogans: a soaring ambition that no business unit is funded or staffed to pursue. The two work as a pair — the hierarchy converts ambition into specific, funded decisions, while the intent ensures the hierarchy is aiming at something worth the coordination cost.
