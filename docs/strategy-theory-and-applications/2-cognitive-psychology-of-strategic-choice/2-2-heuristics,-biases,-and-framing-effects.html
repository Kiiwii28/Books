2-2 Heuristics, Biases, and Framing Effects

Part of 2 Cognitive Psychology of Strategic Choice

Heuristics, Biases, and Framing Effects

The previous subsection established that strategists work with limited attention, incomplete information, and finite computation — the condition Herbert Simon called bounded rationality. This subsection examines what fills the gap. Because deliberation is expensive, human judgement relies on heuristics: fast, frugal rules of thumb that substitute an easy question for a hard one. Most of the time they work remarkably well, which is precisely why they are so hard to give up. Under the specific conditions that strategic decisions create — ambiguity, delay, high stakes, group pressure — the same shortcuts produce systematic and therefore predictable errors. And because those errors are predictable, they can be partly defended against.

Two distinctions frame everything below. First, a bias is not a random mistake or a lapse of intelligence; it is a reliable direction of error that affects experts and novices alike, and affects experts more when they are confident. Second, framing is not a separate topic but the lever through which most biases operate: the same facts, described differently, produce different choices.

Heuristics: Fast Rules That Usually Work

Kahneman and Tversky's research programme identified a small set of judgemental shortcuts that people use to answer difficult questions. Each is genuinely useful; each has a characteristic failure mode.

Heuristic What it does Where it misleads strategy
Availability Judges likelihood by how easily examples come to mind Vivid competitor moves, recent crises, and memorable failures dominate over quieter base rates
Representativeness Judges probability by resemblance to a stereotype A plan that "looks like" a successful company's plan is assumed to work in a different context
Anchoring and adjustment Starts from an available number and adjusts insufficiently First forecasts, last year's budget, and the first speaker's estimate set the range
Affect Lets an emotional reaction stand in for an evaluation A charismatic acquisition target or a disliked rival is judged on feelings, not evidence
Recognition Prefers the familiar option Well-known markets and familiar technologies attract entry; neglected ones are assumed inferior

Availability explains why boards over-weight the last industry crisis and under-weight the slow structural shift. Consider a firm that has just lost a major account through a supply failure: for the next two years, every risk register is dominated by supply continuity, while the more consequential but less narratable risk — gradual erosion of its pricing power — gets two lines. The heuristic is doing its job (it directs attention to a real danger) and its damage simultaneously.

Representativeness is the engine of survivorship bias in strategy. Organisations study successful companies and copy visible features of their strategies, ignoring the base rate of firms that adopted the same features and failed. The question "how many companies that built a culture of experimentation actually prospered?" is far more informative than "what does the successful one do?", and it is almost never asked.

Anchoring shows up wherever numbers are negotiated. An acquisition opening offer, an analyst's first target price, or a founder's gut estimate all shift the final figure toward themselves, regardless of how much analysis follows. The practical implication is procedural: obtain independent estimates before discussion, and be suspicious of any range whose midpoint is the first number spoken.

When Heuristics Become Strategic Biases

The biases most corrosive to strategy are not the ones on the list above but the motivated ones — errors that protect existing beliefs, existing commitments, and existing self-images.

Confirmation bias. The tendency to seek, notice, and remember evidence that supports an existing hypothesis. In strategy its most expensive form is in the diagnosis: once a leadership team believes that growth has stalled because of product quality, market research becomes a search for customer complaints. The most instructive experiments on this — Wason's rule-discovery task — showed that people rarely test their hypotheses by trying to falsify them; they test by generating more confirming cases.

Overconfidence. Three distinct varieties matter. Overprecision — confidence intervals that are far too narrow — is the most damaging in forecasting; planners routinely state that completion will take "between seven and nine months" when the honest interval is four to eighteen. Overestimation of one's own capability and overplacement relative to rivals complete the set, and both are near-universal among entrepreneurs and new entrants.

The planning fallacy. The specific tendency to forecast project cost and duration from an inside view — the plan as designed, case by case — while ignoring the distribution of outcomes for comparable projects. Kahneman's recommended correction is the outside view: ask what happened to the reference class of similar projects, and start from that base rate.

Escalation of commitment. Staw's classic finding is that decision-makers increase investment in a failing course of action, particularly when they are personally responsible for the original decision and the outcome is public. This is not simple stubbornness; it is a coherent response to the social cost of admitting failure. In strategy it appears as the market entry that is "only one more year from breaking even" and the acquisition whose integration is funded three times over.

Group effects. Janis's groupthink describes the deterioration of judgement in cohesive teams under pressure: self-censorship, an illusion of unanimity, and the suppression of dissent. The strategy-setting equivalent is the meeting where the trade-offs are never named, because no one wishes to be the person who kills the initiative. Related is hindsight bias, which makes past decisions seem more predictable than they were and thus teaches the wrong lessons in post-mortems.

None of these are character flaws. They are the predictable output of a mind that must act before it fully knows, in an organisation that rewards conviction. The design question is therefore not "how do we become unbiased?" but "what procedures make these biases expensive to indulge?"

Framing Effects

A frame is the set of assumptions, reference points, and categories through which a decision is presented. Framing effects are the most reliably demonstrated and least intuitive of all the phenomena in this chapter, because they show that preferences are not stable — they are constructed at the moment of choice from the way the question is put.

Tversky and Kahneman's Asian disease problem makes the point with brutal economy. Six hundred people are at risk. Two programmes are described:

  • Gain frame: Programme A saves 200 people for certain; Programme B has a one-third chance of saving all 600 and a two-thirds chance of saving none. Most respondents choose A — the certain gain.
  • Loss frame: Programme C means 400 people die for certain; Programme D has a one-third chance that nobody dies and a two-thirds chance that all 600 die. Most respondents choose D — the gamble.

The two pairs of programmes are mathematically identical. The only difference is whether outcomes are described as lives saved or lives lost, and it reverses the majority preference.


The mechanism is prospect theory. Outcomes are evaluated relative to a reference point, not in absolute terms; losses loom roughly twice as large as equivalent gains; and the value function is concave for gains and convex for losses. This produces a set of familiar strategic pathologies:

  • Status quo and endowment effects. What we already hold is valued more highly than an identical thing we might acquire. Divestment decisions therefore feel like losses and are chronically postponed — the business unit everyone knows should be sold survives because selling would mean realising the loss publicly.
  • Narrow framing and mental accounting. Evaluating each decision on its own rather than as part of a portfolio. A firm that would never accept a 50/50 bet with a positive expected value will happily take ten such bets from separate units and treat each failure as an isolated scandal rather than a cost of the portfolio's design.
  • Attribute framing. "90% survival at five years" versus "10% mortality" changes uptake of identical treatments, and "beef is 75% lean" sells better than "beef is 25% fat". Any strategy argument that rests on a single presented ratio deserves to have its complement stated out loud.
  • Goal framing. A loss-framed goal ("avoid falling behind") and a gain-framed one ("become the standard") mobilise different behaviours; loss frames are energising and risk-seeking, gain frames are steady and conservative. Which is appropriate depends on the diagnosis, not on taste.

Framing also operates in the choice of what is treated as the decision. "Should we enter Germany?" invites a yes/no; "which of these three markets, if any, should receive our next increment of capital?" invites comparison and a null option. The frame determines which arguments can even be made.

A Worked Example

Northwind, the specialty coffee roaster, arrives at the decision of whether to open a second roastery. Watch four separate effects operate on the same decision.

Anchoring. The board's first paper quotes the cost of the first roastery, built six years earlier: £480,000. Every subsequent estimate clusters around that figure, even though equipment, energy, and fit-out costs have moved substantially in opposite directions.

Availability. Two vivid data points dominate the discussion. A neighbouring roaster's expansion failed spectacularly last year, and a well-known competitor opened three sites in quick succession. Both are memorable and both are single cases; neither is a base rate.

Escalation of commitment. Northwind has already spent £90,000 on site search and planning for a specific building. The sunk cost is repeatedly invoked as a reason to proceed ("we'd be throwing that away"), although it is irrelevant to whether the building is the right option now.

Framing. The proposal is presented as "protect our 300 accounts from competitive encroachment" — a loss frame, which makes the risk-seeking option (build now, at a premium, in the only available building) feel prudent. Reframed as "put £2m of capital against a 40% chance of a second training capability being ready on time," the same proposal looks like a different decision.

The board's remedy is procedural rather than exhortative. It commissions an independent reference-class estimate of regional roastery expansions from the last decade (outside view); it obtains the site-selection argument and the capital case in separate papers written by different people (breaking the anchoring and confirmation chain); it states the sunk cost explicitly and rules it inadmissible; and it restates the decision in both frames before voting. The second roastery may still be a good decision — the point is that it is now a decision about the future rather than a decision about the past.

Reducing Exposure: Decision Hygiene

Because biases are systematic, defences can be designed in advance rather than summoned in the moment. The useful metaphor is hygiene: not a cure, but a set of routines that reduce the frequency of contamination.


Several of these are worth naming specifically. The premortem, Klein's technique, asks a team to imagine the plan has failed and to write the reason: the exercise creates a formal permission structure for dissent that ordinary meetings do not. Red teaming assigns someone the job of advocating the option the leadership does not prefer. Kill criteria, written when the strategy is agreed, convert escalation of commitment into a rule-following problem rather than a later argument about whether the last eighteen months "proved" anything. A decision journal — a short record of what was decided, why, and what was expected — is the only practical defence against hindsight bias, because it preserves the reasoning that the outcome makes invisible.

Two caveats keep this list honest. Checklists and structured dissent do not make judgement accurate; they make it disciplined, and they are worth the cost mainly on infrequent, high-stakes, hard-to-reverse decisions. And there is a real risk that debiasing rituals themselves become a compliant performance, complete with premortem slides and no candour. The test, as always, is whether any decision actually changed.

Finally, the boundary of this subsection matters. Heuristics, biases, and framing all describe the shortcuts of judgement. What happens when a decision-maker bypasses the shortcut entirely and responds to a situation directly, out of trained expertise and accumulated feeling, is a different mechanism with different strengths — the subject of the next subsection.
