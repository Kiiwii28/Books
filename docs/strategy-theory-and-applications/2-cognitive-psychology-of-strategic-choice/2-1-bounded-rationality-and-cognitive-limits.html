Bounded Rationality and Cognitive Limits

Strategy is a choice made by people who cannot know everything, cannot consider everything, and cannot hold much in mind at once. That observation sounds like an apology, but it is actually a structural fact about strategy, and it shapes every stage of the formulation process described earlier. This subsection sets out the idea of bounded rationality, the specific cognitive limits that produce it, and — most importantly — what those limits do to the diagnosis, options, and trade-offs a strategist produces.

Simon's Reframing of Rational Choice

Classical economics models a decision-maker who surveys all alternatives, evaluates them against a consistent utility function, and selects the optimum. Herbert Simon called this figure economic man and argued that no organism, and certainly no manager, could meet the model's requirements. His alternative, administrative man, is a being whose rationality is bounded by three things: the information available, the computational capacity of the mind, and the time in which a decision must be made. Bounded rationality is not a theory of error. It is a theory of what rationality looks like when the decision-maker is finite.

Simon's sharpest image for this appears in The Sciences of the Artificial: human rational behaviour is shaped by "a scissors whose two blades are the structure of task environments and the computational capabilities of the actor." Strategy fails when practitioners look only at one blade. The environment — industry structure, competitors, customers — gets most of the analytical attention, but the shape of the choice is equally determined by the mind doing the choosing. A board analysing an industry with great sophistication while operating on four hours of sleep, twelve familiar options, and an unexamined prior is being cut by the second blade.

The Limits, Specifically

Bounded rationality is often treated as a single vague constraint ("we can't know everything"). It is more useful to break it into distinct limits, because each has a different signature in strategic work and a different remedy.

Attention is scarce, not information. Simon's observation that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" has aged into prophecy. Modern strategists are rarely short of data; they are short of the capacity to notice what matters in it. Attention is selective by design — the mind filters aggressively so as not to be overwhelmed — which means the diagnosis a leadership team produces is always a selection from the available evidence, made by people whose attention was already allocated, often by habit or by whoever spoke last.

Working memory is small. The classic estimate of "seven plus or minus two" items has since been revised down to roughly four chunks, though chunking — grouping related items into a single unit — massively extends effective capacity. The strategic consequence is that complexity must be compressed to be thought about. Frameworks, mental models, and even crude analogies are compression devices. They make hard problems tractable and, in doing so, discard things.

Search is local and sequential. People do not scan option space; they search near the problem and near current practice, one alternative at a time, and stop when something acceptable appears. Cyert and March called the organisational version problemistic search: search is triggered by failure, motivated by a specific problem, and biased toward solutions adjacent to what the firm already does. This is why the option set in a strategy review so often consists of variations on the current business, and why genuinely different options tend to require an outsider or an explicit prompt to appear.

Aspiration levels move. Rather than maximising, Simon argued, decision-makers satisfice: they set an aspiration level and accept the first option that clears it. Aspiration levels are not fixed; they adjust to experience, drifting upward after success and downward after failure, and they are powerfully shaped by comparison with peers and with the past. A firm that has just had a good year aspires to more; a firm that has missed twice accepts less. Either way, the aspiration level — not a calculated optimum — is what determines when search stops.

Ambiguity, not merely risk. Much strategic information is not merely probabilistic but ambiguous: the same data support several competing interpretations, and no amount of analysis resolves which is right. Ambiguity is expensive to hold in mind, so decision-makers tend to resolve it early by adopting one interpretation and treating it thereafter as fact.

Willpower and self-interest are bounded too. Kahneman, extending Simon in his Nobel lecture, added that people are also short of self-control and are neither purely selfish nor purely fair. A leadership team can genuinely intend to exit an underperforming business and, quarter after quarter, fail to do so — not from ignorance but from bounded willpower and attachment.

What Bounded Rationality Does to Strategy

The practical consequences cluster in the places where strategy is hardest.

Diagnosis becomes a highlight reel. Because attention is selective and working memory small, a diagnosis reduces a messy situation to a few salient features — usually those that were vivid, recent, or already being tracked. Rumelt's complaint that most strategies lack a real diagnosis is partly a cognitive story: producing a contestable diagnosis requires holding competing explanations side by side, which is exactly what working memory resists.

Option sets shrink toward the familiar. Local search plus satisfiing plus a peer-anchored aspiration level is a recipe for incrementalism. This is not always a defect: incremental, adjacent moves are usually better-informed and more executable than bold departures. The failure mode is when the organisation believes it has conducted a broad search when it has merely enumerated its own history.

Trade-offs are avoided because comparison is costly. Evaluating options against each other demands simultaneous consideration of incommensurable attributes — a heavy cognitive load. Choosing the option that is best on the dimension currently in focus, and not asking about the others, is cheaper. This is one reason strategies so often lack explicit sacrifices: the sacrifice is only visible if you look at two alternatives at once.

Satisficing can be genuinely superior. It would be a mistake to read all this as an argument for more exhaustive analysis. Gigerenzer and colleagues showed that simple, fast, frugal rules often outperform complex models, particularly in uncertain environments where extra information mostly adds overfitting rather than signal. The skill is knowing which decisions deserve expensive deliberation and which are better served by a good-enough rule applied consistently. Bounded rationality is a diagnosis of the condition, not a prescription for paralysis.

Organisation as a Cognitive Technology

Simon's most important move was to treat organisations themselves as responses to individual cognitive limits. A firm's structure, routines, standard operating procedures, and division of labour exist largely so that no single person has to hold the whole problem. Hierarchy decomposes decisions; routines store solutions that no longer need to be reasoned out; departments specialise attention.

That delegation has a cost that is also cognitive. Organisations learn locally — units optimise what they can see, and the knowledge that matters at the boundary between units tends not to reside anywhere. March's analysis of exploration versus exploitation sharpens the point: learning from experience improves what is already done, which makes the organisation better and better at the current game while progressively less able to notice that the game has changed. The "myopia of learning" is not an absence of intelligence but a consequence of where attention is placed. Slack — deliberately held surplus resource — is one of the few mechanisms that funds search far from current practice, which is why cutting it to zero is often a false economy.

Cognitive limit Typical strategic symptom Process-level remedy
Scarce attention Diagnosis tracks what is already measured Assign someone explicitly to look outside the dashboard
Small working memory Two options, one of them obviously weak Write options down; decompose into comparable criteria
Local search Strategy that is last year's with new adjectives Mandate one option from outside current business
Moving aspiration levels Targets set by precedent, not by requirement Set aspiration from the diagnosis and intent, not from last year
Aversion to comparison No named trade-offs Force a two-column "what we give up" statement
Bounded willpower Strategies that are agreed and never executed Pre-commit to review dates and exit conditions

The remedies share a logic: they do not ask people to be less bounded, which never works, but move the work out of individual heads and into structures, artefacts, and commitments.

A Worked Example

Return to Northwind, the specialty coffee roaster, and watch the second blade cut.

The board frames the question as "how do we grow profitably?" and, within an hour, is discussing three options: hire more field trainers, add a second roastery, or raise prices. All three are adjacent to current operations — textbook problemistic search. A fourth possibility, licensing the training curriculum to a national distributor rather than delivering it locally, never arises, not because it is a bad idea but because nobody's attention is allocated to it. One director later recalls thinking of it and not raising it, since the conversation had already "moved on."

The aspiration level is inherited: 20% revenue growth, because that is what last year's plan said. Nobody asks whether 20% is the right number; it clears the bar, so search stops. When a director proposes the home-subscription segment, the discussion evaluates it on one attribute — brand fit — and drops it, because comparing it properly against the training bundle would require holding two different business models in mind at once.

Northwind's strategy is not stupid. It is bounded, in a completely ordinary way. The value of naming the limits is that the remedies become visible: assign one person to search deliberately outside the current business; state the aspiration as a derived requirement rather than a precedent; require each option to be written on a single page with an explicit "what we give up" line; and, as the formulation process suggests, name in advance the evidence that would reopen the question.

Two Cautions

First, bounded rationality is not a licence for relativism or an excuse for poor analysis. Boundedly rational decision-makers can be careful, evidence-driven, and rigorous; they simply cannot be exhaustive, and the discipline lies in choosing what to compress and being honest about what was compressed. Second, the concept is inseparable from the ones that follow. Satisficing is a heuristic; the aspiration level is a reference point against which framing effects operate; and what a strategist notices is shaped by emotion and mental models. Bounded rationality names the container. The next subsections examine what happens inside it.
