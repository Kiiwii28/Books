2-3 Intuition, Emotion, and Mental Models in Judgment

Part of 2 Cognitive Psychology of Strategic Choice

Strategic judgment is never a purely analytical act. The numbers in a board pack are read by people who have seen this film before, who feel something about the outcome, and who are working inside a shared picture of how their industry works. Those three influences — intuition, emotion, and mental models — are not contaminants to be filtered out of an otherwise clean rational process. They are the machinery that makes judgment possible at all, and the reason it sometimes fails in patterned, predictable ways. The previous two subsections showed that attention is scarce and that shortcuts produce biases. This one looks at where those shortcuts come from: the fast pattern-matching of expertise, the signalling function of feeling, and the inherited frames through which strategists see their situation.

Intuition: pattern recognition, not mysticism

Intuition has an unhelpful reputation in management writing, inherited from a caricature of rational decision-making in which analysis is rigorous and intuition is mere guesswork. Cognitive research tells a more specific story. Gary Klein's studies of fire commanders, emergency physicians, and chess players showed that experts rarely compare options at all. They recognise a situation as an instance of a familiar type, retrieve the first course of action that fits, and mentally simulate it; if the simulation throws up a problem, they adjust. Klein called this the recognition-primed decision model. The expert's advantage is not a mysterious sixth sense but a large, well-indexed library of cases and the ability to match a new situation to it quickly.

The crucial qualification, which Klein developed jointly with Daniel Kahneman in a much-cited 2009 paper, is that this machinery only produces trustworthy intuition under two conditions: an environment that is sufficiently regular for patterns to exist, and prolonged practice with rapid, informative feedback that allows those patterns to be learned. Chess, firefighting, and anaesthesia satisfy both. Long-range market forecasting, political risk, and most corporate strategy do not — the feedback is slow, noisy, and confounded by luck. In such domains, intuition still fires with full confidence; there is simply no reason for that confidence to track accuracy.

This produces a practical diagnostic for the strategist. When a senior colleague says "I've seen this before, it won't work," the useful questions are: how many comparable instances have you actually observed? how quickly and clearly did you learn the outcome? and would the same instinct have been right about the cases that failed? Experienced judgment is an asset in stable, high-feedback parts of a business — pricing, supplier negotiation, hiring in a familiar role — and a liability in low-validity ones, where confidence is largely a function of how vivid the memory is.

Emotion: information, energy, and distortion

Antonio Damasio's work with patients who had lost the capacity to feel while retaining their reasoning intact is the classic demonstration that emotion is not the enemy of good decisions. Such patients could analyse options perfectly and then fail to choose, because feeling is what attaches value and salience to consequences. Emotion is how a trade-off is experienced as a trade-off — the discomfort of closing a division, the pull of a customer relationship, the unease that something in a plan does not add up — and teams that suppress feeling do not thereby become objective; they often lose the earliest signal that something is wrong.

The same mechanism, however, distorts. Paul Slovic's affect heuristic shows that evaluations of risk and benefit collapse into a single gut response: activities we feel good about are judged both low-risk and high-benefit, and vice versa, in defiance of the evidence. Mood shapes recall, so a buoyant team retrieves reasons to be optimistic. Anxiety produces what Staw, Sandelands and Dutton called threat rigidity — narrowing attention, centralising authority, and reverting to well-rehearsed routines precisely when novel responses are needed. Elation after a good year produces the opposite: widened scope, loosened controls, and a tolerance for risk that nobody would defend in a quiet moment. And ego involvement makes commitments self-justifying: Barry Staw's escalation research showed that decision-makers who had personally authorised a failing course of action invested more in it, and evaluated its prospects more favourably, than those who had inherited it.

Two practical distinctions help. The first is between integral and incidental emotion: feelings about the decision itself are data worth interrogating, whereas feelings carried in from an argument at home or a rival's press coverage are noise. The second is between emotion as a signal and emotion as a verdict. The signal "I am uneasy about this plan and cannot yet say why" is worth an hour of examination. The verdict "I am uneasy, therefore we should not do it" is not an argument, and treating it as one hands the decision to whoever feels most strongly.

Mental models: the frames we think inside

A mental model is a simplified internal representation of how something works — a market, a competitor, a technology, a career. Strategists think entirely inside such models, and the models do most of their work before any conscious reasoning begins, by determining what counts as data, what counts as a threat, and what options are even visible. Prahalad and Bettis used the term dominant logic for the shared cognitive map that a diversified firm's managers apply to all their businesses, and J.-C. Spender described industry recipes — the taken-for-granted assumptions that make an entire sector regard certain moves as sensible and others as absurd. Both are efficient and both are sticky: a model that has generated success becomes harder to revise precisely because it has earned credibility. Dorothy Leonard-Barton's phrase for the result was core rigidities — the same capabilities that make a firm distinctive becoming the reason it cannot see a shift coming.


The loop is what makes mental models consequential. Because we notice what the model says is relevant and read ambiguous outcomes as confirming it, a wrong model does not fade — it accumulates apparent evidence. Incumbents did not miss digital photography, discount retailing, or the shift to software subscriptions because they lacked data; they had the data and their model told them it was marginal. The discipline that breaks the loop is not "think harder" but deliberate exposure to material the model cannot absorb: outsiders with different recipes, structured contact with disconfirming customers, and scenario exercises designed to make the current model's blind spot concrete rather than abstract.

Mental models are also shared, and this is where they do their most visible damage in strategy. A leadership team with a strongly aligned model reaches decisions quickly and confidently, which is valuable in a stable environment and dangerous in a shifting one; Irving Janis's groupthink is the pathological version of a property that is otherwise a strength. Teams need enough overlap to communicate and enough divergence to notice. That is an argument for cognitive diversity in strategy teams that has nothing to do with fairness and everything to do with error correction.

A worked example

Halden Instruments is a forty-year-old maker of industrial measurement equipment, profitable and respected, whose board is considering a shift from selling instruments to selling a subscription analytics service. Three judgments are in play, and all three carry the fingerprints above.

The CEO's intuition is decisive and mostly right: she has watched three customers migrate in eighteen months and recognises the pattern from a prior firm. But the domain is one in which feedback takes years and the sample is three, so her confidence is running ahead of the validity of the environment. The CFO's opposition is equally patterned: her unease is real and partly integral — the subscription model genuinely cannibalises hardware revenue — but it is also amplified by a bad quarter and by her authorship of the current pricing model, which makes revision feel like a personal defeat. The team's shared mental model, "we are an instrument company that happens to have software," is doing the quietest and most consequential work: it makes the migration appear as a product-line question rather than an identity change, and it has been steadily confirmed by every customer conversation the sales team has chosen to have.

A disciplined version of the meeting does not resolve any of this by willpower. It runs a premortem — "it is three years from now and the subscription move has failed; write the story" — which surfaces the cannibalisation risk the CFO felt but could not argue. It assigns someone to build the strongest case against the CEO's pattern-reading, and someone else to interview the customers who have not migrated. It reframes the decision as reversible in stages, with kill criteria agreed in advance: if fewer than a stated number of customers renew at the new price within two quarters, the model is wrong. And it records in a decision journal what the team expected and why, so that when the feedback arrives in eighteen months they will be able to tell whether they were right for the reasons they thought.

None of these devices replaces judgment or removes emotion from the room. They change what the emotion and the intuition are pointed at — from defending a conclusion to testing one — which is the most that a decision process can do. The next chapter takes the same machinery out of the boardroom and into personal strategy, where the analyst, the decision-maker, and the person who bears the consequences are the same individual.
