From Strategy to Action: Operational Alignment

A strategy becomes real only when the ordinary, unremarkable decisions of an organisation — what gets funded, who is measured on what, which meeting discusses which question, who is allowed to say yes — point in the same direction as the strategy. That condition is what this subsection calls operational alignment. It is not the same as enthusiasm for the strategy, nor compliance with a template, nor a communications campaign. It is the structural property that makes the strategically correct behaviour also the easy behaviour, so that the organisation does not have to rely on heroics, willpower, or the Chief Executive's personal attention to get the strategy enacted.

The framing matters because the gap between strategy and outcome is usually not a gap in intent. Most organisations that fail to execute a strategy agreed it sincerely and communicated it widely. They failed because the machinery beneath the strategy — budgets, incentives, structures, metrics, routines — continued to reward the previous one. The previous chapter's diagnosis of bounded attention applies directly: people do what they are measured, paid, and asked about, and a strategy that contradicts those signals asks individuals to swim against the current for eight hours a day.

Alignment as fit, not as cascade

The intuitive model of alignment is a cascade: the corporate strategy is decomposed into business-unit objectives, then functional objectives, then individual targets, each level a smaller copy of the one above. Cascades are popular because they are auditable and produce a satisfying chart. They also fail in a specific and predictable way.

Mechanical decomposition optimises each box separately. If the sales function's target is volume, the operations function's target is unit cost, and the finance function's target is working capital, each unit can hit its number perfectly while the firm as a whole delivers something no customer wants: high volumes of cheap product with long lead times, sold at a discount to hit the volume target. Every unit is aligned upward and misaligned sideways.

Porter's notion of fit is the better mental model. Alignment is mutual consistency among activities, not fidelity of each subunit to a parent document. The test is not "does this objective appear in the cascade?" but "if every unit does exactly what its own measures reward, does the strategy happen?" That is a harder question, and it is the one that catches incoherence before it costs a year.


Five channels carry the strategy into behaviour. Each is a lever, and each is a place where a firm can be quietly out of alignment.

Channel Alignment question Typical misalignment
Resources Does the budget, headcount, and capital plan match the trade-offs? Strategy named, initiative unfunded
Measures and incentives Would hitting the targets produce the strategy? Commission on volume while strategy demands premium
Decision rights Can the people closest to the strategy actually commit resources? Discount approval centralised to a group with no strategic view
Structure Do unit boundaries put the whole problem in one place? Training owned by sales, treated as a free giveaway
Cadence and routines Does a recurring meeting ask the strategic question? Reviews discuss only variance to budget

Resourcing: the budget is the honest strategy

If a strategy document and a budget disagree, the budget is the strategy. Resource allocation is the least deniable form of commitment, because it is the only one that cannot be met by warm words. The practical discipline is to trace each element of the guiding policy and its coherent actions to a line in the budget, a named person, and a recurring block of their time, and to be willing to look at what the trace reveals.

Three allocation errors recur.

The unfunded initiative. The strategy names a capability the firm intends to build — a service line, a training function, a data platform — and no one is hired, no budget line is created, and no one's objectives are changed. The initiative lives on in the strategy document and in the verbal commitments of managers who are already fully committed elsewhere. The remedy is the capacity question, asked bluntly at the point of decision: who is doing this, and what are they therefore not doing? If nobody answers both halves, the strategy is not resourced.

Incrementalism in allocation. Budgets built by incrementing last year's numbers reproduce last year's strategy, whatever the new document says. Realignment almost always requires a visible reallocation — an explicit reduction somewhere to fund somewhere else — which is politically expensive and therefore diagnostic of whether the trade-offs are real. A strategy that requires no one to lose a budget is usually not a new strategy.

Slack cut to zero. The previous chapter noted that deliberately held surplus resource funds search away from current practice. The same logic applies to alignment: an organisation whose every hour is committed to current operations has no capacity to build the capabilities the strategy depends on, and will discover this in month seven.

Measures and incentives: the current runs where the metrics point

Metrics allocate attention, and attention is the scarcest strategic resource. Two failure patterns follow.

The first is Goodhart's law in its practical form: any measure that becomes a target begins to degrade as a measure. A roastery measured on tonnes roasted will produce long production runs and high changeover efficiency; the same roastery measured on on-time-in-full delivery on a small-batch schedule will produce different behaviour with the same equipment. Neither measure is wrong in itself; one of them simply happens to be the strategy.

The second is conflicting measures across functions, the operational signature of misalignment. Sales is paid on contract value, delivery on cost per unit, service on retained accounts, quality on defect rate. Each function behaves rationally and the customer receives a contradictory experience. The remedy is not to abolish functional metrics but to add a small number of shared measures that no single function can hit alone — revenue per account retained, training attach rate, delivery promise compliance — and to review them together rather than in separate meetings with separate owners.

A useful test before finalising any scorecard: write down what a clever, well-intentioned person would do if they optimised purely for this measure. If the answer is not what the strategy needs, the measure is wrong. A second test: is anything in the metric set measuring the trade-offs? If the premium position depends on declining low-margin volume, some measure must make declining it visible and creditable.

Decision rights and structure

Alignment also requires that the people who hold the strategic judgement can act on it. A firm that has decided to defend a premium position but requires head-office approval for any discount above 2% has, in effect, decided that the strategic judgement about discounting belongs to head office — which is fine, provided head office has the customer information and the speed to exercise it. Frequently it has neither.

The design rule is to centralise what the strategy depends on and decentralise the rest. If the strategy rests on service quality being consistent, the training standard and the audit belong at the centre. If it rests on speed to a specific local customer, pricing discretion within a band belongs to the front line. Structure should follow the same logic: put an entire strategic problem inside one unit, so that the trade-off is made by people who see both sides, rather than split across units that each see half.

Structural change is expensive and slow, which is why it is often the channel left untouched while the others are busy failing. A cheaper first move is to change decision rights without redrawing the organisation chart: state explicitly which decisions are made at which level, and by what rule.

Cadence: the routines that keep strategy present

A strategy that appears on no recurring agenda decays within a quarter, whatever its quality. Alignment requires an operating rhythm in which the strategic question is asked before the operational one.

A practical structure has three recurring reviews with distinct jobs:

  • Weekly or fortnightly operational: are commitments being met, and what is in the way? Nothing strategic is decided here; the point is that operational slippage is surfaced early rather than discovered at quarter end.
  • Monthly business review: are the leading indicators moving, and where are the trade-off decisions queued? The agenda should include one standing item that asks what the strategy requires this month and what it would have us decline.
  • Quarterly strategic review: is the diagnosis still sound, and does the resource allocation still match the intent?


The most frequent defect is that the middle layer is missing: organisations have operational meetings and an annual strategy offsite, and nothing in between where the strategy and the operations meet. Reporting compounds the problem. A pack that contains only financials tells the team whether last year's plan is on track and nothing about whether this year's strategy is working; a pack that includes the strategy's leading indicators — training attach rate, revenue per account, turnaround compliance — makes the strategic conversation possible without anyone having to insist on it.

Diagnosing misalignment

Misalignment is easier to detect in symptoms than in structure, and the symptoms are recognisable.

  • The strategy does not appear on any recurring agenda in any recognisable form.
  • The number of sanctioned initiatives exceeds the capacity to deliver them, and none is ever cancelled.
  • Two functions can each show you a dashboard on which they are performing well, and the customer is unhappy.
  • Business cases cannot be assessed against the strategy, only against their own payback.
  • Ask five managers what the firm is not going to do, and get five different answers, or none.
  • A decision everyone agrees with is made three times because it is unclear who owns it.

A simple audit converts these impressions into a list. For each element of the strategy, name the mechanism that is supposed to deliver it and judge whether it is consistent, silent, or actively contradictory.

Strategy element Mechanism Verdict Action
Small-batch, 48-hour turnaround Production bonus measured on tonnes Contradicts Replace with on-time-in-full and changeover time
Training as core to the bundle Trainers sit in sales; training given free to close deals Contradicts Move trainers to operations; price training as part of the retainer
Serve cafés with 2–10 sites Sales territories built by postcode, not account size Silent Redraw territories by account profile
Exit the grocery channel No assigned owner for the exit Silent Name an owner and a date

The table is deliberately unglamorous. Alignment work is mostly the discovery that a handful of specific, fixable contradictions are doing more damage than any amount of motivational communication.

A worked example

Return to Northwind, the specialty coffee roaster whose strategy is to win by making each independent café more profitable per square metre, selling beans bundled with training and menu design to cafés with two to ten sites within a four-hour drive.

Resources. The board cancels the grocery contract, which releases roughly a fifth of roasting capacity, and reallocates the associated sales and trade-marketing spend to two field trainer posts. The trainers are the strategy made physical; if they are not hired, nothing else matters.

Measures. The commission scheme is the sharpest instrument. It previously paid a percentage of contract value, which rewarded the largest-volume deals regardless of profitability or fit. It is replaced by a scheme weighted to revenue retained per account and the proportion of accounts with an active training plan. The operations bonus, paid on tonnes roasted, is the next casualty: it directly penalises the small-batch schedule the bundle depends on. It is replaced by on-time-in-full compliance and changeover time.

Decision rights. Discounting is the point where a premium position is most often given away by accident. Any discount beyond a stated band now requires the commercial director, and the standard for approval is stated in the strategy's own language: does this account become more profitable for the café, and does it stay within the four-hour radius? Local sales staff keep discretion inside the band so that ordinary deals do not queue.

Structure. The field trainers are moved from sales into operations. This is a two-line change on the organisation chart with a disproportionate effect: under sales, training was used as a closing incentive and given away; under operations, it is a scheduled capability with a visible cost and a delivery standard.

Cadence. The monthly commercial review, previously a volume review, is rebuilt around three numbers — accounts with training attached, revenue per account, and 48-hour turnaround compliance — and gains a standing item on which accounts should be declined. It is this item, more than any speech, that keeps the trade-off alive.

Six months later the picture is instructive. Turnaround compliance has slipped to around 80%, and the cause is not the roastery's capability but the tail of the incentive system: a supervisor still receives an informal weekly tonnage report and schedules accordingly, prioritising long runs. The example matters because it is typical. Alignment is not achieved by one decision; it is achieved by noticing, repeatedly, the places where an old measure, an old routine, or an old habit is still quietly running the organisation.

The limits of alignment

Two cautions prevent alignment from becoming an end in itself.

First, over-alignment produces rigidity. An organisation in which every measure, process, and structure is tightly fitted to the current strategy is very efficient at executing it and very poor at noticing that it should change. Loose coupling — some units with slack, some measures deliberately not tied to incentives, some exploratory activity protected from the alignment logic — is not sloppiness but a hedge against the strategy being wrong. The following subsection takes up precisely this problem: how an aligned organisation also learns, experiments, and revises.

Second, alignment is a means, not a virtue. The point is not that everything should be consistent; the point is that inconsistency between what the strategy requires and what the organisation rewards is where strategy quietly dies. The diagnostic question remains the one the book has used throughout: when the strategy and the everyday machinery disagree, which one is actually running the organisation?
