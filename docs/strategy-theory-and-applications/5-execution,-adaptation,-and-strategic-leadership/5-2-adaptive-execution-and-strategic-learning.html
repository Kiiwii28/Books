5-2 Adaptive Execution and Strategic Learning

Part of 5 Execution, Adaptation, and Strategic Leadership

The preceding subsection showed how strategy becomes aligned operations: priorities translated into budgets, targets, and processes that fit together. Alignment, however, contains a hidden assumption — that the diagnosis in the strategy was right, or at least right enough. Adaptive execution is the discipline that treats that assumption as a testable hypothesis rather than a premise. It is the difference between asking "are we doing what we said?" and asking "is what we said still true, and what is the organisation learning about it?" The first question is the business of operational alignment. The second is the subject of this subsection.

Conformance and adaptation are both required

Execution carries two obligations that pull in different directions. Conformance means delivering the chosen strategy reliably: the same promise to every customer, the same standard in every unit, the same process at scale. It is what makes a strategy recognisable in the market and it depends on discipline, incentives, and repetition. Adaptation means changing what is done when the evidence warrants it, at whatever level in the organisation first sees the evidence.

Most execution failures are described as one or the other — "we didn't deliver" or "our people wouldn't adapt" — but the more common failure is holding the wrong one in the wrong place. Firms adapt their sales pitch and pricing daily while silently drifting on the capability investments the strategy depends on; or they enforce conformance so tightly that a change visible in the front line takes eighteen months to reach an executive meeting. The design question is not how to balance the two on average, but which decisions require uniformity and which require local discretion — and that question is answered by the strategy itself.

A useful test: if two units are doing this differently, is that a problem or a source of information? Price, brand promise, safety standards, and the core value proposition are usually conformance decisions. Customer handling, local channel tactics, and the design of a workaround usually are not.


The loop has an output the formulation process did not anticipate: emergent strategy, the part of realised strategy that was never written down but survives contact with events. Mintzberg's point was descriptive — this is how strategies actually form — but it is also a management opportunity, because emergence can be noticed, evaluated, and either scaled or stopped, rather than discovered retrospectively in a strategy away-day.

Single-loop and double-loop learning

Argyris and Schön's distinction is the most useful piece of vocabulary in this territory. Single-loop learning asks whether actions are producing the intended results and corrects the action. Double-loop learning asks whether the goals, assumptions, and diagnosis are right and corrects those instead. A thermostat is a single-loop learner: it acts, measures, and corrects. It will never question whether the setpoint is sensible.


Organisations are structurally excellent at single-loop learning and structurally resistant to double-loop learning, for three reasons. Single-loop learning is measurable, local, and quick, so it is what review meetings reward. Double-loop learning requires someone to say that the strategy — often the work of senior people in the room — is based on a mistaken belief. And the errors that matter most are the ones nobody is measuring, which is precisely why they persist. The practical discipline is therefore not exhortation but allocation: a standing item and a named owner for the question "what have we learned that contradicts the diagnosis?", separated in time from the performance review, so that the two conversations do not compete.

Making feedback informative

Learning is only as good as the signal it runs on, and organisational feedback is systematically worse than it looks.

Distinguish leading from lagging. Revenue, market share, and margin are lagging indicators: by the time they move, the cause is usually several quarters old. Adaptive execution steers on leading indicators — training throughput, pilot conversion, time-to-serve, complaint categories, backlog quality — that are close to the action and predictive of the outcome.

Expect lag, noise, and confounding. A poor quarter is not evidence that the theory is wrong; a good one is not evidence that it is right. The earlier discussion of decision journals and base rates applies at organisational scale: record what was expected, over what horizon, before the results arrive, or hindsight will silently rewrite the expectation.

Write the test before the experiment. A pilot is only informative if it was framed as a hypothesis with a stated threshold. "Let's try the training bundle in the north for two quarters; if average revenue per account has not risen by at least 6% relative to the control region, the mechanism is not working" produces a decision. A pilot with no threshold produces a story.

Beware what the metric teaches. Goodhart's law — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — is a design constraint, not a warning about people's honesty. If field trainers are measured on training days delivered, they will deliver training days, including to cafés that do not need them. Pair every operational metric with the outcome it is supposed to serve, and inspect the pairing when behaviour looks odd.

Keep the cost of speaking up low. Edmondson's work on psychological safety is often read as a matter of culture and is better read as a matter of rate: the value of a learning system is determined by how early bad news arrives. A defect reported in week two is cheap; the same defect reported in month seven has already been built into a hundred customer relationships.

Commander's intent and the right to adapt

Adaptation cannot be centrally directed, because the information that warrants it sits at the edge. The military solution to this problem — mission command, or Auftragstaktik — is a better template for strategy execution than most management writing acknowledges. The commander specifies the intent: what outcome the unit must achieve and why. Subordinates are then expected to exercise judgement about how, and to deviate from the plan when circumstances change, provided they act consistently with the intent.

Translated into a firm, an intent statement does three things: it states the outcome, it names the non-negotiable constraints, and it says explicitly what may be changed without asking. Northwind's version is: make every café on our list more profitable per square metre; never break a 48-hour delivery promise; how the bundle is configured and priced within the region is yours to decide. The second clause is what stops local adaptation from dissolving the position — it is the conformance boundary drawn around the adaptive space. Without a clearly stated intent, "empowerment" produces incoherence; with one, deviation becomes evidence rather than insubordination.

Two supporting mechanisms make this real. Decision rights should be pushed to the level that has the information, with escalation reserved for decisions that affect the constraints. And funding should be staged in tranches with review points, rather than granted annually in a lump, so that reallocation happens as evidence arrives instead of once a year under political pressure.

The artefacts and the cadence

Learning that has no slot in the calendar and no page in the pack does not happen. A small set of artefacts carries most of the load.

Artefact Rhythm Question it answers
Operating review Weekly or monthly Are we delivering? Where is execution slipping?
After-action review After each significant event What did we expect, what happened, why the gap, what changes?
Experiment register Continuous What are we testing, with what threshold, by when?
Strategy review Quarterly What have we learned that bears on the diagnosis?
Re-formulation trigger review On event Has something invalidated the strategy?

The after-action review deserves particular care because it is so often reduced to a status update. Four questions keep it honest: what did we intend? what actually happened? why the difference? what will we change, and who owns it? It should be conducted by someone who was not in command of the event, and it should be blameless in the specific sense that the object of inquiry is the system — the process, the information flow, the decision rule — rather than the individual. This is not leniency; it is the only way to hear the information that matters.

The strategy review must be kept distinct from the operating review. In practice they collapse, and the consequence is predictable: the discussion fills with delivery variances and the question of whether the theory is still correct is never reached. Separating them by agenda, by day, and ideally by the person chairing them is the cheapest structural fix available.

Learning pathologies to watch for

Organisational learning fails in patterned ways, and naming them is half the defence.

  • The success trap. Firms learn superbly from exploitation and under-invest in exploration, because the returns to improving what already works are certain and near, while the returns to searching elsewhere are uncertain and distant. Success narrows attention, which raises efficiency and lowers the capacity to notice that the game has changed — the dynamic behind most incumbent failures.
  • The failure trap. The mirror image: repeated failure triggers more search, which produces more failure, which triggers more search, and the organisation churns without ever accumulating capability. Persistence and abandonment look identical from the inside, which is why kill criteria agreed in advance matter.
  • Superstitious learning. Success is attributed to whatever the firm happened to be doing, especially when the causal links are ambiguous. The remedy is the same as in the chapter on judgment: treat the causal claim as a hypothesis and look for the cases where the same practice failed.
  • Competency traps. Existing routines are so much more efficient than new ones that the new ones never get enough use to improve. Deliberate subsidisation of the new — protected budgets, ring-fenced teams, tolerance for lower initial productivity — is the only reliable exit.
  • Blame-driven silence. If reporting a problem is career-limiting, the organisation's feedback system is measuring nothing but confidence. This is the single most common reason adaptive execution fails while everyone insists it is happening.

A worked example

Northwind, the specialty roaster, executes its beans-plus-training strategy across three regions. Six months in, the operating review is healthy: accounts up 14%, delivery times within target, no quality incidents. The strategy review, held separately, asks a less comfortable question — is the mechanism doing what the diagnosis claimed?

The diagnosis was that cafés cannot afford dedicated barista training, and that bundling training with beans would raise their profitability and therefore their loyalty and spend. The evidence, assembled in the experiment register, tells a different story. Café owners can afford training; they simply cannot afford to release staff from a shift, and the staff they train leave within a year. Attendance at training days is high — because field trainers are measured on attendance — but average revenue per account has risen only 2%, below the 6% threshold written into the pilot. Meanwhile a single region, acting on local judgement, has quietly redesigned the intervention: ninety-minute sessions run during shift handover, plus a short onboarding pack for new hires. That region's account revenue is up 11%.

Single-loop learning would say: train harder, sell more training days, tighten the attendance target. That is precisely what the Goodhart dynamic rewards. Double-loop learning says: the diagnosis was wrong in an instructive way, and the correct one is staff turnover and release time, not affordability. The bundle is revised — the same strategic intent, a different mechanism — the training-day metric is retired in favour of a retention and spend-pair metric, and the region's design becomes the template. Two things are recorded explicitly: the 2% result and the failed threshold, so that the original reasoning survives for the next review, and the second region's underperformance, which turns out to be an execution gap (no apprenticeship pipeline yet) rather than a theory gap.

None of this required new analysis. It required a forum in which the contradictory evidence had a place to be said, a threshold agreed before the results arrived, and a distinction between the region that had a theory problem and the region that had a capacity problem.

Two cautions

First, adaptation is not licence. A strategy that is revised whenever a quarter disappoints is not learning; it is churn, and it prevents the accumulated effort that makes any strategy work. The discipline is to hold intent stable while revising means, on stated grounds, in a stated forum — the same logic that governs personal strategy, applied where the feedback is noisier and the politics louder.

Second, learning costs money and shows up nowhere helpful. Slack, experimentation budgets, protected teams, and time spent in after-action reviews all reduce this quarter's measured efficiency and all pay back over horizons that the operating review does not see. Cutting them is the easiest decision on any cost-reduction list, and it is the one that quietly converts a learning organisation into a very efficient one that has stopped noticing things. Whether an organisation is willing to bear those costs when they are inconvenient is not an analytical question — it is a question about leadership, which is where the next subsection turns.
