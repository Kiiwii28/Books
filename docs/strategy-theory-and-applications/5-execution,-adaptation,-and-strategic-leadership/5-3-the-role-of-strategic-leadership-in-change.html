5-3 The Role of Strategic Leadership in Change

Part of 5 Execution, Adaptation, and Strategic Leadership

A strategy becomes real only when people do different things, stop doing old things, and accept the losses that both entail. That transition is the work of strategic leadership in change: the exercise of judgement that authorises a direction, interprets it for an organisation, allocates the resources that make it credible, and keeps it alive long enough for the organisation to learn whether it was right. It is not the same as seniority, and it is not a single inspirational act. Strategic leadership is distributed across a chief executive, a management team, unit leaders, and the informal experts whose behaviour tells everyone what is actually expected.

The previous subsections described the mechanics of alignment and adaptive execution. This one examines the leadership function that sits above and around those mechanics: who makes change legitimate, how the case for change is framed, where resistance is informative rather than obstructive, and how a change that begins as a project becomes an ordinary part of how the organisation works.

The distinctive work of leadership in change

Management and leadership are often contrasted too sharply, but the distinction is useful in change. Management produces order and consistency: plans, budgets, structures, controls. Leadership produces movement: a direction that did not previously exist, alignment among people who could otherwise pursue their own priorities, and the motivation to absorb the discomfort of doing things differently. A change programme without management will dissolve into enthusiasm and missed deadlines; a change programme without leadership will become a well-administered version of the status quo.

Strategic leadership in change has four recurring tasks:

  • Interpretation. Making sense of the situation and giving others a frame that explains why change is necessary, what it will involve, and what it will cost.
  • Direction. Choosing the strategic intent and the trade-offs, and refusing to reopen them every time a unit complains.
  • Legitimation. Using authority, resources, and symbolic action to make the change a real priority rather than a competing initiative.
  • Learning. Creating the conditions in which the organisation can discover whether the change is working and revise the means without abandoning the intent.

These tasks are not confined to the top. A middle manager who translates a corporate decision into a workable local routine, or who protects a team's capacity to experiment, is exercising strategic leadership. The top team's distinctive contribution is to set the terms within which that distributed leadership operates.

Diagnosis, framing, and the case for change

Leaders do not merely announce change; they give it meaning. The organisational literature calls this sensegiving — the process by which leaders frame a situation so that others can interpret it and act. A frame that works has three properties: it explains why now, it connects the change to something the organisation already values, and it is specific enough that people can disagree with it. "We must become more agile" fails all three. A diagnosis that says "our grocery volume is consuming the roasting capacity that our café service strategy depends on" can be argued with, tested, and acted upon.

Two framing temptations are worth resisting. The first is the burning platform: a crisis narrative designed to create urgency through fear. Fear is effective at unlocking attention, but it also produces threat rigidity — narrowed attention, centralised authority, and reversion to familiar routines — which is precisely the opposite of the behaviour that adaptive change requires. A credible, evidence-based case for change is usually more durable than a manufactured emergency.

The second temptation is to present change as pure gain. Prospect theory explains why this fails: people weigh losses more heavily than equivalent gains, and change invariably imposes losses — of status, competence, familiar routines, and valued relationships. A leader who does not name those losses does not prevent them; he or she simply makes them unspeakable. Naming what is ending, and why the sacrifice is worth it, is not weakness. It is the precondition for people choosing the change rather than merely complying with it.

Heifetz's distinction between technical problems and adaptive challenges sharpens the diagnosis. A technical problem can be solved by applying existing know-how. An adaptive challenge requires people to change their habits, values, or loyalties, and it cannot be solved by experts alone. Strategic leaders who treat an adaptive challenge as a technical one — a reorganisation, a new system, a training programme — produce activity without change. The leadership task in an adaptive challenge is to regulate the distress enough that people can work on the problem without being overwhelmed, and to resist the temptation to absorb the difficult work on their behalf.

Matching the leadership approach to the type of change

Not all change is the same, and the most common leadership error is to use the rhetoric and tools of transformation for a problem that requires adjustment, or the tools of adjustment for a problem that requires transformation.

Type of change What changes Leadership task Characteristic risk
Fine-tuning Routines, efficiency, minor processes Reinforce, remove friction, avoid over-managing Initiative fatigue for negligible gain
Incremental adjustment Products, services, continuous improvement Set a cadence, reward learning, connect improvements to strategy Change that never accumulates into advantage
Modular change A major component — a system, a business unit, a channel — within an otherwise stable core Manage interfaces, integrate the new with the old, protect the core Treating a modular change as plug-and-play
Discontinuous or transformational change Business model, identity, portfolio, core capabilities Frame-breaking diagnosis, coalition-building, resource reallocation, new metrics Using incremental tools for a frame-breaking problem

Tushman and Romanelli's image of punctuated equilibrium is useful here: organisations tend to experience long periods of incremental convergence, punctuated by episodes of frame-breaking change in which the old alignment no longer fits the environment. Strategic leadership involves recognising which period the organisation is in. During convergence, the leader's job is to sharpen and exploit the existing strategy. During a frame-breaking episode, the job is to authorise a new one and to dismantle the structures that protect the old.

Coalition, mandate, and the uses of dissent

Change is rarely implemented by a leader alone. It is implemented by a coalition — a group with the formal authority, the technical expertise, and the informal credibility to make the change stick. The senior team is the natural starting point, but it is rarely sufficient. Middle managers control the translation of strategy into daily practice, and their scepticism or enthusiasm is often decisive. A coalition that excludes them may win the announcement and lose the implementation.

A coalition is not a consensus. The leader must retain a clear mandate to decide, particularly about trade-offs, because a group constructed to avoid disagreement will avoid exactly the choices that make a strategy real. But a mandate without dissent is a licence for groupthink. The practices described earlier — a premortem, a red team assigned to the leading option, independent judgements written before discussion — are leadership tools as much as analytical ones. They signal that disagreement is a contribution rather than an act of disloyalty, and they make it harder for a confident team to mistake its own coherence for correctness.

Resource allocation and symbolic consistency

Change is what gets funded. A leader who announces a new strategic priority while protecting every legacy budget, every legacy role, and every legacy metric is not leading change; he or she is presiding over a contest that the old priorities will win. Resource reallocation is the point at which strategy stops being rhetoric. Money, headcount, management attention, and measurement systems all need to move, and the movement needs to be visible.

Symbolic action matters because organisations read behaviour more reliably than they read statements. The decision to cancel a flagship legacy contract, to change who reports to whom, to promote someone associated with the new direction, or to spend a Friday visiting customers rather than reviewing internal decks — these are not merely gestures. They are evidence about what the leader actually believes. The most corrosive condition in a change programme is not open resistance; it is the quiet conclusion that the change is optional because the people who matter are still rewarded for the old behaviour.

This is also where the leader's own cognitive limits become consequential. A leader who has built a career on the old model will find the new one harder to see and easier to discount. The earlier discussion of mental models applies with particular force to change: the leader's dominant logic shapes what is noticed, what is funded, and what is dismissed as marginal. Structured exposure to disconfirming evidence — outsiders, dissenting customers, scenario exercises — is not a sign of indecision. It is a defence against the leader's own frame becoming the organisation's blind spot.

Sequencing, pace, and the protection of the core

Kotter's familiar sequence — create urgency, build a coalition, form a vision, communicate it, empower action, generate short-term wins, consolidate gains, anchor the change — remains a useful summary of the leadership sequence, provided it is treated as a logic rather than a checklist. Its most important insight is that change requires a rhythm: enough early evidence of progress to sustain credibility, enough consolidation to prevent reversion, and enough anchoring to make the change survive the departure of its sponsor.

Two refinements are worth adding. First, early wins must be strategically relevant. A cosmetic quick win that is disconnected from the diagnosis buys a month of goodwill and teaches the organisation that the programme is about appearances. A genuine early win — a pilot that demonstrates the new model working for a real customer, a cost that is actually removed, a decision that is actually made — builds the credibility that funds the harder phases.

Second, the core must be protected while the edge is changed. Most organisations cannot transform everything at once. The leader's judgement lies in distinguishing the capabilities that must be preserved — the source of trust, quality, or operational reliability on which the new strategy depends — from the activities that must be retired. Change programmes that damage the core in the name of transformation often discover too late that the core was the platform for the new strategy.

Resistance, loss, and fair process

Resistance is often treated as an obstacle to be overcome. It is better treated as information. Some resistance comes from disagreement with the diagnosis, and it may be right. Some comes from self-interest, which is legitimate and must be addressed rather than moralised away. Some comes from the loss of competence and status that change imposes on people who were successful under the old model. And some comes simply from fatigue, where the organisation has been asked to absorb too many initiatives with too little support.

Fair process matters here, not because it makes change painless, but because it makes it legitimate. Kim and Mauborgne's three elements — engagement, explanation, and expectation clarity — are a practical guide: involve people in the decisions that affect them, explain the reasoning even when the decision goes against them, and be explicit about what is now expected. Procedures that are perceived as fair do not guarantee agreement, but they make it much harder for opposition to harden into cynicism.

Leaders also need to distinguish legitimate opposition from blocking behaviour. The former should be heard and, where necessary, accommodated; the latter may require the removal of a blocker, a change in incentives, or an explicit decision that the person cannot remain in a key role. Coercion can produce compliance, but it is expensive: it consumes trust and makes the next change harder. It is sometimes necessary, but it should be a choice rather than a default.

Adaptive leadership: experiments, learning, and course correction

A strategy is a hypothesis, and a change programme is the test. The leader's job is not to pretend to certainty but to build a learning architecture around the hypothesis: leading indicators, safe-to-fail experiments, after-action reviews, and pre-agreed criteria for revising the plan. This is where strategic leadership connects directly to adaptive execution. The leader creates permission for the organisation to say that a component of the plan is not working, and protects the people who say it.

Two disciplines make that credible. The first is to separate strategic intent from the means of achieving it. The intent — Northwind's ambition to be the roaster no independent café can outgrow, for example — should be stable enough to motivate sustained effort. The means — the bundle design, the route structure, the pricing model — should be revised as evidence arrives. A leader who changes the intent every time a plan disappoints has no strategy; a leader who refuses to change any plan that serves the intent has turned intent into dogma.

The second is to guard against escalation of commitment. Change programmes are especially vulnerable because the leader's identity becomes attached to the original decision. Pre-committing to review dates and kill criteria, and separating the decision from the decision-maker's self-image, are structural protections rather than personal virtues. A leader who can say "the evidence has changed and so should we" without treating it as a humiliation sets the tone for the whole organisation.

Institutionalising change

The final test of strategic leadership in change is whether the change survives the leader. A change that depends on one person's presence, energy, and attention is a intervention, not a transformation. Institutionalisation means embedding the new behaviour in the ordinary machinery of the organisation: roles and responsibilities, hiring and promotion criteria, incentive systems, budgeting, metrics, routines, and the stories people tell about what is valued.

Culture is usually the last thing to change, because culture is largely the residue of what has been rewarded and what has been tolerated. A leader who wants a more experimental organisation but punishes every failed experiment will get compliance and no learning. A leader who wants a customer-focused organisation but promotes people for internal political success will get the same. Changing the formal systems is not sufficient, but it is usually necessary; the informal culture follows when people see that the new behaviour is genuinely consequential.

Succession and leadership development belong here too. If the next generation of leaders is selected for the old model, the change will be reversed within a few years. Institutionalisation requires that the people who will inherit the organisation have been given the opportunity to practise the new capabilities, and that the organisation's definition of a successful leader has actually changed.

A worked example

Return to Northwind, the specialty coffee roaster that has decided to abandon low-margin grocery supply and build a differentiation-focused strategy around independent cafés. The strategic choice is made; the leadership work has only begun.

Northwind's managing director begins with interpretation and framing. She publishes a short diagnosis: grocery volume has been consuming small-batch roasting capacity, grocery margins have been falling for three years, and the cafés that Northwind is best placed to serve need training and menu support that national roasters cannot economically provide. She names the loss explicitly: the grocery contract is 30% of revenue, and the sales team that built it will see the change as a judgement on their work. She does not pretend otherwise.

She builds a coalition around the operations director, the head roaster, and the field-training lead. The sales director is not initially a supporter, because his team's bonuses are tied to volume. Rather than excluding him, she gives him the job of designing the account-profitability model that will replace volume as the sales metric — a difficult assignment that makes him a co-author of the change rather than a spectator. She retains the mandate to decide the trade-offs, but she uses a premortem to surface the risks the coalition is inclined to minimise.

She reallocates resources visibly. The grocery contract is cancelled at renewal, freeing roasting capacity. Two sales roles are converted to account management, and the bonus scheme shifts from kilograms sold to account profitability. Two field trainers are hired, and the training budget is ring-fenced against in-year cuts — a deliberate signal that the service capability, not the beans, is the strategy. She moves her own calendar: every Friday is spent visiting cafés, not in internal reviews.

She sequences the change through a twelve-café pilot. The early win is not a marketing campaign but a measurable improvement in café margin per square metre, which gives the account managers something real to talk about. She protects the core — roasting quality and delivery reliability — while the edge is changed, because a service bundle built on unreliable coffee is not a strategy.

When the pilot shows that menu design is valued more than barista training, she revises the bundle rather than defending the original plan. The intent remains; the means change. She keeps a pre-agreed review point: if the training capacity cannot be scaled economically by the end of the year, the bundle will be redesigned around remote support. Finally, she institutionalises the change: account profitability becomes the primary sales metric, promotion criteria for account managers include service capability, and the apprenticeship pipeline is funded as a permanent feature rather than a pilot.

The example is not a template. Its point is that the leadership contribution is not a single speech or a single decision. It is a series of choices — about framing, coalition, resources, sequence, and learning — that together make the strategy credible enough for people to act on.

Common failure modes

Failure What it looks like Antidote
Transformation theatre A launch event, a new vocabulary, unchanged budgets Move resources and metrics before announcing success
Urgency by fear A manufactured crisis that narrows attention and centralises authority Build a specific, evidence-based case; regulate distress rather than amplify it
Unnamed losses People are told the change is all upside Name what is ending and explain why the sacrifice is worth it
Coalition of the willing The sceptical middle managers are excluded Include the people who control implementation; give them real work
Early victory Success declared after a pilot, before the change is anchored Consolidate; keep reviewing leading indicators
Leader as bottleneck Every trade-off routed to the top; units wait for permission Clarify decision rights; distribute leadership with a clear mandate
Escalation of commitment The plan is defended because the leader owns it Pre-commit to review dates, kill criteria, and public course correction
Change without succession The change depends on one person and reverses when they leave Build the new capability into promotion and hiring criteria

Strategic leadership in change is ultimately a judgement about how much to push and how much to allow, how much to preserve and how much to retire, when to hold the intent and when to revise the means. It cannot eliminate uncertainty, and it cannot make loss painless. Its measure is whether the organisation can act on a strategy it did not previously have — and whether, when the leader eventually leaves, it can keep adapting without them.
